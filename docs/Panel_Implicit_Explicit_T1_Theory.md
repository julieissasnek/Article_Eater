## PART I: POSITION STATEMENTS

### 1. David Kirsh (Moderator) — Opening Statement

Thank you all for joining this panel. Let me frame what we are trying to accomplish and why it matters.

The Goldilocks project models how built environments affect human cognition, affect, and wellbeing. Its existing Tier 1 theories — Predictive Processing, Berlyne's arousal theory, Attention Restoration Theory, Stress Recovery Theory, Biophilia, Fractal Fluency, Prospect-Refuge, and others — share a striking commonality: every one of them treats the human response to architecture as fundamentally *implicit*. Prediction error is computed subpersonally. Arousal potential is a stimulus property. Soft fascination operates precisely because it bypasses deliberate attention. Stress recovery is pre-cognitive. Evolutionary aesthetics operates at the autonomic level.

Yet consider what actually happens when a person enters a building. A physician walks into a hospital and sees a clinical workspace configured for a particular workflow. An architect walks into the same space and sees a composition of volumes, light, and material. A patient's family member walks in and sees a place where someone they love may die. The physical environment is identical. The stimulus array impinging on each retina is, for practical purposes, the same. Yet the effective psychological environment — what I call the *epistemic bubble* — is radically different in each case, because each person has selected a different activity frame, and that selection is explicit.

This is the problem. If all our theories are implicit, we cannot explain why the same room produces different Goldilocks optima for different people, or for the same person in different activity modes.

Now, I want to flag a terminological tension at the outset, because my own use of "implicit" and "explicit" diverges from the way the dual-process literature uses those terms. In my 1990 paper and the 2003 *Encyclopedia of Cognitive Science* entry, I defined the distinction in *representational* terms: information is explicit to the degree that it can be recovered without costly computation; it is implicit to the degree that recovering it requires substantial computational work. This is a continuum, not a binary. A chess grandmaster looking at a board has the best next move *more explicit* in her representation than a novice does, because less computation is required for her to extract it.

Kahneman's Type 1 and Type 2, by contrast, mark a *processing mode* distinction — fast and automatic versus slow and deliberate. These are different cuts across cognitive activity, and they cross-classify. A chef's mise-en-place is explicit in my sense — the ingredients are spatially arranged to make information immediately accessible — but it enables Type 1 processing in Kahneman's sense, because the well-organized workspace allows rapid, automatic sequencing without deliberate search. Conversely, a graduate student struggling with a proof is engaged in Type 2 processing, but the deep structure of the mathematics may be highly implicit in my representational sense — computationally expensive to extract.

We need a theory that handles both senses and specifies which is operative in which architectural phenomenon. That is today's task.

But I want to plant a thought that emerged in earlier sessions: this theory may not simply be Tier 1 framework number eleven, sitting alongside the others. It may be *superordinate* to them. All the existing frameworks describe processes that operate within the implicit channel. The implicit/explicit theory specifies how explicit processing — activity selection, aesthetic judgment, metacognitive override — *configures* which implicit channel is operative and what stimulus ecology it operates over. If that is right, this framework is not at the same logical level as the others; it is the framework that determines which of the others is engaged.

Let me now invite position statements. Jonathan, as co-originator of the Type 1/Type 2 framework, would you begin?

### 2. Jonathan Evans — The Dual-Process Framework

Thank you, David. I appreciate the care with which this project treats the distinction, because I have spent decades trying to sharpen it, and it is frequently misunderstood.

Let me state the core claim precisely. The defining feature of Type 2 processing is that it requires working memory and supports hypothetical thinking — the ability to decouple representations from their default content and manipulate them as suppositions. Type 1 processing is everything else — and I want to stress that "everything else" is enormously heterogeneous. Type 1 is not a single system; it is a label for any cognitive process that does not require working memory for hypothetical reasoning. It includes perceptual processing, motor routines, implicit learning, emotional appraisals, associative memory retrieval, and much more.

Now, for your architectural application, the crucial point is this: Type 2 processing is *interventionist*. It does not simply override Type 1; it reconfigures what Type 1 operates on. When I select an activity frame — "I am going to evaluate this corridor as a nurse thinking about patient flow" — I deploy Type 2 processes (goal representation, deliberate attentional allocation, working memory maintenance of criteria) that *change the effective stimulus* for Type 1 processes (perceptual grouping, affordance detection, emotional valence). This is not a static hierarchy; it is a dynamic reconfiguration loop.

One caution: I have always resisted calling these "two systems." There is not a System 1 box and a System 2 box in the brain. There are Type 1 *processes* and Type 2 *processes*, and a given cognitive episode typically involves both, interleaved and interacting. For your templates, you will want to avoid reifying the distinction into discrete neural modules.

### 3. Andy Clark — Predictive Processing and the Dark Room

I want to take the predictive processing position honestly here, including its limitations.

The standard PP story says the brain is a prediction engine that minimizes surprise — technically, variational free energy. Active inference extends this by noting that the system can minimize surprise not only by updating predictions (perception) but also by acting on the world to make predictions come true. Now, supporters of PP, myself included, have argued that active inference can absorb goal-directed behavior: you minimize surprise relative to a prior preference that specifies your goals. Selecting an activity frame, on this reading, is just selecting a set of precision-weighted priors.

But I must be candid: this move has always felt like a stretch when it comes to genuine explicit deliberation. The dark room problem — why doesn't the prediction-minimizing agent simply find a dark, silent room and stay there? — has never been fully resolved within PP. The standard response is that we have priors that predict variety, novelty, and so on. But that response smuggles in exactly the kind of explicit goal representation that PP was supposed to derive rather than presuppose.

David Kirsh's activity space framework exposes this gap cleanly. When a person *chooses* to engage with a difficult problem — a choice that *increases* prediction error — PP requires us to posit a high-level prior that the person expects to encounter high PE in the service of learning. That is not false, but it is post hoc and unfalsifiable in the way Lakatos would recognize. We are adding epicycles.

So I think the implicit/explicit distinction identifies something real that PP on its own does not handle well: the explicit, deliberate, working-memory-dependent *selection of which prediction landscape to inhabit*. PP can describe what happens once you are in that landscape. It cannot, without supplementation, explain how you got there.

### 4. Jakob Hohwy — In Defense of PP, with Concessions

Andy is more generous to PP's critics than I might be, but I agree on the central point. PP is an account of how the brain processes information once a generative model is in place. It is not, on its own, an account of how the organism selects which generative model to deploy. That selection — which is what David Kirsh's "activity frame" captures — involves what philosophers call *model selection* or *structure learning*, and it requires a kind of meta-processing that standard hierarchical PE minimization does not straightforwardly explain.

I would frame the relationship this way: the implicit/explicit theory specifies the *boundary conditions* under which PP operates. Once an activity frame is selected (explicit), the PP machinery handles the resulting perceptual, cognitive, and affective processing (implicit). The theory you are building today tells us how and when the boundary conditions shift.

What I want to resist is the suggestion that PP is merely one of several implicit-level theories. PP is the most general information-processing framework we have for neural computation. ART, SRT, Berlyne — these can all be recast as special cases of prediction error dynamics under particular priors. But I concede that the *selection of those priors through explicit processing* is not internal to PP.

### 5. Earl K. Miller — The Neural Implementation of Top-Down Control

Let me bring this down to neural brass tacks.

The prefrontal cortex maintains patterns of activity representing goals, rules, and task demands. These representations generate bias signals — top-down influence — that propagate to posterior and subcortical structures, shaping how sensory information is processed, which memories are retrieved, and which motor programs are activated. Jonathan Cohen and I formalized this in our 2001 integrative theory, which remains the most cited framework for understanding how goals shape processing.

The mechanism operates through neural oscillations. Alpha and beta frequency waves — roughly 15 to 35 Hz — carry top-down control signals. Gamma waves — 35 to 60 Hz — encode incoming sensory information. When you impose a goal (Type 2, activity frame selection), prefrontal beta oscillations constrain gamma activity in sensory and association cortex. This is literally the neural implementation of what David Kirsh describes as the epistemic bubble: the goal representation filters which incoming information gets amplified and which gets suppressed.

Here is what matters for your theory: this top-down reconfiguration is *measurable*. You can record it with EEG (beta power over frontal electrodes), with fMRI (PFC–posterior connectivity changes), and with MEG (directed information flow from frontal to posterior regions). When someone shifts activity frames — from casual observation to focused evaluation of a space — the beta/gamma balance changes, and it changes within hundreds of milliseconds.

Furthermore, the neurons doing this work are not dedicated to specific tasks. They exhibit *mixed selectivity* — the same neurons participate in multiple goal representations at different times. This gives the prefrontal cortex extraordinary flexibility: it can configure the same downstream processing machinery in radically different ways depending on the current goal. This is precisely why the same physical room generates different psychological environments under different activity frames.

### 6. David Badre — Hierarchical Control and the Abstraction Gradient

Earl has given you the mechanism at the level of PFC-to-posterior influence. I want to add the *internal structure* of the prefrontal cortex, because it maps onto the levels of control that David Kirsh's framework requires.

My lab and others have shown that lateral PFC is organized along a rostro-caudal gradient of abstraction. Posterior regions (premotor cortex, posterior LPFC) handle concrete, context-dependent action selection — which button to press given this stimulus. More anterior regions (mid-LPFC, anterior LPFC, frontopolar cortex) handle increasingly abstract, temporally extended control policies — the overarching goal that structures a sequence of subgoals.

This matters for architectural experience because "activity selection" — Kirsh's Step 1 — is a high-level, abstract control operation. It lives in anterior PFC, particularly frontopolar cortex (BA 10) and anterior LPFC (BA 46/9). This region represents the superordinate goal: "I am a nurse evaluating patient flow" or "I am an architect assessing spatial composition." That abstract representation then cascades through the hierarchy: mid-LPFC specifies subgoals (evaluate corridor width, assess lighting levels), posterior LPFC selects specific perceptual operations (attend to the width cue, not the color cue), and premotor regions execute the orienting responses.

The cascade is the bridge mechanism. Explicit activity selection at the top of the hierarchy reconfigures processing at every lower level, all the way down to what counts as a salient perceptual feature. And this cascade happens through the oscillatory mechanism Earl described: each hierarchical level imposes beta-frequency constraints on the level below it.

### 7. Wim De Neys — Conflict Detection at the Interface

I study the moment when Type 1 and Type 2 collide. My research program over the past fifteen years has shown something surprising: when people give biased, heuristic responses — when Type 1 "wins" — the brain nonetheless detects the conflict with normative considerations. This detection is reflected in anterior cingulate cortex activation, increased response latency even on "biased" trials, decreased confidence, and elevated skin conductance.

The architectural implication is this: when someone enters a space and has an immediate negative affective response (Type 1: "this feels wrong") but also has explicit knowledge that the space was well-designed or recently renovated (Type 2: "this should be fine"), the ACC registers the conflict. The person may not be conscious of the conflict, but it produces measurable neural and autonomic signatures.

This conflict detection mechanism is what makes the implicit/explicit interface tractable for empirical research. You do not need to ask people to introspect about whether their response was implicit or explicit. You can measure ACC activation (fMRI, EEG — the error-related negativity component), response latency, confidence ratings, and skin conductance to detect when the two channels are in conflict versus alignment. When they align (the space feels good AND you judge it to be well-designed), processing is fluent and rapid. When they conflict, you get measurable perturbation.

I should note a debate in the field: I argue that conflict detection is itself a Type 1 process — an intuitive sensitivity to logical structure that operates automatically. Valerie Thompson and others argue it requires minimal Type 2 engagement. For your architectural application, this debate matters less than the fact that the conflict is detectable by either account.

### 8. Daphna Shohamy — Memory Systems and Environmental Learning

I want to add a dimension that has not yet been discussed: the memory systems that generate implicit versus explicit environmental associations.

The textbook view distinguishes the hippocampal system (explicit, episodic, declarative) from the striatal system (implicit, procedural, habitual). My work has shown that the distinction is real but the interaction is far richer than independence. The hippocampus and striatum cooperate during encoding, and information leaks between systems during decision-making.

For architecture, this means that a person's response to a space is simultaneously shaped by:

1. **Explicit episodic memory** (hippocampal): "The last time I was in a hospital like this, I received a cancer diagnosis." This memory is consciously accessible, context-specific, and can be deliberately recalled.

2. **Implicit associative memory** (striatal): Statistical regularities learned over a lifetime of environmental experience. Spaces with certain proportions, lighting qualities, and material textures have been *associated* with reward or threat, and these associations operate outside conscious awareness through dopaminergically modulated reinforcement learning.

3. **Cross-talk**: Hippocampal episodic memories generalize through pattern completion, influencing striatal associations. Striatal reward signals modulate hippocampal encoding, determining which explicit memories get consolidated.

The key moderating variable is *dopamine*. Dopamine gates which system dominates at any given moment. High dopamine states (reward anticipation, novelty) favor hippocampal encoding and model-based (explicit) reasoning. Low dopamine states favor striatal dominance and habitual (implicit) responding. This means that the same space can drive different implicit/explicit balances depending on the person's dopaminergic state — which is influenced by the environment itself in a recursive loop.

### 9. Lucina Q. Uddin — Network Switching as the Bridge Mechanism

I can offer the large-scale network implementation of what everyone has been describing.

The brain organizes its activity into three macro-scale networks that are particularly relevant here. The *default mode network* (DMN: medial PFC, posterior cingulate, angular gyrus) supports internally directed cognition — self-referential thought, episodic memory retrieval, mind-wandering, future simulation. The *central executive network* (CEN: dorsolateral PFC, posterior parietal cortex) supports externally directed, task-focused cognition — the kind of deliberate, goal-structured processing that defines Type 2. The *salience network* (SN: anterior insula, dorsal anterior cingulate cortex) detects behaviorally relevant stimuli and, critically, *drives the switch* between DMN and CEN.

Vinod Menon and I proposed in 2010 that the anterior insula, as the hub of the salience network, initiates dynamic switching between internally and externally directed processing modes. Subsequent work using dynamic causal modeling has confirmed this: the salience network causally drives transitions between DMN-dominant and CEN-dominant states.

Now map this onto David Kirsh's bridge mechanism:

- **Step 1 — Activity selection (explicit)**: The CEN activates. Dorsolateral PFC represents the goal; posterior parietal cortex specifies the attentional set.
- **Step 2 — Activity frame reconfigures affordance ecology**: CEN-to-posterior connectivity changes, altering which features are processed.
- **Step 3 — Reconfiguration is largely implicit**: The new processing regime becomes the default; if well-practiced, it may even shift toward DMN support (internalized expertise).
- **Step 4 — PE-based processing operates over different input**: With the processing ecology reconfigured, the prediction machinery (which lives partly in DMN for self-relevant predictions and partly in CEN for task-relevant predictions) operates over different effective stimuli.

The salience network is the *gate*. When you enter a new space, the anterior insula detects environmental novelty (bottom-up salience), triggering a switch from whatever prior processing mode was dominant toward CEN engagement — which then selects the appropriate activity frame. The speed and efficiency of this switching is a measurable individual difference that should moderate architectural response.

### 10. Vinod Goel — Neural Dissociations in Dual-Process Reasoning

My contribution is direct neuroimaging evidence for the dissociability of the two processing types.

Using fMRI during syllogistic reasoning with belief-bias manipulations, I have shown a clean crossover in prefrontal activation. When belief and logic conflict and the participant successfully follows logic (Type 2 overriding Type 1), the right inferior prefrontal cortex activates — consistent with its role in cognitive monitoring and inhibition of prepotent responses. When belief-bias wins (Type 1 overriding Type 2), the ventromedial prefrontal cortex activates instead — consistent with its role in affectively based evaluation.

Furthermore, I have shown that reasoning with meaningful, familiar content activates a left-lateralized temporal–frontal semantic network, while content-free formal reasoning activates bilateral parietal and dorsal frontal regions. The brain processes logically identical material through different pathways depending on semantic richness.

The architectural parallel is immediate. The same spatial geometry — a corridor with particular proportions — will be processed through different neural pathways depending on whether it is semantically loaded (a hospital corridor associated with personal memories) or semantically neutral (an unfamiliar building). The semantic loading is explicit — it comes from knowledge, memory, cultural meaning — and it routes processing through different circuits.

My collaborative work with Wim De Neys and Oshin Vartanian showed that even when people give biased responses, the ACC detects the conflict. The brain is, as we put it, "smarter than we think." This means there is always a neural trace of the implicit/explicit interaction, even when the explicit channel does not prevail.

### 11. Anjan Chatterjee — Neuroaesthetics and Environmental Judgment

I study how the brain generates aesthetic judgments — which is a paradigm case of explicit processing that modulates implicit response.

Aesthetic evaluation of architecture involves at least three dissociable neural components: (1) perceptual processing of formal properties like symmetry, proportion, and contour (largely occipital and parietal, largely Type 1); (2) affective response — the hedonic valence, the felt pleasure or discomfort (medial OFC, ventral striatum, amygdala, largely Type 1); and (3) *aesthetic judgment* — the deliberate evaluation of beauty, fitness, meaning, which recruits dorsolateral PFC and is clearly Type 2.

The important finding from my work and that of others is that these three components are dissociable. You can have strong perceptual fluency (Type 1: the proportions are processed easily) and positive hedonic valence (Type 1: it feels good) but negative aesthetic judgment (Type 2: "it's trite" or "it's derivative"). Conversely, you can have perceptual disfluency and mild negative valence but highly positive aesthetic judgment — think of deconstructivist architecture that is deliberately challenging but intellectually admired.

This dissociation is what makes the implicit/explicit framework essential: no purely implicit theory can explain why the same perceptual fluency produces different evaluations depending on the person's aesthetic framework. The aesthetic framework is explicit — it is culturally acquired, intellectually elaborated, and deliberately deployed.

### 12. Moshe Bar — Predictions, Associations, and Proactive Processing

I approach this from the angle of how the brain generates predictions about upcoming information, which is relevant to how environments are experienced moment to moment.

My research shows that the brain is constantly generating associative predictions — the orbitofrontal cortex and medial temporal lobe produce "gist" representations of scenes and objects that precede detailed perceptual analysis. When you walk into a room, within the first 130 milliseconds your brain has generated a coarse prediction of what that space contains and what it means. This is entirely Type 1 — automatic, fast, based on statistical regularities in prior experience.

But here is the nuance: the *content* of those predictions is shaped by learning history, which is itself a product of prior explicit engagement. An architect's 130-millisecond gist representation of a room is different from a layperson's, not because the perceptual machinery differs but because the associative networks that generate predictions have been differently trained. Years of explicit study (Type 2 deliberate practice) reshape the implicit prediction machinery (Type 1 associative generation). This is expertise — the sedimentation of explicit processing into implicit representations.

Your theory needs to capture this *temporal depth*: at any moment, the implicit response is partly a product of prior explicit processing that has been consolidated into automatic routines. The current activity frame (explicit) and the sedimentated expertise (formerly explicit, now implicit) jointly determine the prediction landscape.

### 13. Matthew Botvinick — Cost of Control and the Expected Value of Control

I want to formalize something that has been implicit in the discussion: the allocation of cognitive control is itself a decision under uncertainty, and the brain has mechanisms for computing whether explicit, Type 2 processing is *worth the cost*.

The anterior cingulate cortex is not simply a conflict detector — though it does that. More precisely, it computes the *expected value of control*: the anticipated benefit of allocating additional cognitive control (Type 2 processing) minus the anticipated cost (metabolic, temporal, opportunity cost). When EVC is high — the situation is important, the conflict is salient, the stakes are real — the ACC signals the need for greater dorsolateral PFC engagement. When EVC is low, the system defaults to Type 1 processing.

For architecture, this means there is a *decision* about whether to engage explicitly with a space. Most of the time, people walk through buildings on autopilot — Type 1 processing handles navigation, obstacle avoidance, and basic affective response. Explicit engagement kicks in when: (a) something is novel or surprising (salience network detection, per Lucina's account); (b) the task demands it (you are an inspector, a designer, a researcher); or (c) something goes wrong (prediction error exceeds a threshold, conflict is detected).

The EVC computation is the neural mechanism that determines *whether* the bridge from implicit to explicit is traversed. Not every environmental encounter triggers explicit processing. The theory needs to specify the conditions under which it does.

### 14. Vittorio Gallese — Embodied Simulation and the Pre-Reflective Ground

I want to ensure that we do not lose sight of the bodily, pre-reflective dimension. Embodied simulation — the automatic, pre-reflective activation of motor and somatosensory representations when perceiving actions, objects, and spaces — is the deepest layer of Type 1 processing in architecture.

When you see a staircase, your motor cortex simulates ascending. When you see a narrow passage, your body schema anticipates compression. When you see another person in a space, your mirror system simulates their experience. All of this is automatic, pre-reflective, and embodied.

But here is where the explicit matters: embodied simulation is *modulated* by context, intention, and expertise. A dancer simulates movement differently than a non-dancer. A claustrophobe simulates the narrow passage with amplified autonomic response. And — crucially — the activity frame determines which embodied simulations are *foregrounded*. When you are thinking about the space as a user, you simulate your own movement through it. When you are thinking about it as a designer, you simulate the movements of hypothetical users. Same body, same space, different simulated perspectives — and the selection of perspective is explicit.

### 15. Harry Mallgrave — Architecture and the Design Implications

As the architect on this panel, let me translate what we are discussing into built-form implications.

Architects have always implicitly understood the dual-process distinction, even without the cognitive science vocabulary. We design for two registers simultaneously: the *atmospheric* register (what Zumthor calls "architectural atmosphere" — the immediate, felt, pre-reflective quality of a space) and the *programmatic* register (the explicit activities the space is designed to support — workflow, wayfinding, social interaction patterns).

The atmospheric register corresponds to Type 1 processing: the immediate affective impact of light quality, material texture, spatial proportion, acoustic character. The programmatic register corresponds to Type 2 processing: the deliberate engagement with the space's functional organization.

What this theory gives architecture that existing frameworks do not is a principled account of why *the same design move* can be successful in one activity context and fail in another. A bold, attention-capturing feature (high prediction error) is excellent in a museum entrance (the activity frame rewards novelty) but terrible in a hospital recovery room (the activity frame demands restoration). Existing theories can describe the prediction error but cannot explain why the response to it reverses. The implicit/explicit theory can, because it specifies how the activity frame reconfigures the evaluation function.

### 16. Eve Edelstein — Measurement and the Empirical Interface

I want to speak to how we test this in actual buildings.

The neuroscience of architecture has been hampered by the difficulty of measuring brain activity in ecologically valid settings. But we now have mobile EEG, ambulatory skin conductance, eye-tracking glasses, and heart rate variability monitors that can be deployed in real buildings while people perform real tasks.

The implicit/explicit distinction is measurable. Implicit processing shows up in: autonomic markers (HR, SCR, HRV), EEG signatures (event-related potentials, alpha/beta power), eye movement patterns (fixation duration and sequence), and behavioral measures (navigation efficiency, task completion time). Explicit processing shows up in: increased frontal theta power (working memory engagement), gaze patterns reflecting deliberate search rather than stimulus-driven capture, verbal reports, and deliberate behavioral modifications.

The critical test for this theory will involve giving participants different activity frames for the same space and measuring whether the implicit response profile (autonomic, EEG, eye-tracking) shifts as a function of the explicit instruction. If I tell you "evaluate this corridor for patient flow" versus "evaluate this corridor for aesthetic quality," your skin conductance, fixation patterns, and EEG spectra should differ — not because the corridor changed, but because the explicit frame reconfigured the implicit processing ecology.

## PART II: THE SUPERORDINATE CLAIM — DEBATE

**Kirsh**: I want to put the central claim on the table for debate. We have discussed the implicit/explicit distinction as though it were a new theory alongside the existing ten. But I think the correct formulation is stronger: this framework is *superordinate* to the other Tier 1 theories. PP, Berlyne, ART, SRT, Biophilia — all of these are theories of implicit processing channels. The implicit/explicit theory specifies how explicit processing determines which implicit channel is engaged, what stimulus ecology it operates over, and how its output is evaluated.

If this is right, the implication is architectural: you cannot apply any of the existing frameworks without first specifying the activity frame, because the activity frame determines the effective stimulus, and the effective stimulus determines which framework's predictions hold. The Goldilocks optimum for complexity (Berlyne) shifts when the activity changes. The restorative potential (ART) shifts when the activity changes. The prediction error profile (PP) shifts when the activity changes. The implicit/explicit theory tells you *how* and *why* they shift.

**Hohwy**: I want to push back on the word "superordinate." PP is already a very general information-processing framework. What you are calling "explicit activity frame selection" can be modeled within PP as hierarchical model selection — the brain maintaining competing generative models and allocating precision to the one that best fits current goals. I would say the implicit/explicit distinction is *absorbed by* PP at a sufficiently high level of the hierarchy, not superordinate to it.

**Clark**: Jakob, I think you are demonstrating exactly the problem I flagged. You can *describe* activity selection in PP terms — "precision-weighted model selection" — but this is a redescription, not an explanation. PP does not predict when or why a person will shift from one model to another. It can only retrospectively characterize the shift in its own vocabulary. The implicit/explicit theory, by contrast, makes *process-level* predictions: activity selection requires working memory (Evans), engages anterior PFC in the hierarchical control cascade (Badre), operates through beta-frequency top-down bias signals (Miller), and its deployment is gated by expected value of control computations (Botvinick). PP has no mechanism at this level of specificity for the selection event itself.

**Evans**: I agree with Andy. The dual-process distinction is not merely taxonomic. It identifies a *processing bottleneck* — working memory — that is computationally and neurally distinct from the processes it controls. Working memory is limited to approximately four items, requires active maintenance via persistent neural firing or oscillatory coupling, and is metabolically expensive. This bottleneck is what makes Type 2 processing qualitatively different from Type 1, not merely "more precise" Type 1. The PP framework does not contain this bottleneck; it operates on a single computational principle (PE minimization) with a single currency (precision). The dual-process framework says: there are two fundamentally different *kinds* of computation, and one of them configures the other.

**Miller**: The neural evidence supports Jonathan's point. Top-down control via PFC is not just "more processing." It involves a different oscillatory regime — beta-frequency coherence between PFC and posterior cortex, which is distinct from the gamma-frequency processing that characterizes bottom-up sensory analysis. These are different neural dynamics, not different amounts of the same dynamic. When we record from PFC during task-switching, we see the entire oscillatory landscape reconfigure in under 200 milliseconds. PP describes a single computational architecture. The brain appears to have *two*.

**Bar**: I want to complicate the "superordinate" claim slightly. I agree that explicit processing configures which implicit channel operates. But the relationship is not one-directional. The implicit system — the 130-millisecond predictive gist — *constrains* which explicit engagements are possible. You cannot select an activity frame that is wildly incongruent with the perceptual gist without paying a substantial cognitive cost. If the space reads immediately as threatening (implicit), deploying a calm, contemplative activity frame (explicit) requires significant cognitive control resources and may not be sustainable. The implicit sets the *palette* from which the explicit selects.

**Botvinick**: Moshe's point connects directly to the expected value of control computation. The implicit assessment determines the *baseline* — the default Type 1 response. The EVC calculation then determines whether deploying Type 2 control is worth the cost, given the deviation between the baseline and the desired state. So yes, the relationship is bidirectional: explicit configures implicit, but implicit determines the cost of that configuration.

**Kirsh**: This is excellent. The theory is not that explicit is "above" implicit in a simple hierarchy. It is that the two systems operate in a *dynamic loop*:

1. Implicit processing generates an initial environmental assessment (Type 1: gist, affect, prediction).
2. The salience network evaluates whether this assessment warrants explicit engagement (Lucina's switching mechanism, Matthew's EVC).
3. If explicit engagement is triggered, Type 2 processing selects an activity frame (Badre's hierarchical control, Miller's beta-frequency bias).
4. The activity frame reconfigures the implicit processing ecology (the epistemic bubble).
5. The reconfigured implicit system generates new assessments — which may or may not trigger further explicit engagement.

This is a cybernetic loop, not a hierarchy. But it is nonetheless *superordinate* in the sense that you cannot specify the operating conditions for *any* of the implicit-level theories without specifying where in this loop the person currently is.

## PART III: CANDIDATE ARCHITECTURAL PHENOMENA

The panel identifies twelve phenomena where the implicit/explicit distinction is architecturally consequential.

### Phenomenon 1: Activity-Relative Complexity Preferences

The same physical space has different optimal complexity levels depending on the activity. A moderately complex visual environment is optimal for focused work (Berlyne's inverted-U, ART's soft fascination) but a highly complex environment may be optimal for creative exploration. The activity frame shifts the Goldilocks optimum.

**Existing framework limitation**: Berlyne predicts a single optimum for a given stimulus. ART predicts restoration from a single type of environment. Neither can explain why the optimum shifts with activity.

### Phenomenon 2: Expertise-Modulated Aesthetic Response

An architect and a layperson have systematically different aesthetic responses to the same building. The architect's years of explicit training have reconfigured implicit processing: different features are salient, different associations are activated, different predictions are generated.

**Existing framework limitation**: PP and Berlyne treat the response as determined by stimulus properties and generic prior knowledge. They cannot explain expert/novice differences without acknowledging that explicit training reshapes implicit machinery.

### Phenomenon 3: Semantic Override of Perceptual Fluency

A space that is perceptually fluent (Type 1: processed easily, positive valence) receives a negative evaluation because of explicit semantic content. Example: a beautiful corporate lobby that a climate activist finds objectionable because it represents fossil fuel wealth. The explicit meaning overrides the implicit fluency.

**Existing framework limitation**: Fractal Fluency, PP, and Berlyne predict that high perceptual fluency → positive response. They cannot explain evaluative reversal driven by meaning.

### Phenomenon 4: Hospital Corridor Reframing

The same corridor is experienced as threatening by a patient's family ("a place where people die"), efficient by a nurse ("well-organized for rapid response"), and interesting by an architecture student ("a compelling use of natural light"). Same physical stimulus, three different phenomenological experiences.

**Existing framework limitation**: All implicit-level theories predict a single response profile for a given stimulus configuration. Only the implicit/explicit framework can explain activity-relative response.

### Phenomenon 5: The Decision to Persist in a Challenging Space

A grad student continues working in an uncomfortable, noisy library because the discomfort is outweighed by the instrumental value of being near reference materials. This is Type 2 override of Type 1 approach/avoidance: the student *explicitly* tolerates an environment that implicitly signals "leave."

**Existing framework limitation**: SRT predicts avoidance of stressful environments. ART predicts seeking restorative environments. Neither can explain deliberate persistence in non-optimal environments.

### Phenomenon 6: Wayfinding Under Different Goal States

Navigating a complex building when searching for a specific room (high urgency, focused activity frame) versus exploring the same building as a visitor (low urgency, open activity frame). The same spatial complexity that is frustrating under urgency is delightful under exploration.

**Existing framework limitation**: Prospect-Refuge and wayfinding theories treat spatial legibility as a fixed property. They cannot explain how the evaluation of legibility shifts with goal state.

### Phenomenon 7: The Architecture of Ritual and Sacred Space

Religious spaces produce responses that involve explicit cultural knowledge (Type 2: "this is sacred ground"), embodied simulation (Type 1: the bodily response to vast height and filtered light), and learned emotional associations (cross-talk between hippocampal episodic memories of prior ritual and striatal associative conditioning). The power of sacred architecture depends on the alignment of all three channels.

**Existing framework limitation**: Biophilia and PP can describe the implicit dimension (vast spaces, natural light) but not the explicit dimension (cultural meaning, ritual significance). Embodied cognition can describe the bodily response but not the semantic layer.

### Phenomenon 8: Placebo Architecture

Telling people a building was designed by a famous architect changes their evaluation of it — even when the building is identical to one attributed to an unknown designer. The explicit knowledge (Type 2) modulates the implicit experience (Type 1). This is the architectural analog of the wine-tasting effect (wine rated higher when told it is expensive).

**Existing framework limitation**: All implicit-level theories predict response based on stimulus properties, not on knowledge about the stimulus. Placebo architecture is inexplicable without the explicit channel.

### Phenomenon 9: The Café-Problem: Why People Work in Suboptimal Environments

People often choose to work in busy cafés rather than in quiet, optimally designed home offices. The café is suboptimal by every implicit-level metric (higher noise, more distraction, less ergonomic). But the explicit framing — "working in a café makes me feel productive, connected, part of the world" — overrides the implicit assessment.

**Existing framework limitation**: ART, SRT, and ergonomic optimization models all predict that the quieter, more controlled environment should be preferred. The café phenomenon requires explicit framing effects.

### Phenomenon 10: Architectural Habituation and Re-Seeing

People habituate to their everyday environments — implicit processing becomes increasingly efficient and automatic, and the environment becomes "invisible." But explicit intervention (a visiting friend says "your apartment has amazing light") can trigger *re-seeing* — a shift from habituated Type 1 processing to renewed Type 2 engagement with the same physical stimulus.

**Existing framework limitation**: PP predicts habituation (reduced PE over time). It cannot, on its own, explain the re-seeing event, which requires an external trigger to shift processing mode.

### Phenomenon 11: The McMansion Effect — Explicit Rejection of Implicit Pleasure

Certain architectural designs produce positive implicit responses (large, symmetrical, materially rich) but negative explicit evaluations by informed observers ("it's tasteless," "it violates every principle of proportion"). This creates a measurable conflict between channels — the ACC should activate, response latency should increase, confidence should decrease.

**Existing framework limitation**: Fractal Fluency and Biophilia predict positive responses to symmetry and material richness. They cannot explain why aesthetic knowledge reverses the evaluation.

### Phenomenon 12: Trauma-Space Interactions

A veteran with PTSD enters a space with acoustic properties similar to a combat environment. The implicit system (amygdala-driven fear conditioning, Type 1) generates intense threat response. Explicit knowledge ("I am in a hospital, I am safe") attempts to modulate this response, but the implicit channel may overwhelm the explicit override. The balance depends on PFC integrity, current stress load, and the strength of the conditioned association.

**Existing framework limitation**: SRT and ART have no mechanism for individual trauma history modulating environmental response. PP can model the fear conditioning but has difficulty explaining why the explicit override sometimes succeeds and sometimes fails — this requires the EVC framework (Botvinick) and the conflict detection mechanism (De Neys).

## PART IV: THE SUPERORDINATE FRAMEWORK — FORMAL SPECIFICATION

### T1-11: Implicit–Explicit Dual-Process Theory of Environmental Response

**Scope**: Specifies how explicit cognitive processes (Type 2: goal-directed, working-memory-dependent, deliberate) configure, modulate, and evaluate the outputs of implicit processes (Type 1: automatic, associative, embodied) in architectural and environmental experience.

**Core Claims**:

**C1 (Superordinate Status)**: The implicit/explicit framework is not coordinate with existing T1 theories but superordinate to them. All existing T1 theories (PP, Berlyne, ART, SRT, Biophilia, Fractal Fluency, Prospect-Refuge, Multisensory Integration, Embodied Cognition, Activity Space) describe mechanisms that operate *within the implicit channel*. The implicit/explicit theory specifies the *boundary conditions* under which each operates — specifically, the activity frame that determines the effective stimulus ecology.

**C2 (Activity-Relative Optimum)**: The Goldilocks optimum for any environmental channel (complexity, coherence, fascination, restoration potential, etc.) is activity-relative. The same physical space generates different optimal profiles depending on the activity frame selected by explicit processing.

**C3 (Dynamic Loop)**: The relationship between implicit and explicit processing is a cybernetic loop, not a static hierarchy:
- Implicit → initial assessment → salience evaluation → [explicit engagement or not] → if yes: activity frame selection → reconfiguration of implicit ecology → new implicit assessment → …

**C4 (Measurability)**: The implicit/explicit interaction generates measurable neural and autonomic signatures:
- Conflict between channels → ACC activation, increased response latency, decreased confidence, elevated SCR
- Alignment between channels → processing fluency, rapid response, positive affect
- Explicit override of implicit → increased frontal theta, beta-frequency PFC–posterior coherence, dlPFC activation
- Implicit dominance → vmPFC activation, amygdala/striatal dominance, autonomic drive

**C5 (Sedimentation)**: Expertise and repeated experience cause explicit processing to *sediment* into implicit representations. What was once deliberate becomes automatic. This means the implicit baseline for environmental response is itself a product of prior explicit engagement history — which is individual, cultural, and professional.

**C6 (Cost Gating)**: Explicit engagement is metabolically costly and is gated by the expected value of control (EVC) computation in the ACC/dACC. Not every environmental encounter triggers explicit processing. The theory must specify when explicit engagement is triggered: novelty, conflict detection, task demand, social context, or voluntary initiative.

**C7 (Dual Sense Reconciliation)**: The theory operates with two senses of "implicit/explicit":
- **Kirsh-sense (representational)**: Explicitness is the computational cost of recovering information from a representation. Spatial arrangements that make information cheap to extract are Kirsh-explicit.
- **Kahneman-sense (processing mode)**: Type 1 (automatic, fast, low working memory demand) versus Type 2 (deliberate, slow, high working memory demand).
- **Bridge**: Kirsh-explicit environments *enable* Kahneman-Type 1 processing by reducing the computational cost of information extraction. Kirsh-implicit environments *require* Kahneman-Type 2 processing to extract the relevant information. But the design of the environment itself may be the product of Kahneman-Type 2 decisions (an architect's explicit choice to make certain information Kirsh-explicit).

**Boundary Conditions**:
- The theory applies to adult environmental experience. Infant and early childhood responses may operate primarily in the implicit channel before the maturation of PFC-mediated Type 2 processing.
- The theory presupposes normal PFC function. Patients with PFC damage, acute stress (which impairs PFC), or substances that degrade working memory may show reduced explicit modulation.
- The theory is *not* a substitute for the implicit-level theories. It specifies their operating conditions but does not replace their mechanistic accounts.

## PART VI: TEMPLATES

### T_IE_001: Activity-Frame Complexity Retuning

**Antecedent conditions**: Physical environment E with complexity level C. Person P in activity frame A₁ (focused work) then shifted to activity frame A₂ (creative exploration).

**Predicted outcome**: The optimal complexity level for positive affect/performance shifts upward: C_opt(A₂) > C_opt(A₁). The same complexity level C that is optimal for A₁ is below optimal for A₂, producing understimulation.

**Moderating variables**: Expertise in the activity domain (experts tolerate higher complexity); working memory capacity (higher WMC → larger shift); current cognitive load (high load → smaller shift, explicit processing partially occupied).

**Expected effect sizes**: Based on analogous findings in optimal stimulation research, complexity preference shifts of d ≈ 0.4–0.7 between task-focused and explorative activity frames.

**Architectural test case**: Open-plan office with adjustable environmental complexity (screens, partitions, visual density). Measure preference and performance under two instructions: "focus on completing this report" versus "brainstorm novel approaches to this problem."

**Neural signature**: Higher frontal theta and beta PFC–posterior coherence during the retuning event. Shift in EEG alpha power distribution (more posterior alpha suppression under exploration frame).

### T_IE_002: Expertise-Modulated Aesthetic Divergence

**Antecedent conditions**: Architectural space S with feature set F. Two groups: architects (high expertise) and laypersons (low expertise).

**Predicted outcome**: Architects and laypersons show significantly different aesthetic evaluations of S, and these differences are mediated by different implicit processing profiles (different fixation patterns, different autonomic responses, different ERP components), which are themselves the product of sedimented explicit training.

**Moderating variables**: Years of training, type of architectural education (modernist vs. traditional emphasis), cultural background.

**Expected effect sizes**: Expert/novice aesthetic divergence: d ≈ 0.6–1.0 for evaluative ratings; eye-tracking: experts show more distributed fixation patterns with shorter mean fixation duration (d ≈ 0.5–0.8); ERP: experts show earlier and larger P300 to architectural anomalies (d ≈ 0.4–0.6).

**Architectural test case**: Present images or VR environments of buildings spanning canonical to unconventional designs. Record eye movements, EEG, skin conductance, and aesthetic ratings simultaneously.

**Neural signature**: Experts show greater functional connectivity between visual cortex and semantic networks (left temporal); laypersons show stronger amygdala–visual cortex connectivity (affective route dominates).

### T_IE_003: Semantic Override of Perceptual Fluency

**Antecedent conditions**: Space S is perceptually fluent (high symmetry, good proportion, warm materials — positive Type 1 response). Participant P has negative semantic associations with S (e.g., the building houses an organization P finds morally objectionable).

**Predicted outcome**: P reports negative overall evaluation despite measurable positive implicit markers (autonomic relaxation, fast perceptual processing). ACC conflict signal detected. Response latency for evaluation is increased relative to a control group without negative associations.

**Moderating variables**: Strength of moral conviction; salience of the semantic information (cued vs. uncued); cognitive load (under load, implicit positive response dominates).

**Expected effect sizes**: Fluency–evaluation dissociation: d ≈ 0.5–0.8. ACC activation increase: d ≈ 0.4–0.6. Response latency increase: d ≈ 0.3–0.5.

**Architectural test case**: Present the same building images with or without information about the building's occupant/purpose. Measure autonomic responses, ACC activation (EEG source localization or fMRI), and evaluative ratings.

### T_IE_004: The Bridge Mechanism — Activity Frame Shift in Real Time

**Antecedent conditions**: Person P navigating space S under activity frame A₁. At time t, P receives instruction (or decides) to shift to activity frame A₂.

**Predicted outcome**: Measurable reconfiguration of processing within 500ms–2s: (a) shift in PFC beta power spectrum, (b) change in PFC–posterior functional connectivity, (c) change in fixation patterns (eye-tracking), (d) change in autonomic profile. The new processing configuration produces different feature salience, different affective valence, and potentially different Goldilocks optimum for the same space.

**Moderating variables**: Familiarity with A₂ (practiced frames shift faster); similarity between A₁ and A₂ (similar frames → smaller reconfiguration); cognitive flexibility (individual difference).

**Expected effect sizes**: Fixation pattern change: d ≈ 0.6–0.9 (measured by scanpath divergence). EEG reconfiguration: shift in frontal beta power detectable within 200–500ms (d ≈ 0.5–0.7). Autonomic shift: SCR latency 1–3 seconds (d ≈ 0.3–0.5).

**Architectural test case**: Participants in VR environment. Mid-trial instruction change: "You are now evaluating this space for [different purpose]." Record all channels continuously.

### T_IE_005: Cost-Gated Explicit Engagement Threshold

**Antecedent conditions**: Person P in space S. The environment generates prediction error PE at level x. The expected value of control (EVC) is modulated by task importance, novelty, and current cognitive load.

**Predicted outcome**: Explicit engagement (Type 2) is deployed when PE × task_importance > cognitive_cost_threshold. Below threshold: pure implicit processing (Type 1 autopilot). Above threshold: explicit engagement restructures processing.

**Moderating variables**: Current stress level (high stress → elevated cost threshold → less explicit engagement); motivation; individual differences in need for cognition.

**Expected effect sizes**: Threshold detection via pupillometry: pupil dilation increase when threshold is crossed (d ≈ 0.5–0.7). Frontal theta increase at threshold crossing (d ≈ 0.4–0.6).

**Architectural test case**: Gradually increase environmental complexity/novelty during a standardized task. Identify the threshold at which frontal theta and pupil dilation indicate explicit engagement onset. Vary task importance to shift the threshold.

### T_IE_006: Placebo Architecture

**Antecedent conditions**: Identical spaces S₁ and S₂. Participants are told S₁ was designed by a famous architect; S₂ by an unknown designer.

**Predicted outcome**: S₁ receives higher aesthetic ratings, longer viewing times, and more positive autonomic markers than S₂. The effect is mediated by explicit knowledge (Type 2) modulating implicit response (Type 1), and should be attenuated under cognitive load (which degrades Type 2 processing).

**Moderating variables**: Architecture knowledge (experts less susceptible?), suggestibility, cognitive load.

**Expected effect sizes**: Rating difference: d ≈ 0.3–0.5. Load attenuation of the effect: interaction d ≈ 0.2–0.4.

**Architectural test case**: Between-subjects design with identical VR environments, different attribution information. Add a cognitive load condition to test whether the effect depends on Type 2 processing capacity.

## PART VIII: DISAGREEMENTS AND UNRESOLVED ISSUES

### Disagreement 1: Is the framework truly superordinate, or merely complementary?

**Hohwy** maintains that PP can absorb the explicit dimension through hierarchical active inference with sufficiently rich prior specification. On this view, IE is a useful *heuristic* for what PP would predict if fully specified, but it is not a separate theory.

**Evans, Miller, Clark, Kirsh** argue that the working-memory bottleneck and the oscillatory dissociation between top-down beta and bottom-up gamma represent a genuine *architectural* feature of neural computation that PP does not contain. The superordinate status is not just convenient but reflects a real discontinuity in the system.

**Panel consensus**: 12 of 16 panelists endorse the superordinate reading. Hohwy and Bar express reservations, preferring a "strong complementarity" reading that preserves PP's generality. Gallese and Mallgrave are agnostic on the meta-theoretical point but endorse the practical utility.

### Disagreement 2: Is conflict detection Type 1 or Type 2?

**De Neys** argues that conflict detection between heuristic and normative responses is itself an automatic, Type 1 process — a "logical intuition" that operates without deliberate effort.

**Evans** and **Botvinick** argue that some form of minimal monitoring — perhaps not full Type 2 engagement, but more than pure Type 1 — is required for conflict detection, and that this monitoring draws on ACC resources that bridge the two types.

**Panel consensus**: The disagreement is empirically tractable (does conflict detection survive dual-task loading?) and does not affect the architectural application, since both positions agree that the conflict is *detectable* and generates measurable signatures.

### Disagreement 3: How sharp is the Type 1/Type 2 boundary?

**Goel** and **De Neys** present evidence for clean neural dissociations (RLPFC vs. vmPFC). **Clark** and **Gallese** argue for a continuum, with embodied simulation occupying an intermediate zone that is neither purely automatic nor fully deliberate.

**Panel consensus**: The distinction is best treated as a *bimodal distribution* rather than a binary: there are clear prototypical Type 1 and Type 2 cases, but intermediate cases exist. For architectural templates, the distinction is sharp enough to generate different predictions.

### Disagreement 4: Can the theory be tested in ecologically valid settings?

**Edelstein** is optimistic: mobile EEG, eye-tracking, and ambulatory physiology allow real-building studies. **Miller** cautions that oscillatory measurements are degraded by movement artifact in ambulatory settings. **Shohamy** notes that memory-system contributions require controlled laboratory paradigms (fMRI) that are hard to deploy architecturally.

**Panel consensus**: A two-tier empirical strategy. Tier 1: controlled laboratory and VR studies to establish the mechanism. Tier 2: ambulatory studies in real buildings to test ecological validity, accepting some loss of neural measurement precision.

## PART IX: CONSENSUS SYNTHESIS

The panel endorses the following formulation:

**The Implicit–Explicit Dual-Process Theory of Environmental Response (IE-DPT)** holds that adult human experience of built environments is constitutively shaped by the interaction between implicit (Type 1: automatic, associative, embodied, subcortical and posterior-cortical) and explicit (Type 2: deliberate, goal-directed, working-memory-dependent, prefrontal) processing. The explicit channel does not merely add a layer of evaluation on top of implicit response; it *reconfigures the operating conditions* of the implicit channel by selecting the activity frame that determines the effective stimulus ecology. This makes the IE framework superordinate to existing implicit-level theories (PP, Berlyne, ART, SRT, Biophilia, etc.), which describe mechanisms that operate *within* the implicit channel under boundary conditions set by explicit processing.

The theory is mechanized at the neural level through: (a) hierarchical prefrontal cognitive control (Badre), implemented via (b) oscillatory top-down bias signals (Miller), gated by (c) expected value of control computations in the ACC (Botvinick), triggered by (d) salience network detection of behaviorally relevant events (Uddin), and modulated by (e) the balance between hippocampal (explicit-memory) and striatal (implicit-learning) contributions to environmental association (Shohamy). Conflict between implicit and explicit channels is detected by the ACC and generates measurable neural and autonomic signatures (De Neys, Goel).

The theory reconciles two senses of "implicit/explicit": Kirsh's representational sense (computational cost of information extraction) and Kahneman's processing-mode sense (automatic vs. deliberate). Environments that are Kirsh-explicit (information is cheap to extract) enable Kahneman-Type 1 processing; environments that are Kirsh-implicit (information is costly to extract) require Kahneman-Type 2 processing. The design of environments is itself often a product of Kahneman-Type 2 decisions aimed at making future processing Kahneman-Type 1 — which is the cognitive foundation of good architectural design.
