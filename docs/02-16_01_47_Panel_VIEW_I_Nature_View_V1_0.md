# PANEL VIEW-I: VIEW, NATURE CONTACT, AND VISUAL ACCESS TO THE OUTDOORS
## Mechanistic Templates for How Windows, Views, and Nature Visibility Affect Wellbeing
## February 16, 2026 — Document 47

---

## Panel Charge

Roger Ulrich's finding that hospital patients with a view of trees recovered faster than those with a view of a brick wall (Ulrich, 1984, ~7,000 citations) is the single most cited study in the architecture-wellbeing literature. Yet the project has no dedicated template for the VIEW mechanism. Ulrich's finding is referenced by T1 (fractal fluency of natural scenes), the Biophilia reduction (Doc 19), SC2 (isovist as visual prediction horizon), and T25 (directed attention and ART). But the specific mechanism — how the CONTENT of what is visible through a window affects the occupant — has not been formalized as a template.

The gap is not merely that Ulrich is uncited but that the VIEW mechanism involves a convergence of multiple existing templates that has not been made explicit: a window view of nature simultaneously engages fractal fluency (T1), prospect/refuge balance (T5/SC2), attention restoration (T25/ART), circadian light entrainment (L2), dynamic temporal variation (L5), and ecological familiarity (MAT4's convergence principle applied to visual scenes). The view is a multi-channel stimulus, and the convergence is what makes it powerful.

The panel is charged with producing 1–2 templates covering: (a) view content as a PE mechanism — how the brain generates predictions about what should be visible from a window and how different view contents (nature, urban, wall, sky) modulate PE, attention restoration, and affect, and (b) the window as a multi-channel environmental mediator — how windows provide not just a view but daylight (L2/L3), temporal information (L5), acoustic connection, and olfactory access to the outdoors. These must connect to T1 (fractal fluency), T25 (directed attention/ART), T29 (allostatic regulation), L2/L3 (circadian/daylight), SC2 (isovist), and the Biophilia reduction (Doc 19).

## Panel Composition

### View, Nature, and Health

**1. Roger Ulrich** (Chalmers University of Technology, Sweden; formerly Texas A&M)
Author of the foundational study demonstrating that hospital patients with a view of trees recovered faster, required less analgesic medication, and had fewer negative nursing notes than patients with a view of a brick wall (Ulrich, 1984, ~7,000 citations). His subsequent Stress Reduction Theory (SRT) proposes that nature scenes produce rapid, automatic stress reduction through an evolutionarily prepared response to natural environments — environments that signaled safety, resources, and navigability during human evolutionary history (Ulrich et al., 1991, ~5,000 citations).
*Role*: Establishes the health-outcome evidence. Ulrich's finding is the empirical anchor for the entire view-and-nature literature. His SRT provides one theoretical interpretation: nature views reduce stress through an automatic, evolved response. The PE framework offers a complementary interpretation: nature views provide multi-channel prediction confirmation (fractal patterns match the visual system's prediction for natural scenes; green vegetation confirms ecological safety; spatial depth confirms prospect; dynamic elements confirm temporal variation) that reduces allostatic load.

**2. Rachel and Stephen Kaplan** (University of Michigan, Department of Psychology; both now retired)
Developers of Attention Restoration Theory (ART) — the framework proposing that natural environments restore directed attention capacity that has been depleted by sustained effortful focus (Kaplan, 1995, ~7,000 citations; Kaplan & Kaplan, 1989, ~5,000 citations). ART identifies four properties of restorative environments: (a) being away (psychological distance from routine demands), (b) extent (scope large enough to engage the mind), (c) fascination (effortless attention to interesting stimuli), and (d) compatibility (match between environmental demands and the person's purposes). Natural environments score high on all four; built environments typically score lower.
*Role*: Provides the attentional mechanism. T25 already formalizes ART at the general level. The Kaplans' specific contribution to VIEW is that VISUAL ACCESS to nature — even through a window — provides attentional restoration because natural scenes engage "soft fascination": involuntary attention to gently interesting stimuli (leaves moving, clouds drifting, water flowing) that permits the directed attention system to rest. A window view of nature is a restoration device embedded in the wall of the building.

**3. Mathew White** (University of Exeter, European Centre for Environment and Human Health)
Lead researcher on the BlueHealth project and co-developer of nature dose-response research — the question of how MUCH nature contact produces HOW MUCH benefit (White et al., 2019, ~1,500 citations; White et al., 2013, ~600 citations). His large-scale epidemiological work established that 120 minutes per week of nature contact is the threshold for significant health and wellbeing benefits, and that even small amounts of nature visibility (green space visible from home) produce measurable effects on self-reported health.
*Role*: Provides the dose-response perspective. Architecture mediates nature contact primarily through VISUAL access (windows, courtyards, roof gardens, atria with planting). White's work establishes that this visual access is not merely decorative — it contributes measurably to health outcomes. The dose-response curve suggests that even modest nature visibility (a window with partial tree view) produces benefits, with diminishing returns at high doses. This is the empirical basis for view as a design variable rather than a luxury.

**4. Peter Grahn** (Swedish University of Agricultural Sciences, Alnarp)
Researcher on therapeutic gardens and the health effects of nature quality — not just presence vs. absence but WHAT KIND of nature contact produces WHAT KIND of benefit (Grahn & Stigsdotter, 2003, ~1,000 citations). His "perceived sensory dimensions" framework identifies eight qualities of nature environments that separately contribute to wellbeing: serene, wild, lush, spacious, rich in species, a refuge, festive/social, and cultural/historical. Different populations need different qualities: stressed individuals benefit most from serene and refuge; socially isolated individuals benefit most from festive/social.
*Role*: Provides the nature-quality differentiation. Not all nature views are equal. A view of a manicured lawn produces different effects than a view of a wild forest, a flowing river, or a flower garden. Grahn's framework suggests that the specific PE contribution of a nature view depends on its perceptual properties: serene natural scenes produce low PE (prediction confirmation → calm); wild scenes produce moderate PE (dynamic, unpredictable elements → soft fascination); species-rich scenes produce moderate-high PE (visual complexity → sustained engagement).

### Window Science and Daylighting

**5. Christoph Reinhart** (MIT, Building Technology Program)
Expert on daylight modeling and building simulation whose work quantifies the non-visual benefits of windows — not just illumination but view quality, daylight autonomy, and spatial daylight distribution (Reinhart et al., 2006, ~600 citations). His research demonstrates that window design involves a multi-objective optimization: maximizing daylight (L2/L3), managing glare (L1), providing view (this template), and controlling solar heat gain. The view function is often sacrificed to the other objectives — frosted glass for privacy, electrochromic glass for glare control, deep floor plates that push workstations away from windows.
*Role*: Provides the building-science perspective on window design as a view delivery system. Reinhart's work establishes that VIEW is a quantifiable window function — "view out" metrics (EN 17037) assess the quality of what is visible, including the fraction of sky, landscape, and ground visible from each workstation. This connects the psychological literature (Ulrich, Kaplan) to the architectural design process: view quality can be modeled, optimized, and specified.

**6. Lisa Heschong** (formerly Heschong Mahone Group; author of *Thermal Delight in Architecture*)
Researcher who conducted the landmark studies on daylight and human performance, demonstrating that students in classrooms with more daylight performed better on standardized tests, and that retail stores with skylights had higher sales (Heschong et al., 2002, ~500 citations). Her work separates the VIEW effect from the DAYLIGHT effect: students in rooms with windows to nature performed better than students in rooms with windows to built environments, even controlling for light levels. This suggests that view content contributes independently of illumination.
*Role*: Provides the evidence that separates view from daylight. Heschong's finding that view CONTENT matters (nature > built, controlling for light level) is critical for isolating the view mechanism from the daylight mechanism. In the PE framework: L2 and L3 formalize the light benefits of windows; VIEW-I formalizes the content benefits. Both operate through windows, but through different channels.

### Environmental Psychology and Biophilia

**7. Stephen Kellert** (posthumous; formerly Yale University School of Forestry and Environmental Studies)
The scholar who most systematically applied E. O. Wilson's biophilia hypothesis to architectural design (Kellert, 2005, ~500 citations; Kellert et al., 2008, ~700 citations). His framework identifies six elements of biophilic design: environmental features (natural materials, water, plants, animals), natural shapes and forms (botanical motifs, fractal patterns), natural patterns and processes (sensory variability, temporal change), light and space (natural light, spatial variability), place-based relationships (ecological connection, landscape orientation), and evolved human-nature relationships (prospect/refuge, curiosity, mastery).
*Role*: Provides the design framework. Kellert's biophilic design elements are essentially a practitioner's checklist for the multi-channel nature-convergence that this panel formalizes. The PE framework refines Kellert's list by specifying the MECHANISMS through which each element operates: natural materials → MAT4 convergence; natural patterns → T1 fractal fluency; natural light → L2/L3 circadian; prospect/refuge → SC2/T5; and now view of nature → VIEW1's multi-channel convergence.

**8. Richard Louv** (journalist and author, *Last Child in the Woods*)
Author whose concept of "nature-deficit disorder" — the idea that reduced contact with nature produces cognitive, emotional, and physical costs, particularly in children — has influenced policy and design worldwide (Louv, 2005, ~3,000 citations). While not a scientist, Louv synthesized a substantial research literature and created the conceptual framework that motivates nature-accessible building design: if nature contact is a human need, then buildings that deny nature access are denying a basic requirement for wellbeing.
*Role*: Provides the cultural-design motivation. Louv's "nature-deficit" concept frames the architectural question: buildings mediate the human-nature relationship, and buildings that eliminate nature contact (windowless classrooms, deep-plan offices, sealed environments) produce deficits that manifest as attention problems, stress, and reduced wellbeing. The PE framework offers the mechanism: nature contact provides multi-channel prediction confirmation (T1 fractal, MAT4 convergence, ART soft fascination, L2 circadian) that contributes to allostatic balance (T29). Nature-deficit IS chronic PE from missing channels.

## Position Statements

### VIEW AND HEALTH

**Position Statement 1: Roger Ulrich — The View That Heals**

The original finding requires no embellishment: patients recovering from cholecystectomy in rooms with a view of deciduous trees had shorter hospital stays (7.96 vs. 8.70 days), required less analgesic medication, and had fewer negative evaluative comments in nursing notes than patients in otherwise identical rooms with a view of a brick wall (Ulrich, 1984, ~7,000 citations). The effect size is clinically meaningful — nearly a full day shorter recovery. The finding has been replicated and extended: intensive care patients with nature views have lower blood pressure and shorter stays; prison inmates with nature views report fewer sick calls; office workers with nature views report lower stress and higher satisfaction.

The mechanism I proposed — Stress Reduction Theory — posits that natural scenes produce rapid, automatic parasympathetic activation through an evolved "safety signal." Seeing green vegetation, water, and spatial depth signals an environment that historically provided resources, refuge, and navigability. The response is fast (within minutes), automatic (does not require conscious appreciation of nature), and physiological (reduced cortisol, reduced heart rate, increased parasympathetic tone).

For the PE framework: nature views produce multi-channel prediction CONFIRMATION. The visual system evolved in natural environments and its prediction structures are calibrated to natural statistics — 1/f spectral profiles (T1), fractal geometry (D ≈ 1.3), depth perspective, and green wavelength dominance. When a window provides a nature view, these predictions are confirmed across multiple channels simultaneously → processing fluency → reduced physiological arousal → reduced allostatic load. A brick wall view provides only one channel (surface texture, and not a fractal one) and denies the others → reduced prediction confirmation → no restorative effect.

**Position Statement 2: Rachel and Stephen Kaplan — Attention Restoration Through the Window**

The window view of nature serves a specific cognitive function: it provides the "soft fascination" that allows directed attention to rest and recover (Kaplan, 1995, ~7,000 citations). Directed attention is a finite resource that is depleted by sustained effortful focus — the demands of work, study, decision-making. When directed attention is depleted, cognitive performance declines: errors increase, patience decreases, irritability rises. Nature scenes restore directed attention because they engage involuntary attention through gently fascinating stimuli (leaves moving, cloud patterns, birds) that do not demand effortful processing.

The window is the delivery mechanism for attention restoration in buildings. An office worker who looks out a window at trees for a few minutes is not "wasting time" — the attentional system is recovering from depletion, and the worker returns to the task with restored capacity. An office without windows (or with windows facing a blank wall) provides no restoration opportunity. The attentional cost of windowless environments is not dramatic in any single hour but cumulative over days and weeks: chronic attentional depletion without recovery → sustained cognitive degradation → reduced productivity and increased error rates.

The PE framework adds precision: the window view provides stimuli that engage the "default mode network" rather than the "task-positive network" — a shift from effortful, top-down processing to receptive, bottom-up processing. Natural scenes are processed with minimal top-down prediction error (their statistical properties match the visual system's baseline expectations) while maintaining sufficient complexity to prevent complete disengagement. This is the attentional Goldilocks zone: enough engagement to prevent boredom, insufficient demand to require effort.

**Position Statement 3: Mathew White — Nature Dose and Architectural Access**

The question is not WHETHER nature contact benefits health but HOW MUCH and THROUGH WHAT CHANNELS of access (White et al., 2019, ~1,500 citations). Large-scale epidemiological data from 20,000+ respondents establishes that 120 minutes per week of nature contact is the threshold for significant health and wellbeing benefits. Below this threshold, benefits are present but modest; above it, benefits plateau. The 120-minute threshold applies to nature contact through ALL channels — walking in nature, sitting in a garden, and VIEWING nature through a window.

Architecture mediates nature contact primarily through visual access. A window with a view of trees, a courtyard with planting, a roof garden, an atrium with living walls — all provide nature contact minutes that contribute to the 120-minute weekly threshold. The implication: building design that maximizes nature VISIBILITY (not just nature proximity) directly contributes to occupant health. Every workstation with a nature view is providing a continuous (during daylight hours) stream of nature contact that accumulates toward the health threshold.

### VIEW QUALITY AND MULTI-CHANNEL MEDIATION

**Position Statement 4: Peter Grahn — Not All Nature Views Are Equal**

The quality of a nature view — not just its presence — determines its restorative effect (Grahn & Stigsdotter, 2003, ~1,000 citations). A view of a dense, biodiverse forest with moving water is more restorative than a view of a mown lawn. A view of a wild meadow with flowers is more restorative than a view of a parking lot with a few trees. The restorative capacity of the nature view depends on its perceptual properties: spatial depth (prospect), vegetative density (enclosure without entrapment), dynamic elements (wind, water, fauna), species richness (complexity), and absence of threat (no traffic, no construction, no human density).

These properties map directly onto the PE framework. Spatial depth → SC2 prospect (long isovist, high prediction range). Vegetative density → T1 fractal complexity (D ≈ 1.3 from natural branching patterns). Dynamic elements → L5-like temporal variation (visual change that maintains soft fascination). Species richness → T2 complexity Goldilocks (sufficient variety to sustain engagement without overwhelming). Absence of threat → T5 safety confirmation (low HPA activation). A high-quality nature view engages MULTIPLE restorative mechanisms simultaneously — it is a multi-channel convergence, structurally parallel to MAT4 (wood) and L3 (daylight).

**Position Statement 5: Christoph Reinhart — The Window as Multi-Channel Mediator**

A window is not one thing. It is a multi-channel environmental mediator that simultaneously provides: (a) daylight — circadian (L2) and visual (L3) light, varying in spectrum, intensity, and direction through the day; (b) view — visual access to exterior content (nature, sky, urban scene); (c) temporal information — time of day (sun position), weather (clouds, rain, snow), season (foliage change, snow); (d) connection to exterior space — the sense that the interior is part of a larger environment; and (e) in operable windows, acoustic connection (birdsong, rain, wind) and olfactory connection (fresh air, vegetation, petrichor).

Architecture that reduces the window to a single function (light delivery → frosted glass; view → fixed glazing; ventilation → operable but opaque louver) loses the multi-channel convergence that makes windows powerful. The PE framework predicts that the full multi-channel window (clear glazing, nature view, operable, with daylight and temporal variation) produces stronger wellbeing effects than any single channel alone — the same super-additive convergence principle that operates for L3 daylight and MAT4 wood.

**Position Statement 6: Lisa Heschong — View Content Matters Independently of Light**

The critical finding from the school daylighting studies: view CONTENT predicts performance independently of illumination level (Heschong et al., 2002, ~500 citations). Students in classrooms with views of nature (trees, gardens, sky) performed better than students with views of built environments (parking lots, other buildings), even when light levels were comparable. This isolates the view-content mechanism from the daylight mechanism — something this panel is specifically charged with doing.

The PE interpretation: daylight benefits (L2 circadian, L3 convergence) are about the LIGHT that comes through the window. View benefits (VIEW1) are about the CONTENT that is visible through the window. Both use the window as their medium, but they engage different neural pathways and produce different benefits. Light quality determines circadian regulation and alertness. View content determines attentional restoration and stress reduction. The full window benefit is the SUM of both.

## Cross-Examination and Debate

### Debate 1: Is It Biophilia or Is It Fractal Fluency?

**Challenge**: Ulrich explains nature benefits through evolved biophilia — an innate affinity for natural environments. Taylor explains them through fractal fluency — processing ease for natural spatial frequencies. These are different mechanisms. Which is correct?

**Ulrich**: SRT proposes an evolved, automatic response to natural environments as safety signals. The response is fast, subcortical, and physiological. This is a PREPARED response — the organism is predisposed to respond positively to settings that historically signaled survival resources.

**Palmer** (from COL-I, by analogy): The EVT model for color shows that preferences reflect the statistical valence of associated objects. By analogy, nature-view preferences may reflect the statistical valence of natural environments — they are associated with safety, resources, and survival. The mechanism is associative, not specifically fractal.

**Taylor** (from VF-I, by reference): Fractal fluency provides a specific, measurable mechanism: natural scenes have D ≈ 1.3 fractal statistics that match the visual system's processing structure, producing maximal fluency and minimal PE. This is testable and parameterizable in a way that "biophilia" is not.

**Panel consensus**: **Both mechanisms likely contribute, and the PE framework accommodates both.** Fractal fluency (T1) provides the visual-perceptual mechanism — why natural scenes are easy to process. Evolutionary preparedness (biophilia) provides the motivational mechanism — why the brain assigns positive valence to processed-fluent natural scenes rather than just neutral fluency. The multi-channel convergence model (VIEW1) incorporates both: fractal fluency is one channel; ecological safety signaling is another; attentional restoration is a third. The convergence of multiple mechanisms, not any single one, explains why nature views are so consistently beneficial.

## Panel Synthesis: What VIEW1 Reveals About Multi-Channel Convergence

### Opening Remarks from the Chair

**Ulrich**: This panel has produced a single template, VIEW1, rather than the two the charge anticipated. I want to explain why this is correct. The charge distinguished between (a) view content as a PE mechanism and (b) the window as a multi-channel mediator. During the panel's work, it became clear that these are not two mechanisms — they are one mechanism with different emphases. The nature view IS a multi-channel stimulus. Separating the channels from the content would have produced a redundancy: the "content" template listing five benefits and the "mediator" template listing the same five channels as delivery pathways. VIEW1 captures both: the content IS the convergence.

### The Third Convergence Template

**Kellert**: VIEW1 now joins L3 (daylight) and MAT4 (wood) as the third template exhibiting the multi-channel convergence structure. This convergence pattern — a natural stimulus engaging five or more PE channels simultaneously, producing super-additive effects — may be the single most important structural insight of the template system. The pattern recurs because natural stimuli evolved alongside human perceptual systems: they are multi-dimensional precisely because the sensory systems that process them are multi-dimensional.

**Reinhart**: The architectural implication is immediate and practical. A window that provides BOTH daylight (L3 convergence) AND a nature view (VIEW1 convergence) is delivering TWO super-additive bundles simultaneously. This explains a finding that has puzzled building science for decades: why windows are valued far out of proportion to what any single-variable analysis would predict. The answer is that a window is not one variable — it is ten, and they are correlated in nature but can be decoupled by design. Frosted glass retains L3 but eliminates VIEW1. A video wall provides some VIEW1 channels but none of L3. Only a clear window with a nature view delivers the full convergence of both.

**White**: The dose-response perspective adds nuance. The 120-minute weekly threshold for nature contact (White et al., 2019) is cumulative across all channels. A worker with eight hours of nature-view access per workday accumulates 40 hours per week — far above threshold. But the benefit does not scale linearly. It is the ABSENCE of nature access that is most costly, producing a steep deficit; PRESENCE produces a more gradual benefit curve. Architecturally: the priority is ensuring that NO occupant has zero nature access, rather than maximizing view quality for those who already have some.

### Where Evidence Is Strong, Where It Is Contested

**Kaplan**: The evidence for attention restoration through nature views is robust at the aggregate level — multiple studies, consistent direction, biologically plausible mechanism. The SPECIFIC claim that "soft fascination" is the operative mechanism, as opposed to mere distraction from demanding tasks, or reduction of mental fatigue through positive affect, remains debated. Berman, Jonides, and Kaplan (2008, ~2,500 citations) demonstrated that walking in nature improved directed attention performance more than walking in an urban environment, but the component process driving the improvement was not isolated. The PE framing (nature views produce low PE across multiple channels, permitting executive attention to rest) is a plausible mechanistic refinement of ART, but it is not yet experimentally distinguished from the original ART formulation.

**Grahn**: The nature-quality gradient is empirically supported but the CHANNEL mapping I proposed — which view properties engage which PE channels — is a theoretical synthesis by this panel, not an experimentally tested model. We know that biodiverse natural views are more restorative than sparse ones (Grahn & Stigsdotter, 2003; Fuller et al., 2007, ~1,200 citations). We do not yet know whether this is because biodiverse views engage MORE channels (the convergence account) or because they engage the SAME channels more strongly (the intensity account). Channel-separation experiments have not been conducted for views.

**Panel consensus on maturity**: VIEW1 is **established** for the overall finding (nature views produce health, cognitive, and affective benefits) and **supported** for the five-channel convergence model (each channel is independently supported; the convergence claim is theoretically well-motivated and structurally parallel to L3 and MAT4, but the super-additive combination has not been directly tested through channel-isolation experiments).

## Testable Predictions and Critical Experiments

### Panel Discussion: What Should We Predict, and What Would Falsify VIEW1?

**Ulrich**: VIEW1's multi-channel convergence model generates predictions that a single-mechanism account (biophilia alone, or ART alone, or fractal fluency alone) does not. These predictions are where the template earns its keep.

**Prediction 1: The Channel Degradation Experiment**

**Reinhart**: This is the direct analog to the L3 convergence test. Start with a full nature view (clear window, biodiverse landscape with spatial depth, temporal dynamics, and ecological safety cues). Then systematically degrade individual channels:

- **Remove fractal fluency (Channel 1)**: Replace the nature view with a non-fractal but still "natural" scene — a smooth lake surface, a flat snow field, a mown lawn with no trees. Prediction: reduced benefit relative to full view, but substantial benefit retained through Channels 2–5.
- **Remove prospect (Channel 2)**: Provide a nature view with close vegetation that blocks spatial depth — a dense hedge at short distance. Prediction: reduced benefit, but Channels 1, 3, 4, 5 sustain partial convergence.
- **Remove temporal dynamics (Channel 4)**: Display a HIGH-RESOLUTION PHOTOGRAPH of the same nature scene in the window frame. Prediction: significant but not total loss — the static image retains Channels 1, 2, and partially 5, but loses Channels 3 and 4 (no soft fascination from movement, no temporal variation).

**White**: The critical falsification condition: if removing ANY single channel eliminates the ENTIRE nature-view benefit, then that channel is dominant, the convergence model is wrong, and a simpler single-mechanism account suffices. Conversely, if each degradation produces a PARTIAL decrement and the decrements are roughly additive (or super-additive when combined), the convergence model is confirmed.

**Heschong**: The photo vs. real view comparison is particularly informative because it has been studied — and the results consistently favor the real view (Kahn et al., 2008, ~500 citations). A plasma screen displaying a high-definition nature scene produces some stress reduction but significantly less than a real window view. The convergence model EXPLAINS this: the photo retains Channel 1 (fractal statistics) but loses Channel 3 (movement-based soft fascination), Channel 4 (temporal variation), and partially Channel 5 (the brain detects the absence of ecological signals — fixed light, no wind, no depth parallax).

**Prediction 2: The View-Quality Dose-Response Gradient**

**Grahn**: VIEW1 predicts a QUALITY gradient, not just a presence/absence effect. The five-channel model specifies that view quality is a function of how many channels are engaged and at what intensity. This generates a quantitative prediction:

- Biodiverse landscape with water, depth, dynamics → 5 channels engaged → maximum convergence
- Managed garden with trees → 4 channels engaged → substantial convergence
- Sparse vegetation (single tree, lawn) → 2–3 channels weakly engaged → modest convergence
- Sky only → 1 channel (temporal variation) → minimal convergence
- Blank wall → 0 channels → no convergence

**White**: Test: measure stress recovery (cortisol, heart rate variability), attention restoration (backward digit span), and self-reported affect across these five view conditions, controlling for daylight exposure. If the recovery metrics follow the predicted five-level gradient — not just a binary nature/no-nature split — the quality-gradient prediction is confirmed. If all nature views produce equivalent benefit regardless of quality, the convergence model over-specifies and a simpler "nature present = benefit" account suffices.

**Prediction 3: The Convergence Interaction Between VIEW1 and L3**

**Reinhart**: The most architecturally consequential prediction is that VIEW1 and L3 interact super-additively. A clear window with a nature view delivers BOTH convergences; the combined benefit should exceed the sum of (daylight without view) + (view without daylight).

**Test design**: Four conditions, all in identical rooms:
- **Full window**: clear glazing, nature view, daylight → VIEW1 + L3
- **Daylight only**: frosted glazing (high light transmission, no view) → L3 only
- **View only**: clear glazing with nature view, but room illuminated by artificial light matched to daylight levels → VIEW1 only (partially — some L3 channels present through the window)
- **Neither**: windowless room with matched artificial illumination → baseline

If the full-window condition exceeds the sum of the daylight-only and view-only decrements from baseline, the super-additive convergence prediction is confirmed. This has immediate design implications: it means that preserving BOTH functions of windows (light AND view) is not just desirable but disproportionately important — degrading either one costs more than half the total benefit.

## VIEW-II Prospectus: What a Second View Panel Would Do

### Priority 1: Channel-Separation Experiments

The most urgent need is empirical testing of the five-channel convergence model through the channel-degradation experiment described in Prediction 1. VIEW-II would review existing evidence for channel independence (the photo-vs-real-view literature provides partial channel separation) and design or evaluate protocols that isolate individual channels.

**What VIEW-II would produce**: Either a confirmed convergence model with estimated channel weights, or a simplified model identifying 1–2 dominant channels.

**Panelists who would return**: Ulrich (stress reduction endpoint), Kaplan (attention restoration endpoint), White (dose-response framework).

**Additional panelist needed**: Kahn (University of Washington, technology-mediated nature experience, ~500 citations on virtual vs. real nature) or Hartig (Uppsala University, restorative environments research, ~8,000 total citations — the most rigorous experimental work on ART and SRT).

### Priority 2: View Quality Parameterization

Grahn's quality gradient (five levels from biodiverse landscape to blank wall) is theoretically motivated but not quantitatively parameterized. VIEW-II would establish dose-response functions for view quality → stress reduction, view quality → attention restoration, and view quality → self-reported wellbeing.

**What VIEW-II would produce**: Quantitative mapping from view properties (percent vegetation, biodiversity index, spatial depth, water presence, temporal dynamics) to predicted effect sizes for stress, attention, and affect.

**Panelists who would return**: Grahn (nature quality dimensions), White (dose-response methodology).

**Additional panelist needed**: Gilchrist (University of Northampton, visual ecology and landscape preference, ~200 citations) or Velarde (restorative qualities of different landscape types, ~600 citations).

### Priority 3: Digital Nature and Virtual Views

The increasing use of digital nature displays, virtual windows, and biophilic media in windowless spaces requires assessment: which of VIEW1's five channels are engaged by virtual nature? The photo-vs-real-view literature suggests Channel 4 (temporal) and parts of Channel 5 (ecological signals requiring depth cues) are lost. But high-fidelity dynamic displays (4K video with natural sound) may recover Channel 4. VIEW-II would assess the channel profile of virtual nature substitutes.

**What VIEW-II would produce**: A channel-by-channel assessment of virtual nature displays, specifying which channels are preserved, degraded, or absent, and predicting the fraction of the full nature-view benefit that virtual substitutes can deliver.

**Panelists who would return**: Reinhart (building technology), Heschong (view vs. light separation).

**Critical addition**: Kahn (2011, *Technological Nature*, ~400 citations — the leading researcher on whether technology-mediated nature contact produces genuine restoration or merely a placebo-like partial effect).

### Priority 4: Interior Nature — Plants, Green Walls, Atria

VIEW1 is formalized for EXTERIOR nature visible through windows. But interior nature (potted plants, living walls, planted atria, interior water features) also engages some convergent channels. VIEW-II would assess which channels are activated by interior nature and whether the convergence principle extends to nature that is IN the room rather than visible THROUGH the room boundary.

**What VIEW-II would produce**: A modified convergence assessment for interior nature, specifying channel engagement (e.g., interior plants engage Channel 1 fractal and Channel 5 ecological safety, but not Channel 2 prospect or Channel 4 macro-temporal variation).

### Estimated Impact of VIEW-II on Coverage

If VIEW-II accomplishes Priorities 1–2, the channel-separation evidence would strengthen VIEW1's calibration from "partially calibrated" to "substantially calibrated," enabling the system to make quantitative predictions about view-quality effects. No A-domain rating would change because VIEW1 is cross-cutting, but the precision of evaluations invoking VIEW1 would improve substantially.

## Scope Exclusions — Work That Belongs Elsewhere

**Acoustic environment and nature sound**: Birdsong, water sounds, wind — the acoustic dimension of nature contact — belongs in a future acoustic-nature panel or as an extension to the M-series. VIEW1 addresses VISUAL access to nature only. Operable windows that provide acoustic nature access are noted as a moderator but not formalized.

**Olfactory access to nature**: The smell of vegetation, petrichor, and fresh air through operable windows is addressed by OLF1 (olfactory PE at transitions) and would be extended in an OLF-II panel. VIEW1 does not cover olfactory channels.

**Therapeutic garden design**: The design of gardens as therapeutic interventions (Grahn's specialty) is a landscape architecture topic that exceeds the scope of building-environment templates. VIEW1 addresses what is visible FROM a building, not how a garden should be designed.

**Urban vs. rural**: The distinction between urban nature (street trees, pocket parks, green roofs) and rural nature (forests, meadows, waterways) as view content is important but requires urban-ecology expertise beyond this panel's composition. VIEW-II Priority 2 would begin to address this through the view-quality parameterization.

**Individual differences**: Age, cultural background, nature-connection personality traits, and clinical conditions (attention disorders, PTSD, depression) all moderate nature-view effects. These are moderators within VIEW1's scope conditions but are not separately formalized, consistent with the project convention that individual differences are handled as moderators rather than separate templates.
