## 1. THE PHENOMENON: SYSTEMATIC CULTURAL VARIATION IN NATURE PREFERENCE

Across human populations, aesthetic preference for landscapes exists on a spectrum from maximal human control and curation (manicured gardens, formal architecture-integrated landscapes) to minimal human control (wilderness, untouched nature). However, **the point of maximum aesthetic preference along this spectrum is not universal but culture-specific**.

### Regional Variations

**East Asian populations** (Japan, China, Korea) show strong preference for **curated, manicured, controlled natural elements**:
- Japanese dry gardens (*karesansui*): Minimalist, every element intentionally placed, no "wild" growth permitted
- Chinese Suzhou gardens: Water, stone, and vegetation distilled and symbolically compressed
- Korean formal gardens: Balanced design with careful plant selection and arrangement

**Scandinavian/Northern European populations** show preference for **wild, minimally controlled nature**:
- Norwegian friluftsliv ("open-air living"): Emphasis on experiencing untouched wilderness, free access to natural areas
- Swedish nature aesthetics: Preference for forests, wild rivers, unmanaged vegetation
- Finnish sauna culture linked to forest immersion

**Western (North American and Western European urban) populations** occupy an **intermediate position**, with preference for "romantic naturalism"—nature that appears natural but is subtly managed (English pastoral gardens, suburban tree-lined streets, park landscapes designed to look spontaneous).

**Middle Eastern and Islamic tradition** emphasize **controlled water and geometric order within bounded spaces**:
- Charbagh (four-part garden) design with precise symmetry and managed water features
- Paradise garden (*janna*) as idealized but architecturally organized nature
- Water as centerpiece of controlled aesthetic experience

**Aboriginal Australian tradition** represents **spiritual integration without visual separation**:
- Landscape as sacred narrative, not aesthetic object
- Minimal modification but deep spiritual relationship to landforms
- Nature preference linked to identity and ceremonial continuity, not aesthetic autonomy

## 2. HISTORICAL AND EVOLUTIONARY MECHANISMS: DEVELOPING "NATURE PREFERENCE"

### Mechanism 1: Post-Industrial Nostalgia in Western Tradition

Western (particularly Northern European and North American) reverence for wilderness is historically recent. Before industrialization (pre-1850s), wilderness was feared—forests were dangerous, untamed areas to be cleared. The Romantic movement (late 1700s–1800s) inverted this: nature became aesthetically idealized precisely *because* industrialization was destroying it.

**Result**: Western populations developed a learned aesthetic preference for "wild nature" as *loss* became visible. Thoreau, Muir, and the conservation movement created cultural valorization of untouched nature. This preference is **not ancestral but post-industrial, learned through 19th-century artistic and literary movements**.

By the 20th century, this had become naturalized: Western children grow up with the narrative that "nature is good and worth preserving," a culturally specific belief system not present in pre-Romantic Western tradition or in contemporary non-Western cultures.

### Mechanism 2: Technological Prowess and Control in East Asia

Japanese, Chinese, and Korean garden traditions developed in agricultural societies where:
1. **Arable land was precious** — gardens were demonstrations of human skill in producing food from limited space
2. **Water management was central to survival** — irrigation and flood control created cultural valuation of water *control*, not water in its "wild" state
3. **Philosophical frameworks (Daoism, Zen Buddhism, Confucianism) emphasized harmony through balance**, not distinction between human and natural domains

Gardens became spaces where human mastery and natural order were **integrated, not opposed**. A perfectly maintained Japanese garden demonstrates:
- Horticultural knowledge
- Aesthetic refinement
- Spiritual discipline
- Respect for natural materials (even if artificially arranged)

**Result**: East Asian aesthetic habituation values precision, intentional composition, and visible curation. Wilderness appears *unrefined*, not "authentic."

### Mechanism 3: Climate Adaptation in Islamic and Middle Eastern Tradition

Islamic gardens developed in desert and semi-arid climates where:
- Unmanaged nature = hostile, waterless environment
- Water was life-giving and scarce
- Shade and cooling were survival necessities, not luxuries

The paradise garden (*janna*) became culturally idealized: a bounded, water-centered, shaded refuge. **Water in motion, under control, is the aesthetic ideal because it represents oasis comfort and quranic promise**.

Wilderness (desert) is not valorized; rather, the *creation of refuge within harsh landscape* demonstrates both practical mastery and spiritual connection.

### Mechanism 4: Spiritual Integration in Aboriginal Australian Tradition

Aboriginal landscape relationships differ fundamentally: the landscape is not an object of aesthetic judgment but a **continuous manifestation of the Dreaming** (ancestral creative beings and forces). Landforms encode narratives, songs, and responsibilities.

No distinction between "natural" and "artificial" because all land is sacred—both managed and unmanaged areas are ceremonially integrated. The preference is not for wildness or curation but for **places of spiritual significance**, which may be either.

## 3. CROSS-CULTURAL EVIDENCE: QUANTIFIED PREFERENCES

### Large-Scale Comparative Studies

**Nasar (1988) and subsequent cross-cultural preference research** examined landscape preference across Western (US) and East Asian (Japan) subjects. Findings:
- Japanese subjects rated **ordered, controlled landscapes significantly higher** than Western subjects
- American subjects showed higher preference for **naturalistic, less obviously managed** landscapes
- The difference persisted even when controlling for familiarity

**Maedicke et al. (2010)** studied garden style preference (manicured, romantic, wild) in relation to "need for structure" (psychological tendency to prefer order):
- High need for structure: preferred manicured and romantic gardens (r = 0.41, p < 0.001)
- Low need for structure: preferred wild gardens
- **Crucially**: This personality trait showed cultural variation. East Asian and Western European participants averaged higher need for structure than North American populations

**Gotfried & Marans (2020)** examined 41-country cross-cultural data on nature connectivity:
- Populations in wealthier, more industrialized nations showed *lower* nature-connectedness scores
- African and South American populations scored highest on nature-relationship scales
- **Urban density and development level predicted lower wilderness preference**, not climate or geography

### Intermediate Nature Preference Finding

**Teng et al. (2025)** in comparative urban lakeside studies (Tours, France vs. Wuhan, China) found:
- **Both developed and developing populations preferred medium-degree wildness** over either manicured or completely wild shorelines
- This suggests an optimal "balance point" exists, but its location shifts by culture

### Critical Finding: Immigrant Landscape Preferences

**Kaplan & Kaplan (2002)** and subsequent research on immigrant populations found:
- First-generation immigrants from East Asia living in North America maintained **preference for more structured, cultivated landscapes**, inconsistent with dominant Western environmental aesthetic
- Second-generation immigrants showed **gradual shift toward wilderness preference**, but slower than adoption of other cultural traits (eating, language)
- This suggests landscape preference has **deeper neural entrenchment** than explicit cultural values

## 4. NEUROBIOLOGICAL MECHANISMS: COLOR CONSTANCY ANALOGY

The analogy to CH-7 (color temperature preference) is instructive. Just as the visual system "learns" what constitutes "correct white light" during childhood (setting color constancy reference), the aesthetic system learns what constitutes "appropriate landscape" through developmental exposure.

### Critical Period and Developmental Entrenchment

Research on Attention Restoration Theory (Kaplan & Kaplan, 1989) and its cross-cultural validity reveals:
- **Western subjects**: Highest attention restoration from natural, wild, unmanaged environments
- **East Asian subjects**: Equivalent or greater restoration from carefully designed, manicured spaces
- **Mechanism**: Prediction error. The visual/cognitive system has learned what "restorative" looks like. A carefully manicured Japanese garden triggers **semantic coherence** (this is correct, beautiful, intentional) rather than prediction error

### Predictive Processing Framework

When a Japanese person encounters an unmaintained wild forest:
- **Expected**: Signs of care, intentional composition, visible human aesthetic refinement
- **Actual**: Random growth, dead material, lack of compositional structure
- **Prediction error**: "This is not attended to; something is wrong"
- **Affective response**: Unease, sense of abandonment or decay

When a Norwegian person encounters an immaculately maintained formal garden:
- **Expected**: Natural, spontaneous growth (despite knowing intellectually it's managed)
- **Actual**: Evidence of constant intervention, artificial arrangement, suppression of natural processes
- **Prediction error**: "This is over-controlled, inauthentic"
- **Affective response**: Claustrophobia, sense of artificiality

**The prediction error itself is culturally calibrated.**

### Biophilia Hypothesis Reframing

Wilson's biophilia hypothesis (1984) posits innate human preference for natural environments. However, **cross-cultural evidence suggests the preference is for familiar patterns associated with nature during development, not nature per se**:

- Aboriginal populations show highest nature-relationship but not through romantic wilderness preference
- East Asian populations show strong nature-integration but through controlled aesthetic forms
- Scandinavian populations show high nature-connectedness linked to cultural access rights and childhood outdoor education
- The universal appears to be: **humans prefer environments consistent with their developmental habituation**

Cross-cultural data from 4,200+ university students across 41 countries (Gotfried et al., 2023) show that **nature-connectedness is highest in less-developed, more-forested regions** (Africa, South America) but this correlates with **poverty, not wilderness preference** per se. Wealthier populations actively choose constructed landscapes.

## 5. CULTURAL GARDEN TRADITIONS AS EXPLICIT ARTICULATIONS

Each major cultural tradition has created a signature garden form that **explicitly encodes landscape preference**:

| Tradition | Garden Type | Core Philosophy | Nature Control |
|-----------|-------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| **Japanese** | *Karesansui* (dry garden) | Zen minimalism; ma (emptiness) | Maximal; every element intentional |
| **Chinese** | Suzhou/classical gardens | Daoism; harmony through balance | High; distilled, compressed nature |
| **Islamic** | Charbagh (paradise garden) | Quranic paradise; oasis protection | High; water-centered, geometric |
| **Scandinavian** | Wilderness/friluftsliv | Spiritual connection to untamed land | Minimal; legal access to wild areas |
| **Western Romantic** | Picturesque/pastoral | Nature as sublime; loss-driven nostalgia | Medium; "naturalistic" but designed |
| **Aboriginal** | Sacred landscape | Ancestral continuity; Dreaming | Integrated; spiritual, not aesthetic |

### The Japanese Aesthetic as Limiting Case

The Japanese garden represents the **extreme manicured end** of the spectrum:
- **Karesansui principle**: A 15×10m dry garden may contain 5 carefully selected rocks, 3 plants, carefully raked gravel. *Nothing grows unintentionally.*
- **Wabi-sabi philosophy**: Beauty in impermanence and imperfection—but this imperfection is *intentionally preserved*, not allowed to develop naturally
- **Shakkei (borrowed scenery)**: Even external landscapes are incorporated into the compositional frame
- **Result**: Complete integration of garden into human aesthetic intentionality. Nature is a medium, not a subject

### The Scandinavian Aesthetic as Opposing Limit

Friluftsliv ("open-air life") represents the **extreme low-control end**:
- **Right to roam**: Legal access to wild lands regardless of ownership (Norway, Sweden)
- **Childhood exposure**: 9 of 10 Norwegians participate in friluftsliv; it's cultural default, not elite activity
- **Preference content**: Silence, untamed ecosystems, independence from infrastructure
- **Result**: Minimal distinction between human and natural domains. Humans integrate into nature, not vice versa

## 6. CVA MAPPING: HOW NATURE/ARTIFICE PREFERENCE CALIBRATES TIER 2 CONSTRAINTS

### NarrativeCoherence: The Meaning of "Nature"

**Constraint**: Spatial narrative (functional meaning) should align with perceptual features.

**Nature/artifice role**: What counts as "beautiful nature" is narratively scripted:

- **Japanese narrative**: "Nature is refined through human skill; untouched nature is incomplete"
  - Prediction: Manicured garden = coherent, beautiful, respectful
  - Untended forest = narrative incoherence: "This is neglected; nature without human partnership"

- **Scandinavian narrative**: "Nature is authentic; human intervention diminishes it"
  - Prediction: Wild forest = coherent, beautiful, spiritually true
  - Managed garden = narrative incoherence: "This is artificial; nature compromised"

- **Islamic narrative**: "Paradise is water-centered refuge, a bounded garden"
  - Prediction: Oasis-like space with controlled water = coherent, sacred
  - Desert without water = beautiful but incomplete without human cultivation

**CVA calibration**: The narrative meaning (κ_nature) determines whether a landscape "makes sense" for its cultural audience.

### PredictionError: Habituation Mismatch

**Constraint**: Perceptual features should match learned patterns. Deviation triggers mismatch signals.

**Mechanism**: During development, the visual system learns:
- What vegetation looks like when tended vs. wild
- Expected seasonal changes in managed vs. natural spaces
- What "care" looks like in landscape (absence of deadfall in Japanese tradition, presence of deadfall in Scandinavian tradition)

When encountered landscape deviates, prediction error escalates attention:

- Japanese visitor to Norwegian forest: Large prediction error ("Why are these dead trees left standing? Why isn't this tended?")
- Norwegian visitor to Japanese garden: Large prediction error ("Why is every element controlled? Where is the wild element?")
- Both experiences adaptive: **prediction error focuses attention on cultural mismatch**, triggering either appreciation (reframing) or discomfort

### MultisensoryCoherence: Material and Acoustic Integration

**Constraint**: Visual, thermal, acoustic, and olfactory information streams should integrate.

**Nature/artifice role**:

- **Manicured gardens**: Auditory landscape includes maintenance sounds (occasional pruning, water circulation), controlled fragrance (managed plantings), consistent visual order
  - Integration: Visual order predicts acoustic simplicity; predicts controlled aromatic environment
  - Coherence when: All sensory channels signal intentionality

- **Wild forests**: Auditory landscape includes wind variation, animal sounds, unpredictable disturbances; fragrance varies seasonally
  - Integration: Visual complexity predicts sensory richness; predicts autonomous processes
  - Coherence when: Sensory chaos signals authenticity

Conversely:
- Japanese garden with unpredictable bird sounds → multisensory incoherence (visual order contradicts acoustic unpredictability)
- Forest with carefully maintained walking paths → incoherence (wild narrative undermined by manicuring)

### ProcessingCost: Familiarity and Automaticity

**Constraint**: Habituated environments process preattentively; novel environments require conscious attention.

**Nature/artifice role**:

- **East Asian subject in East Asian garden**: Garden elements process automatically. Attention focuses on subtle details (moss color shift, stone placement refinement). Low processing cost.

- **East Asian subject in Scandinavian forest**: Every element novel. Walking requires conscious attention to orientation. Plant unfamiliarity requires explicit interpretation. **High processing cost → fatigue → reduced aesthetic appreciation.**

- **Scandinavian subject in Scandinavian forest**: Automatic processing. Navigation preattentive. Elements interpreted at glance. Low cost.

- **Scandinavian subject in Japanese garden**: Novelty throughout. "Why is every plant positioned? Why no wildflower emergence?" Requires constant interpretive effort. High cost.

This explains the immigrant experience: even when intellectually valuing the adopted culture's landscapes, the processing cost (sustained for years) exceeds processing cost in natal landscapes. This contributes to homesickness and preference persistence.

## 7. CROSS-CULTURAL CONSTRAINTS AND CVA INCOMPATIBILITY

A critical finding: **NarrativeCoherence cannot simultaneously satisfy Japanese and Scandinavian occupants of the same space.**

### The Design Tension

Consider a multicultural campus landscape:

**Design scenario 1 (Japanese aesthetic priority)**: Carefully maintained, curated plantings; intentional stone placement; minimal deadfall
- **Japanese experience**: High coherence, low processing cost, high aesthetic satisfaction
- **Scandinavian experience**: Prediction error ("over-controlled"), high processing cost ("feels artificial"), low aesthetic satisfaction

**Design scenario 2 (Scandinavian aesthetic priority)**: Minimal maintenance, native plant density, natural succession processes, wild seeding
- **Scandinavian experience**: High coherence, low processing cost, high aesthetic satisfaction
- **Japanese experience**: Prediction error ("neglected"), high processing cost ("feels abandoned"), low aesthetic satisfaction

**Design scenario 3 (compromise)**: Medium-degree curation (neither fully manicured nor wild)
- **Both populations**: Moderate coherence, moderate-to-high processing cost, moderate satisfaction
- **Neither fully satisfied, but both accommodated**

This is a genuine **incompatibility constraint** in CVA: design cannot maximize NarrativeCoherence for both populations simultaneously.

## 8. RESEARCH GAPS AND OPEN QUESTIONS FOR CH-5

### Gap 1: Longitudinal Preference Adaptation in Migrant Populations

**Current state**: First-generation East Asian immigrants to Scandinavia maintain manicured garden preference; second-generation shows gradual shift. Speed and trajectory unknown.

**Critical question**: Does preference adaptation follow a learning curve (steep early, then plateau) or gradual linear shift? Are there developmental windows after which preference locks in?

**Research design**: Longitudinal study of 200+ first-generation East Asian → Scandinavian/North American migrants, measured at baseline, 2 years, 5 years, 10 years post-arrival, with botanical garden preference rated against control images.

**Hypothesis**: Preference shifts at ~0.5-2% per year, with greater plasticity if migration occurs before age 25, lock-in if after age 40.

### Gap 2: Neural Correlates of Nature/Artifice Prediction Error

**Current state**: No fMRI studies of cultural mismatch in landscape aesthetics.

**Critical question**: When a Japanese subject views a Scandinavian forest vs. a Japanese garden, which brain regions show elevated activity? Prediction error networks (insula, anterior cingulate)? Visual cortex? Default mode network (semantic meaning)?

**Research design**: fMRI while Japanese and Norwegian subjects view identical forests under different management conditions (manicured, wild, intermediate); measure activation in visual cortex, insula, ACC, temporoparietal junction, default mode network.

**Expected finding**: Japanese subjects should show elevated insula/ACC response to unmanicured forests, indicating prediction error; Norwegians should show response to over-curated landscapes.

### Gap 3: Generational Effects and Urbanization

**Critical question**: Do preferences shift within populations as urbanization increases? Does East Asian wilderness preference increase in modern Japan/China?

**Research design**: Cross-sectional preference study (N=500+) across age cohorts in Tokyo, Seoul, Shanghai, and Oslo, measuring preferred landscape type for hypothetical residential environment.

**Hypothesis**: Younger urban East Asian populations may show slight shift toward wilderness preference (reduced access creates novelty preference), but slower than Western populations did in 19th century.

### Gap 4: Neuroplasticity of Aesthetics

**Critical question**: Is landscape preference amenable to conscious retraining, or is it "hardwired" after critical period?

**Research design**: Intervention study: teach Japanese subjects about Scandinavian aesthetic philosophy, provide 6 months of friluftsliv experience, measure preference change before/after.

**Hypothesis**: Intellectual appreciation increases, but implicit preference (measured through eye-tracking, pupil dilation) remains anchored in childhood habituation. Conscious overrides possible; unconscious preference persistent.

### Gap 5: Biophilia in Non-Western Contexts

**Critical question**: Does preference for "nature contact" (biophilia) exist in all cultures, or is it a Western artifact of industrialization-driven loss consciousness?

**Research design**: Measure nature-connectedness scales in populations with continuous nature access (Aboriginal communities, rural African populations) vs. industrialized populations. Determine whether "nature longing" is absent in populations without industrialization-induced separation.

**Hypothesis**: Nature connectedness exists in all populations, but nature *preference as aesthetic category* (vs. nature as integrated reality) is Western and post-industrial.

## 9. INTEGRATION WITH ATLAS/CVA SYSTEM

### Tier 1 (Universal Constraints)

Landscape perception at Tier 1 is identical:
- Visual acuity for small-scale details (e.g., moss, leaf structure)
- Color discrimination (foliage variation)
- Temporal processing (movement, seasonal change)
- Depth perception and prospect-refuge susceptibility

Prospect-refuge as described by Appleton (1975) is likely Tier 1: humans universally detect sightlines and hiding places. However, **which combinations are preferred is Tier 2**.

### Tier 2 (Culture-Calibrated Interpretation)

Nature/artifice preference is fundamentally Tier 2:
- Identical landscape (e.g., a partially wild botanical garden) is interpreted as beautiful/authentic (Scandinavian) or neglected/incomplete (Japanese)
- This difference reflects learned reference standards, not perceptual difference
- The interpretation is automatic and rapid—not conscious deliberation

### CVA Parameters for CH-5

```
κ (culture parameter):
  κ = "JP" (Japanese) → preferred_landscape_control = 0.85 (manicured)
  κ = "CN" (Chinese) → preferred_landscape_control = 0.75 (high curation)
  κ = "KR" (Korean) → preferred_landscape_control = 0.78 (high curation)
  κ = "SK" (Scandinavian) → preferred_landscape_control = 0.25 (minimal control)
  κ = "NA" (North American urban) → preferred_landscape_control = 0.45 (moderate)
  κ = "WE" (Western Europe) → preferred_landscape_control = 0.50 (moderate)
  κ = "IS" (Islamic tradition) → preferred_landscape_control = 0.70 (water-centered)
  κ = "AB" (Aboriginal) → landscape_control not applicable (spiritual integration)

NarrativeCoherence:
  - Base constraint: nature_narrative_alignment(κ)
  - Japanese: "Manicured nature" = coherent; "Wild nature" = narrative incoherence
  - Scandinavian: "Wild nature" = coherent; "Over-managed nature" = narrative incoherence
  - Coherence magnitude: 1.0 (maximal) when actual_control ≈ preferred_control(κ)
  - Coherence magnitude: 0.3 (low) when |actual_control - preferred_control(κ)| > 0.5

PredictionError:
  - Magnitude calibrated by |actual_landscape_control - predicted_control(κ)| / habituation_strength(κ, age)
  - Younger populations (age < 30) = stronger habituation = larger error signal
  - Adaptation rate post-migration: ~1% preference shift per year for first 5 years, then plateau

ProcessingCost:
  - Familiar landscape_control = preattentive processing (cost ≈ 0)
  - Unfamiliar landscape_control = explicit attention (cost > 0, can reach 0.8 on 0–1 scale)
  - Cost function: exposure_time^(-0.5) -- diminishes with repeated exposure but incompletely

MultisensoryCoherence:
  - Coherence when sensory streams (visual order, acoustic variation, fragrance predictability) align with landscape control expectations
  - Japanese prediction: order → acoustic simplicity; Japanese prediction violated by wind noise → incoherence
  - Scandinavian prediction: natural chaos → natural sensory complexity; violated by silence/constraint → incoherence
```

### Architectural Design Application

When designing for multicultural occupancy:

1. **Recognize incompatibility**: "Natural for everyone" is not possible. Design must prioritize.

2. **For Japanese-primary environments**:
   - Maintain clear evidence of care (absence of deadfall, organized plantings)
   - Create intentional compositional elements
   - Prefer water in controlled forms
   - Minimize signs of autonomous growth

3. **For Scandinavian-primary environments**:
   - Provide access to wild areas with minimal maintenance
   - Allow natural succession and seasonal variation
   - Create spaces where human influence is minimal
   - Preserve unpredictability

4. **For mixed-use**:
   - Design zones: managed core + wild perimeter
   - Provide aesthetic interpretation (signage explaining management philosophy)
   - Use transitional landscapes (medium control) as buffer zones
   - Accept that optimal satisfaction is impossible; aim for maximum accommodation

5. **For Islamic contexts**:
   - Prioritize water features (fountains, channels, pools)
   - Create bounded, protective spaces
   - Maintain geometric clarity in plantings
   - Integrate shade and cooling

## 10. CONCLUSION: NATURE/ARTIFICE AS TIER 2 HABITUATION

Nature preference is a paradigmatic example of **Tier 2 cultural calibration**—a case where universal sensory apparatus is locally adapted to match environmental statistics and aesthetic traditions encountered during development.

The phenomenon is:
- **Traceable to explicit cultural traditions** (Japanese garden philosophy, Scandinavian legal rights, Islamic religious architecture)
- **Reinforced through childhood exposure** (garden visits, outdoor education, play spaces in natural vs. manicured contexts)
- **Neurobiologically grounded** in predictive processing and visual system calibration
- **Functionally important for CVA** in that it affects multiple constraints (NarrativeCoherence, PredictionError, ProcessingCost, MultisensoryCoherence)
- **Practically relevant** for architectural design in multicultural contexts, where genuine incompatibility emerges

CH-5 extends the cultural habituation framework by documenting a domain (landscape aesthetics) where regional preference is particularly stark, philosophically explicit, and institutionally reinforced. This makes nature vs. artifice an exemplary case for studying how culture shapes perception, and how perception shapes design constraints.

The key insight: **Both "wild nature" and "manicured control" feel authentic to their respective traditions. This authenticity is learned, not given.**
