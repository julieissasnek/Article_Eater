# PANEL L-I: LIGHT & LUMINANCE IN ARCHITECTURAL EXPERIENCE
## Mechanistic Templates for How Light Affects Cognition, Emotion, and Health
## February 16, 2026 — Document 34

---

## Panel Charge

Light is arguably the most pervasive and least formalized variable in the Article Eater project. Document 33 (Dual-Index Cross-Reference Layer) identified Light & Luminance (attribute domain A4) as the project's single largest gap: coverage rating ★ (Minimal), with only four templates bearing weak-to-moderate relevance to light phenomena and no dedicated light templates whatsoever. This despite the fact that light affects every occupant in every building at every moment, that its neural pathways are among the best-characterized in sensory neuroscience, and that it integrates naturally with the prediction error (PE) framework that unifies the project's theoretical architecture.

This panel convenes fifteen experts spanning circadian neuroscience, visual psychophysics, lighting design, daylight engineering, color science, neuroaesthetics, and the phenomenology of architectural light. The charge is to produce formalized mechanistic templates — structured causal pathway accounts — for how light properties in buildings affect human perception, cognition, emotion, and health. Each template must specify its causal links across levels (environmental → sensory → neural → cognitive/affective → behavioral), its scope conditions, moderators, maturity, and key references.

The panel must address at minimum:
1. **Luminance contrast as visual prediction error** — how the brain processes light-dark distributions and why contrast patterns produce aesthetic and emotional responses
2. **Circadian architecture** — how building design mediates the ipRGC → SCN → melatonin/cortisol pathway and its consequences for health and cognition
3. **Daylight, windows, and cognitive performance** — the mechanisms linking natural light access to attention, mood, and productivity
4. **Light color temperature and spectral composition** — how correlated color temperature (CCT) affects alertness, mood, and temporal prediction
5. **Dynamic light and temporal prediction** — how light changes over time (diurnal cycle, weather, movement through a building) generate and resolve temporal PE
6. **Sacred and phenomenological light** — the mechanisms by which dramatic light (a shaft from an oculus, stained glass in a dark nave, a Turrell Skyspace) produces awe, transcendence, and heightened aesthetic experience

## Position Statements

### CIRCADIAN ARCHITECTURE: THE NON-VISUAL LIGHT PATHWAY

**Position Statement 1: Russell Foster — The Third Photoreceptor and Its Architectural Consequences**

The discovery that a subset of retinal ganglion cells — the intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells, or ipRGCs — express the photopigment melanopsin and project directly to the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) via the retinohypothalamic tract has fundamentally altered our understanding of how light affects human biology (Berson, Dunn, & Takao, 2002, ~3,500 citations; Hattar, Liao, Takao, Berson, & Yau, 2002, ~2,800 citations). These cells do not contribute to pattern vision. They measure ambient light irradiance and spectral composition, with peak sensitivity at approximately 480nm (blue), and transmit this information to the brain's master clock.

The SCN orchestrates circadian rhythms in virtually every tissue and organ system. Its outputs regulate the pineal gland (melatonin synthesis and secretion, peaking at night in darkness), the adrenal gland (cortisol rhythm, peaking shortly after waking), core body temperature, metabolic rate, immune function, and cognitive performance. When the light signal to the SCN is appropriate — high-irradiance, blue-enriched light during the biological day; dim, warm light during the biological evening — the circadian system entrains to a stable ~24-hour cycle and downstream rhythms are aligned. When the light signal is inappropriate — insufficient daytime light, excessive evening light, or irregular timing — the system drifts, internal rhythms desynchronize, and the consequences span sleep disruption, mood deterioration, cognitive impairment, metabolic dysfunction, and elevated long-term disease risk (Foster & Kreitzman, 2017, ~200 citations).

The architectural implication is radical: **buildings are circadian instruments**. Every design decision that affects light — window size and orientation, glazing spectral transmittance, shading strategy, artificial lighting spectrum and intensity, lighting control scheduling — modulates the ipRGC → SCN pathway. A building that provides abundant daylight during working hours and dims to warm, low-intensity light in the evening is, in circadian terms, a well-tuned instrument. A sealed, deep-plan office with fixed-CCT fluorescent lighting at 300 lux from 8am to midnight is a circadian disruptor — it provides too little blue-enriched light during the day to properly suppress melatonin and too much in the evening to permit melatonin onset.

For the PE framework: circadian disruption IS a form of prediction error, but at the temporal and physiological level rather than the perceptual level. The SCN generates predictions about the time of day based on accumulated light history. When the light environment contradicts these predictions — blue-enriched light at a time the SCN has registered as "night" — the result is a temporal PE that propagates through the allostatic system. The body's temperature rhythm says "night" but the light signal says "day." This internal conflict is the mechanistic basis of jet lag, shift-work malaise, and the chronic low-grade circadian disruption that characterizes most modern indoor environments.

**Position Statement 2: George Brainard — The Action Spectrum and the Dose-Response Problem**

The circadian system does not respond to "light" in the way the visual system does. The action spectrum for melatonin suppression — the function describing which wavelengths are most effective at inhibiting melatonin production — peaks at approximately 460–480nm, well into the short-wavelength (blue) range, and does not match any known visual photopigment (Brainard et al., 2001, ~2,200 citations; Thapan, Arendt, & Skene, 2001, ~1,800 citations). This mismatch between the visual and circadian systems means that a room can appear brightly lit to the visual system (high photopic illuminance) while being circadianly dim (low melanopic irradiance), or vice versa.

The dose-response relationship is nonlinear and depends on prior photic history, timing relative to the circadian phase, duration of exposure, and the spatial distribution of light on the retina (ipRGCs are concentrated in the inferior retina, meaning light from above — such as overhead daylight — is more circadianly effective than light from below). The practical consequence is that illuminance alone — the standard metric for lighting design — is a poor predictor of circadian impact. A 500-lux office lit by warm-white (2700K) LEDs delivers substantially less circadian stimulus than a 500-lux office lit by cool-white (6500K) LEDs, and both deliver less than 500 lux of natural daylight, which has a broader and more blue-enriched spectrum.

For the template framework, this means that the circadian pathway cannot be modeled as a simple linear chain from "light intensity" to "health outcome." The environmental variables that matter are spectral power distribution (especially melanopic content), corneal irradiance (not just horizontal illuminance), timing relative to circadian phase, duration of exposure, and the spatial pattern of light arrival on the retina. All of these are determined by architectural design decisions, but none of them appear on a standard lighting schedule.

**Position Statement 3: Satchin Panda — Light Timing, Gene Expression, and the Metabolic Cascade**

The circadian clock is not merely a timekeeper; it is a master regulator of gene expression. In humans, approximately 10–15% of all genes are under circadian control (Zhang et al., 2014, ~2,000 citations). These clock-controlled genes regulate metabolic enzymes, immune signaling molecules, DNA repair machinery, cell-cycle proteins, and neurotransmitter synthesis pathways. The light signal to the SCN — mediated by ipRGCs and the retinohypothalamic tract — is the primary synchronizer of this transcriptional architecture.

When light exposure is temporally inappropriate, the consequences extend far beyond sleep disruption. Aberrant light schedules in animal models produce metabolic syndrome, impaired glucose tolerance, weight gain, and accelerated tumor growth (Fonken et al., 2010, ~600 citations). In human epidemiological studies, chronic shift work — the most extreme form of circadian light disruption — is associated with elevated rates of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, obesity, depression, and certain cancers (Knutsson, 2003, ~1,200 citations). The International Agency for Research on Cancer classified shift work involving circadian disruption as a probable human carcinogen (Group 2A) in 2007.

The architectural relevance is this: buildings that fail to provide appropriate light timing are not merely uncomfortable or suboptimal — they impose a measurable biological cost. This cost accumulates over years of occupancy and affects health outcomes far downstream of the immediate experience of the space. The allostatic framework already in the template library (T29, ALLOSTATIC_MASTER_001) provides the right theoretical scaffold: chronic circadian disruption increases allostatic load through sustained misalignment between the body's internal temporal predictions and the environmental light signal.

For the PE framework specifically: the circadian system maintains a temporal prediction model of the 24-hour cycle. Light is the primary signal that calibrates this model. When light timing is consistent and appropriate, prediction errors are small, the allostatic system operates efficiently, and downstream gene expression follows its optimal temporal profile. When light timing is erratic or phase-shifted, the prediction errors accumulate, the allostatic system expends energy on re-entrainment, and the downstream biological costs manifest as the metabolic and health effects described above.

### DAYLIGHT, COGNITION, AND WELLBEING

**Position Statement 4: Mariana Figueiro — Circadian Stimulus as Architectural Metric**

The gap between circadian neuroscience and architectural practice is a measurement gap. Architects and lighting designers work with illuminance (lux), correlated color temperature (CCT), and the Color Rendering Index (CRI). None of these metrics captures the circadian effectiveness of a light environment. The Circadian Stimulus (CS) metric was developed to bridge this gap (Rea, Figueiro, Bullough, & Bierman, 2005, ~500 citations; Rea & Figueiro, 2018, ~300 citations). CS ranges from 0 to 0.7 and represents the effectiveness of a given light condition at activating the circadian system, computed from spectral irradiance at the cornea, weighted by the spectral sensitivity of ipRGCs and the post-receptoral opponent process that modulates the circadian signal.

Field studies using CS have demonstrated that architectural light interventions can be quantified in circadian terms. In Alzheimer's care facilities, increasing morning CS from ~0.1 to ~0.3 through daylight access and tailored electric lighting improved sleep efficiency, reduced nocturnal activity, and decreased agitation and depression symptoms (Figueiro et al., 2014, ~200 citations). In office environments, workers with CS > 0.3 during the first half of the day reported better sleep quality, lower depression scores, and greater vitality than those with CS < 0.15 (Figueiro & Rea, 2016, ~150 citations).

For the template framework, CS provides what has been missing: a quantitative environmental variable that sits at the top of the circadian causal chain and is directly determined by architectural design. The template can specify: architectural variables (window design, orientation, glazing, electric lighting spectrum and intensity, timing control) → CS at the occupant's cornea → ipRGC activation → SCN entrainment → downstream circadian alignment → health and cognitive outcomes. Each link has measurable parameters and known dose-response relationships.

**Position Statement 5: Yvonne de Kort — Light, Affect, and Arousal Regulation**

The effects of light on psychological state are not limited to circadian pathways. Even within acute exposure durations too short for circadian mechanisms (minutes rather than hours), light parameters modulate alertness, vitality, hedonic tone, and cognitive performance (Kort & Smolders, 2010, ~300 citations; Smolders, de Kort, & Cluitmans, 2012, ~200 citations). Bright light (~1000 lux at the eye) increases subjective alertness and vitality within 30 minutes, likely through direct ipRGC projections not only to the SCN but also to brainstem arousal centers (locus coeruleus, dorsal raphe) and to limbic structures including the amygdala (Vandewalle et al., 2009, ~400 citations).

These acute effects suggest that the non-visual light pathway has both a circadian function (SCN entrainment, slow, cumulative) and an acute alerting function (brainstem arousal, fast, immediate). The architectural implication is that light level and spectrum affect cognitive state in real time, not just over 24-hour cycles. A meeting room with 200 lux at the eye is not just circadianly suboptimal — it is acutely suboptimal for alertness. Conversely, bright blue-enriched light in the evening is both acutely alerting (potentially useful for shift workers) and circadianly disruptive (harmful for next-night sleep).

For the PE framework: acute light effects can be modeled as direct arousal modulation rather than as prediction error per se. The ipRGC → brainstem arousal pathway functions as a gain-control mechanism — it sets the baseline activation level of the cortical prediction hierarchy. Higher ambient light → higher cortical arousal → tighter predictions → more resources available for prediction error processing. Lower ambient light → lower arousal → looser predictions → broader associative processing (which may explain the Steidle creativity finding). This is not PE but a modulator of PE processing capacity.

**Position Statement 6: Anna Steidle — Dim Light, Creativity, and Processing Style**

When ambient illumination is dim (~150 lux versus ~1,500 lux), individuals show enhanced performance on creative insight tasks, broader conceptual scope, and greater willingness to explore novel solutions (Steidle & Werth, 2013, ~300 citations; Steidle, Werth, & Hanke, 2011, ~100 citations). The proposed mechanism is that dim light reduces perceptual constraints and promotes a more abstract, relational processing style — individuals feel less observed, less evaluated, and more free to engage in unconstrained thought.

This parallels the Meyers-Levy and Zhu (2007, ~1,200 citations) finding that high ceilings promote relational processing and low ceilings promote item-specific processing. In both cases, the environmental variable (illumination level, ceiling height) modulates processing style along the same abstract-vs-concrete dimension. The PE interpretation would be: dim light reduces the precision of visual predictions (less reliable sensory input → wider prediction intervals → more permissive error tolerance → more exploratory processing). Bright light sharpens predictions (more reliable input → narrower intervals → stricter error tolerance → more confirmatory processing).

This is consistent with the predictive processing literature on precision weighting (Feldman & Friston, 2010, ~800 citations): the brain adjusts the gain (precision) on prediction errors based on the reliability of the sensory input. Under dim conditions, visual PE precision is downweighted, freeing attentional resources for internally generated associations. Under bright conditions, visual PE precision is upweighted, biasing cognition toward stimulus-driven processing.

**Position Statement 7: Lisa Heschong — Daylight and the Full-Spectrum Human Response**

The "Daylighting in Schools" study (Heschong, 1999/2002; Heschong, Wright, & Okura, 2002, ~800 citations) found that students in classrooms with the most daylight progressed 20% faster in math and 26% faster in reading than those in the least daylit classrooms, controlling for teacher, socioeconomic status, and other confounds across 21,000 students in three school districts. This remains one of the largest and most robust findings in the architecture-and-performance literature.

But the mechanism is almost certainly not unitary. Daylight provides simultaneously: (a) high circadian stimulus (bright, blue-enriched, from above), (b) acute alerting through ipRGC → brainstem pathways, (c) a view to the outdoors (visual access, attention restoration, stress reduction), (d) dynamic variation over the day (clouds, sun angle, weather — temporal interest rather than monotony), (e) accurate color rendering (broad spectrum → veridical surface colors → reduced visual processing effort), and (f) connection to the diurnal rhythm (implicit temporal orientation — knowing what time it is by the quality of light). No single mechanism accounts for a 20% performance advantage. It is the convergence of all six that produces the large effect.

This multi-mechanism convergence is precisely what the project's AX6 (Multi-Modal PE Integration) template describes for aural architecture. For light, the analogous principle is: **daylight is not a single signal but a multi-channel stimulus that simultaneously confirms multiple predictions** — circadian temporal prediction, spatial-depth prediction (daylight reveals three-dimensional form better than flat artificial lighting), color prediction (natural surfaces under natural light match their expected chromaticity), temporal prediction (light quality matches time of day), and environmental prediction (outdoor view confirms the building's location in weather, season, and geography). Artificial lighting that matches daylight on one channel (illuminance) while failing on others (spectrum, directionality, variability, view) does not produce equivalent effects because it achieves only partial prediction confirmation.

### VISUAL NEUROSCIENCE AND LUMINANCE PREDICTION

**Position Statement 8: Lars Muckli — Predictive Processing in Early Visual Cortex and Luminance**

The visual cortex does not passively receive luminance information. From the earliest cortical processing stages (V1), neurons receive both feedforward input from the retina/LGN and massive feedback predictions from higher cortical areas — V2, V4, inferotemporal cortex, and prefrontal regions (Muckli & Petro, 2013, ~300 citations; Muckli et al., 2015, ~200 citations). These feedback signals carry predictions about what the visual input SHOULD be, given the current context. The feedforward signal that actually arrives is compared against these predictions, and the residual — the prediction error — is what propagates upward for further processing.

For luminance in architectural settings, this means the brain actively predicts the light distribution it expects to encounter. These predictions are based on: (a) prior experience with similar spaces (a cathedral is expected to have high luminance contrast with directional light; a modern office is expected to have uniform diffuse illumination), (b) the current spatial context (entering a deep room from a bright corridor generates a prediction of decreasing luminance), (c) material expectations (a white wall is predicted to have higher luminance than a dark wood panel under the same illumination), and (d) temporal context (afternoon light is predicted to be warmer and more directional than midday light).

When the actual luminance distribution matches predictions, processing is efficient, and the subjective experience is one of visual coherence — things look "right." When predictions are violated, PE signals propagate, and the subjective experience depends on the magnitude and valence of the violation. Moderate violations — a surprising pool of light on a dark floor, a luminance gradient that reveals spatial depth in an unexpected way — produce aesthetic interest (Goldilocks zone). Large violations — a sudden blinding shaft of light, a completely dark room where light was expected — produce strong emotional responses ranging from awe to disorientation to fear.

**Position Statement 9: Bevil Conway — Color Processing and Chromatic Prediction Under Architectural Illumination**

Color is not a property of surfaces; it is a property of the interaction between surface reflectance, illumination spectrum, and neural computation (Conway, 2009, ~400 citations; Conway et al., 2010, ~300 citations). The same architectural surface changes chromaticity dramatically under different illuminants — a warm wood panel appears golden under incandescent light, greenish under fluorescent, and blue-grey under overcast daylight. The visual system performs color constancy — the computation that extracts stable surface identity despite varying illumination — but this computation has limits, and failures of color constancy produce perceptual discomfort and processing inefficiency.

The architectural relevance is that lighting design simultaneously determines three things: how bright surfaces appear (luminance), what color surfaces appear (chromaticity), and how accurately the brain can extract stable surface identity (color constancy demand). LED lighting with narrow spectral peaks can produce metameric failures — two surfaces that match under one illuminant but differ under another — creating unpredictable color shifts that violate surface-identity predictions.

For the PE framework: color constancy IS a prediction process. The brain predicts what a surface should look like based on its stored representation of the illuminant's spectral properties (estimated from the spatial average and spectral composition of the ambient light). When a surface appears as predicted, the chromatic PE is small and processing is fluent. When the illuminant distorts surface color beyond the constancy mechanism's ability to correct — as frequently occurs with narrow-band LED sources or colored architectural lighting — the result is chromatic PE: the surface does not look as expected. This can range from subtle unease ("something looks off but I can't say what") to gross distortion (colored theatrical lighting that transforms surface identity entirely — used deliberately in stage design and James Turrell's installations for emotional and perceptual effect).

**Position Statement 10: Anya Hurlbert — Daylight Color Rendering and the Ecological Prediction**

Natural daylight has evolved to be the reference illuminant for human color vision. Our color constancy mechanisms are optimized for the spectral regularities of daylight — its smooth, broadband spectral power distribution and its systematic variation from warm (sunrise/sunset, ~2000K) to cool (overcast sky, ~6500K) across the diurnal cycle (Hurlbert, 2007, ~100 citations). Under natural daylight, color constancy is at its most accurate, surface colors are richly differentiated, and the visual system operates with minimal chromatic PE.

Artificial light departs from this reference in ways that impose a predictive processing cost. Fluorescent and LED sources have spiky spectral power distributions (narrow emission peaks with gaps between them) that challenge color constancy, reduce the discriminability of surface colors, and can produce systematic color distortions in specific hue ranges. The CRI metric was designed to quantify this departure but has well-known limitations — a high-CRI light can still distort specific colors, and the metric does not capture the subjective quality of color rendering.

The ecological prediction perspective connects to the Palmer and Schloss (2010, ~800 citations) ecological valence theory of color preference: people prefer colors associated with objects they tend to like (blues of clear sky and clean water, greens of healthy vegetation, warm oranges of sunset and firelight) and dislike colors associated with negative objects (browns of rot, yellows of contamination). Architectural light that preserves these ecological color associations — warm wood looks warm, green foliage looks healthy, skin tones look natural — confirms ecological color predictions. Light that distorts these associations (green-tinged fluorescents that make skin look sallow, narrow-band LEDs that make food look unappetizing) violates ecological color predictions and produces aversion.

### LIGHTING DESIGN AND ARCHITECTURAL PRACTICE

**Position Statement 11: Christoph Reinhart — Daylight Metrics and the Building-as-Light-Modulator**

A building is a daylight modulation device. It intercepts the exterior luminous environment — sky luminance distribution, sun position, ground reflectance — and transforms it through architectural geometry (window placement, room depth, ceiling height, light shelves, atriums, light wells) and material properties (glazing transmittance and spectrum, interior surface reflectance, furnishing) into the interior luminous environment that the occupant actually experiences (Reinhart, Mardaljevic, & Rogers, 2006, ~800 citations; Reinhart & Wienold, 2011, ~600 citations).

Climate-based daylight modeling (CBDM) can now predict, hour by hour, the daylight conditions in a proposed building based on local climate data and architectural geometry. Metrics like Spatial Daylight Autonomy (sDA — the percentage of floor area receiving at least 300 lux from daylight for at least 50% of occupied hours) and Annual Sunlight Exposure (ASE — the percentage receiving more than 1000 lux of direct sun for more than 250 hours/year, indicating potential glare) provide quantitative tools for evaluating how well a building serves its occupants' visual and circadian needs.

For the template framework, these metrics supply the quantitative architectural variables at the top of the causal chain. sDA quantifies daylight availability (relevant to circadian stimulus, alertness, and cognitive performance). ASE quantifies glare risk (relevant to visual discomfort and luminance PE). The distribution of daylight across the floor plate — gradient from window to core — determines which occupants receive adequate circadian input and which do not, creating a spatial inequality in circadian health that is architecturally determined.

**Position Statement 12: Deborah Burnett — Integrative Human-Centric Lighting Design**

Lighting design practice has historically optimized for three criteria: visual task performance (can you see what you need to see?), energy efficiency (how many watts per lux?), and aesthetics (does it look pleasant?). Human-centric lighting adds a fourth: biological appropriateness (does the light support circadian, cognitive, and emotional health?). This requires designing with spectrum, intensity, timing, spatial distribution, and dynamics — not just illuminance levels.

The practical challenge is that these criteria often conflict. A circadianly optimal morning office (bright, blue-enriched, ~6500K, > 500 lux at the eye) may feel harsh and clinical. A warm, dim, evening-appropriate light (~2700K, < 100 lux) may be circadianly ideal for an evening residential environment but provides insufficient task illuminance for reading. A dramatically contrasting light environment that is aesthetically powerful may cause adaptation problems and visual discomfort. The designer must navigate these tradeoffs knowing that different biological systems respond to different aspects of the light environment and that no single setting satisfies all needs simultaneously.

**Position Statement 13: Rogier Holthuijsen — Dynamic Light and the Building Over Time**

The most critical missing dimension in lighting research is time. Most studies compare fixed light conditions: 300 lux vs. 500 lux, 2700K vs. 6500K. But real architectural light changes continuously — daylight shifts with weather, time, and season; occupants move through zones of different light character; even electric lighting can be programmed for dynamic change. The experience of architectural light is fundamentally temporal: the shaft of morning sun that traverses a room over the course of hours, the seasonal variation in a window's light contribution, the moment of stepping from a dim corridor into a light-filled atrium.

Dynamic light connects to the PE framework more naturally than static light. A static light condition generates predictions that are quickly confirmed and habituated — the luminance distribution becomes the baseline expectation, and PE diminishes over minutes. A dynamic light environment — one that changes gradually (natural daylight) or in response to events (clouds, movement, dimming controls) — continuously generates new predictions and moderate PE. This is the luminous equivalent of the Goldilocks zone: enough variation to sustain interest and engagement, not so much as to be disorienting or fatiguing.

The best natural light in buildings is dynamic by nature — it changes throughout the day, casting different shadows, illuminating different surfaces, creating an ever-shifting composition that maintains the occupant's awareness of time, weather, and season. This dynamism is aesthetically valued precisely because it sustains prediction-error-driven engagement. Fixed artificial lighting, by eliminating all temporal variation, eliminates all temporal PE and produces the flat, deadening experience that occupants instinctively dislike in windowless spaces.

### PHENOMENOLOGY AND AESTHETICS OF ARCHITECTURAL LIGHT

**Position Statement 14: Henry Plummer — A Taxonomy of Architectural Light Experiences**

The architectural experience of light is not a single phenomenon but a family of qualitatively distinct experiences, each with its own spatial structure, temporal behavior, and emotional character. Over three decades of documentation across cultures and centuries, the following taxonomy of natural light types in architecture has emerged (Plummer, 2009, ~200 citations; Plummer, 2012, ~100 citations):

The **shaft**: a beam of light entering through an aperture into a darker space — the oculus of the Pantheon, a clerestory in a Cistercian monastery, a slit window in a Tadao Ando chapel. The shaft makes light visible as a substance, reveals dust and atmosphere, marks time through its movement, and creates a zone of heightened luminance that draws the eye and the body. Its emotional register ranges from the revelatory (sacred light) to the contemplative (a reading niche) to the dramatic (theatrical spotlight effect).

The **wash**: an even, gentle illumination of a surface by indirect or reflected light — north-facing windows in a gallery, a light shelf bouncing daylight onto a ceiling, Le Corbusier's light cannons at Ronchamp diffusing through deep embrasures. The wash reveals surface texture and material quality without glare, creates visual calm, and supports sustained attention.

The **glow**: luminosity that appears to emanate from within a surface or volume rather than striking it from outside — alabaster screens in Islamic architecture, translucent marble at the Beinecke Library, backlit onyx in a bar, the sky seen through a Turrell aperture. The glow dematerializes the boundary between light source and illuminated surface, creating a sense of radiant substance. Its emotional register is typically warmth, intimacy, and wonder.

The **dapple**: a complex, semi-random pattern of light and shadow created by multiple partial occlusions — sunlight through leaves, light through latticework, the moiré patterns of overlapping screens. The dapple is the luminous equivalent of a fractal pattern: self-similar at multiple scales, moderately complex, never exactly repeating. It connects directly to the project's T1 (spectral match / fractal fluency) template — dappled light has 1/f spectral characteristics in its spatial luminance distribution, potentially engaging the same processing fluency mechanisms as fractal visual patterns.

The **aureole**: a halo of light surrounding a brighter source or silhouetting a form — backlighting through a doorway creating a luminous threshold, the corona of light around a figure standing before a window, the scalloped light patterns thrown by a Moorish lamp. The aureole creates figure-ground separation through luminance contrast and often carries symbolic resonance (sanctity, revelation, the numinous).

The **chiaroscuro**: the dramatic interplay of light and deep shadow within a single composition — Baroque churches, Tadao Ando's concrete chapels, Louis Kahn's Salk Institute. Chiaroscuro maximizes luminance contrast within the visual field, creating strong spatial depth cues and intense emotional atmosphere. Its PE signature is high: the luminance prediction must constantly update as the eye moves between bright and dark zones, and the contrast between them exceeds the range that adaptation can fully accommodate.

Each of these light types is a distinct stimulus configuration with a characteristic PE profile. The shaft is a spatial PE event (intense localized luminance against a dark background). The wash is low PE (uniform, predictable). The glow violates the prediction that light comes FROM a direction (surface luminosity contradicts the assumed point-source model). The dapple generates moderate, fractal PE (complex but statistically regular). The chiaroscuro generates high PE through extreme luminance range. A comprehensive light panel must produce templates that account for this diversity, not collapse all light into a single "illuminance" variable.

**Position Statement 15: James Turrell — Making Light Visible**

[Note: This position statement is rendered in the phenomenological register appropriate to the practitioner, then translated into mechanistic terms by the panel.]

The Skyspaces isolate light from object. In ordinary experience, we see objects — walls, floors, trees — and light is merely the medium that makes them visible. We do not see light itself; we see what light reveals. The Skyspace reverses this. You sit in a chamber with a large rectangular aperture in the ceiling, open to the sky. As twilight deepens, the interior is washed with LED light whose spectrum is carefully controlled. The sky visible through the aperture appears to change — it becomes a dense, palpable substance, seeming to descend toward you like a colored slab of marble. You become aware that what you are seeing is not "sky" but light itself, freed from its usual role as illuminator of things.

The Roden Crater extends this to celestial scale. The naked-eye observatories frame specific light events — lunar alignments, the blue sky glow at the edge of the earth's shadow — so that the architecture directs attention to light phenomena that are always present but normally invisible against the noise of daily visual experience. The architecture functions as a perceptual filter: it removes visual complexity (the chambers are geometrically simple) to reveal light complexity (the infinite variation of sky luminance).

*Panel translation to PE terms*: What Turrell does is engineering luminance prediction error. The Skyspace works by establishing a strong luminance prediction (the interior light creates a stable adaptation state) and then violating it with the sky aperture (the sky's luminance and chromaticity change on a timescale that the visual system cannot habituate to, because the natural sky genuinely varies minute by minute during twilight). The LED interior wash is tuned to maximize the contrast between expected and perceived sky color — making the aperture appear to hold a solid substance is a perceptual illusion produced by manipulating the surround's luminance and spectrum relative to the sky. The awe response that viewers report is consistent with AX3 (Awe as High-PE Accommodation Response): the luminance PE is high (the sky does not look like "sky" anymore), requires schema accommodation (the category boundary between "surface" and "opening" is challenged), and produces the characteristic awe signature of small-self, wonder, and contemplative stillness.

## Cross-Examination and Debate

### Debate 1: Is Luminance PE a Distinct Mechanism or Just Visual Complexity Applied to Light?

**Challenge** (from existing template framework): The project already has T2 (Complexity Goldilocks Zone), which predicts that moderate visual complexity produces maximal aesthetic pleasure. Luminance contrast is a dimension of visual complexity. Do we need a separate luminance PE template, or does T2 already cover this?

**Muckli**: Luminance contrast processing has distinct neural substrates from general visual complexity. The magnocellular pathway processes luminance contrast with high temporal and spatial resolution but low chromatic sensitivity, while the parvocellular pathway processes fine detail and color (Livingstone & Hubel, 1988, ~3,000 citations). Luminance contrast is processed earlier, faster, and through different channels than the overall complexity that T2 describes. Furthermore, the predictive processing architecture for luminance involves specific feedback loops between V1 and V2 that operate on luminance-defined borders, not on overall scene statistics. The mechanisms are neurally distinguishable.

**Conway**: Additionally, luminance contrast interacts with color in ways that T2 does not capture. A red wall next to a green wall creates chromatic contrast but may have identical luminance. A black wall next to a white wall creates maximum luminance contrast but no chromatic contrast. The aesthetic and emotional responses differ, and the neural processing differs. Collapsing these into "complexity" loses the mechanistic specificity that the template framework is designed to preserve.

**Plummer**: The experiential evidence is conclusive. The shaft, the wash, the glow, and the chiaroscuro are categorically different experiences, not points on a single "complexity" dimension. Chiaroscuro is not simply "more complex" than a wash — it is a different spatial-temporal configuration of luminance that engages different emotional registers. The template framework needs to distinguish between them or it cannot serve the expert evaluating a finding about, say, the effect of directed versus diffuse light on mood.

**Panel consensus**: **Luminance PE warrants a dedicated template** distinct from T2. T2 captures overall visual complexity; the luminance PE template captures specifically the prediction and evaluation of light-dark distribution. The two interact (luminance contrast contributes to overall complexity) but are mechanistically separable.

### Debate 2: Are Circadian Effects "Prediction Error" or Something Else?

**Challenge** (from theoretical framework): The PE framework describes a computational process: brain generates prediction, sensory input arrives, mismatch is computed, error propagates. Circadian entrainment involves a photochemical signal (melanopsin → SCN → gene expression) that operates on a much slower timescale and may not involve "prediction" in the same computational sense. Is it appropriate to call circadian disruption "prediction error," or is this stretching the metaphor?

**Foster**: The SCN does generate predictions — it produces a ~24-hour oscillation in neural firing rate, hormone secretion, and gene expression that ANTICIPATES the daily light-dark cycle. Melatonin begins to rise before darkness arrives; cortisol begins to rise before dawn. These are temporal predictions. Light input calibrates them. When the light signal is phase-shifted relative to the endogenous oscillation, the result is functionally equivalent to a prediction error: the system expected darkness (based on its internal clock) but received light. The re-entrainment response — the several-day adjustment process we experience as jet lag — is the circadian system's error-correction mechanism. This is not metaphorical; it is computationally analogous to perceptual PE, just operating on a 24-hour timescale rather than a millisecond timescale.

**Panda**: At the molecular level, the clock genes (CLOCK, BMAL1, PER, CRY) form transcriptional-translational feedback loops that are literally oscillating prediction generators. The PER/CRY proteins accumulate as a "prediction" of impending darkness; light-induced PER expression adjusts this prediction. This is a biological prediction-error-correction mechanism — phylogenetically ancient and mechanistically distinct from cortical PE, but functionally homologous.

**Muckli**: From the PE framework perspective, the key question is whether the same computational principle — generate prediction, compare against input, propagate error — operates at different timescales and in different neural systems. There is strong evidence that it does. Circadian prediction operates on a 24-hour cycle in subcortical structures; perceptual prediction operates on millisecond-to-second timescales in cortical structures; allostatic prediction (already captured in T29) operates on intermediate timescales in hypothalamic and insular circuits. These are different implementations of the same principle, not a metaphorical extension.

**Panel consensus**: **Circadian disruption is legitimately modeled as temporal PE**, but the template must clearly distinguish it from perceptual PE. The timescale is different (hours vs. milliseconds), the neural substrate is different (SCN vs. cortical hierarchy), and the error-correction mechanism is different (phase-shifting the oscillator vs. updating the generative model). The template should specify these distinctions while acknowledging the computational homology.

### Debate 3: How Much of the Daylight Performance Effect Is Circadian vs. Attentional vs. View-Related?

**Challenge** (from Heschong's own data): The "Daylighting in Schools" study found a 20% performance advantage, but daylight provides circadian stimulus, alerting light, a view to outdoors, dynamic variation, and color rendering simultaneously. Can these be separated?

**Figueiro**: Partially. Studies that provide bright light WITHOUT a view (windowless rooms with high-CS electric lighting) show circadian and alertness benefits but not the full magnitude of the daylight effect (Figueiro et al., 2017, ~100 citations). Studies that provide a view WITHOUT adequate light level (small windows, tinted glazing) show mood and satisfaction benefits but less cognitive performance improvement. The full daylight effect appears to require both the photobiological signal AND the visual-psychological benefits.

**Heschong**: The school study controlled for view (a separate variable in the regression) and still found independent daylight effects, but the view coefficient was also significant and positive. Importantly, the best-performing classrooms had BOTH daylight and view — they were not separable in practice because the same architectural element (the window) provides both. This is an argument for modeling daylight as a multi-channel stimulus rather than trying to isolate a single mechanism.

**de Kort**: Laboratory studies that manipulate illuminance and spectrum independently of view, dynamics, and color rendering find significant but smaller effects than the field studies. This is consistent with the convergence hypothesis — each channel contributes a real but modest effect, and the full daylight advantage is the multiplicative convergence of all channels simultaneously.

**Steidle**: The processing-style effect (dim → creative, bright → analytical) is specifically an illuminance effect, not a view effect. It operates even in windowless laboratories. This suggests at least one mechanism — arousal-mediated precision weighting — that is independent of the view-and-circadian pathway.

**Panel consensus**: **The daylight effect is a convergence phenomenon**, not a single mechanism. The template must model it as multi-channel: circadian (ipRGC → SCN), alerting (ipRGC → brainstem), visual comfort (luminance distribution, color rendering), attentional (view → soft fascination, compatible with T27 DMN), and temporal (dynamic variation → sustained moderate PE). No single channel accounts for more than a fraction of the total effect. This parallels the wood-surfaces convergence principle from the materials domain and the BRECVEMA multi-mechanism convergence for music-evoked emotion (M12, M16).

### Debate 4: Is Sacred Light a Distinct Mechanism or Extreme Luminance PE?

**Challenge**: Plummer's taxonomy includes experiences — the shaft, the aureole, the chiaroscuro — that are phenomenologically described as "sacred," "transcendent," or "numinous." Are these produced by a distinct mechanism, or are they simply high-magnitude luminance PE that triggers the awe pathway (AX3)?

**Plummer**: The phenomenological evidence suggests that certain light configurations — particularly the shaft entering a dark volume, and the glow of a luminous surface — have a qualitative character that is not merely "more intense" than ordinary lighting. They produce a sense of presence, of encounter, of something being revealed. This is closer to the awe literature than to the aesthetic pleasure literature.

**Turrell**: The experience in a Skyspace at twilight is not adequately described as "high prediction error." It is a fundamental reorganization of perceptual categories — the boundary between substance and void, between surface and opening, between interior and exterior, becomes uncertain. This is closer to what the templates describe as schema accommodation than to error magnitude.

**Muckli**: From a predictive processing perspective, what Plummer and Turrell describe IS high PE with a specific character: the predictions being violated are not just about luminance magnitude but about categorical boundaries. When a shaft of light makes light visible as a substance, the category "illumination" (a property of scenes) is violated by the percept "luminous object" (a thing in the scene). This categorical PE — a violation of the type hierarchy, not just the expected value within a type — is consistent with what the awe template (AX3) describes as schema accommodation requiring DMN reconfiguration.

**Conway**: The "sacred" quality may also involve an evolved response to specific luminance configurations that historically signaled important environmental information. A shaft of light entering a dark space resembles the light at a cave entrance — an evolutionarily significant signal of egress, safety, and orientation. The emotional response may be partially ecological/conditioned (like the evaluative conditioning mechanism in BRECVEMA) rather than purely PE-driven.

**Panel consensus**: **Sacred light experiences are best modeled as high-magnitude luminance PE that engages the awe pathway (AX3) plus ecological conditioning**, not as a wholly distinct mechanism. However, the specific luminance configurations that produce these experiences (shaft, glow, aureole) should be identified in the template as scope conditions — they are the stimulus types most likely to push luminance PE above the Goldilocks zone and into the awe range. Plummer's taxonomy serves as the catalog of these trigger configurations.

## Panel Synthesis: What the Five Templates Collectively Reveal

### Opening Remarks from the Chair

**Foster**: Before we adjourn, I want the panel to address something that has emerged from the templates but has not been stated explicitly. We have produced five templates — luminance PE, circadian regulation, daylight convergence, color-temperature-as-ecological-signal, and dynamic light. These are not five independent mechanisms. They form an interlocking system, and the way they interlock has consequences for how the Article Eater evaluates findings about light in buildings.

### The Dual-Pathway Architecture

**Brainard**: The most fundamental structural feature across our templates is the dual-pathway architecture. L1 (luminance PE) and L4 (CCT as ecological signal) operate primarily through the image-forming visual system — rods, cones, magnocellular and parvocellular pathways, LGN, V1/V2 and beyond. L2 (circadian regulation) operates primarily through the non-visual system — ipRGCs, retinohypothalamic tract, SCN, pineal. These pathways originate in the same organ (the retina), respond to the same physical stimulus (light), but process different information and produce different downstream effects.

**Rea** [written comment incorporated by chair]: This is not a subtle distinction. The image-forming system cares about spatial luminance patterns, contrast, color. The non-visual system cares about irradiance (total photon flux at the retina), spectral composition (especially short-wavelength content), timing, and duration. An architectural lighting decision that optimizes for one system may be neutral or detrimental for the other. A warm, dim, dramatically shadowed restaurant interior (excellent for L1 aesthetics, excellent for L4 evening-appropriate CCT) is circadianly null — it delivers essentially zero circadian stimulus. A bright, cool-white, uniformly diffuse office (excellent circadian stimulus for L2) may be aesthetically deadening (low L1 PE, no L5 dynamic variation).

**Figueiro**: The practical consequence is that when the Article Eater encounters a finding about light — say, "exposure to bright light in the morning improved alertness" — the system must ask: through which pathway? If the mechanism is circadian (L2), then the relevant architectural variables are melanopic irradiance, spectral composition, timing, and duration. If the mechanism is acute alerting (a direct ipRGC → brainstem pathway that does not require circadian entrainment), that is a different causal chain with different design implications. And if the mechanism is simply arousal from increased visual stimulation (closer to L1 or Steidle's processing-mode effect), the relevant variables are illuminance and spatial distribution, not spectrum.

**Steidle**: This is exactly the kind of mechanistic disambiguation that the template system is designed to support. A finding about "bright light improves alertness" is ambiguous between at least three templates. The expert consulting Article Eater needs to see all three and assess which mechanism the study's design actually tests.

### The Convergence Principle

**Heschong**: L3 — the daylight multi-channel convergence template — is the one I want to make sure the synthesis does not understate. The reason daylight consistently outperforms artificial light in field studies is NOT that any single mechanism is dramatically large. It is that daylight simultaneously engages MULTIPLE templates: circadian entrainment (L2), luminance PE from natural shadow and contrast patterns (L1), ecologically appropriate color rendering (L4), dynamic temporal variation (L5), and — critically — view, which engages the existing templates T27 (DMN soft fascination), T5 (threat/safety via environmental monitoring), and AX5 (cross-modal congruence between visual and auditory environments).

**de Kort**: The convergence principle has a methodological implication that the panel should be explicit about. It means that studies comparing "daylight" vs. "no daylight" (or "window" vs. "no window") are testing a BUNDLE of mechanisms, not a single one. The effect sizes from such studies cannot be attributed to any individual template. They are the joint product of L1+L2+L3+L4+L5+(T27+T5+AX5). When Article Eater encounters such a finding, it should flag this: "This finding is consistent with L3 multi-channel convergence. The reported effect size likely reflects the combined operation of multiple mechanisms and cannot be attributed to any single pathway."

**Reinhart**: And conversely, studies that isolate a single variable — say, providing bright light without a view, or a view without adequate daylight — should show SMALLER effects than the combined bundle. If they show LARGER effects, that is evidence against the convergence model and in favor of a single dominant mechanism. The template system generates this prediction automatically: if L3 is correct, then partial manipulations should produce partial effects.

### The Temporal Hierarchy

**Holthuijsen**: There is a temporal structure across our templates that I want to make explicit. L1 operates on a perceptual timescale — milliseconds to seconds. The brain evaluates a luminance distribution and computes PE essentially instantaneously. L4 operates on a recognition timescale — seconds to minutes. The visual system assesses color temperature and compares it against the ecological time-of-day prediction. L5 operates on an experiential timescale — minutes to hours. Dynamic light changes sustain engagement over the period of occupancy. L2 operates on a physiological timescale — hours to days. Circadian entrainment requires sustained exposure over multiple hours, and the consequences of disruption unfold over days to weeks.

**Panda**: And the metabolic and health consequences of chronic circadian disruption operate on a timescale of months to years. A building that is circadianly inappropriate for a single day is inconsequential. A building that is circadianly inappropriate for five years of daily occupancy is a health hazard. The templates must convey this: the urgency of L2 findings scales with duration of exposure.

**Foster**: This temporal hierarchy means that different experimental designs are appropriate for testing different templates. A 30-minute laboratory exposure can test L1 (luminance PE response) and L4 (CCT recognition) but CANNOT test L2 (circadian entrainment requires days) or L5 (dynamic engagement requires hours). When Article Eater encounters a short-exposure study claiming to test "the effect of daylight on wellbeing," the system should note that the timescale is sufficient for L1/L4 mechanisms but insufficient for L2/L5 mechanisms. This is a scope-condition check that the templates can perform automatically.

### Where the Evidence is Strong, Where it is Contested

**Conway**: Before we finish I want the panel to be honest about where the scientific support differs across our templates. L2 (circadian regulation) is built on a foundation that includes Nobel Prize-winning work on molecular clock mechanisms (Hall, Rosbash, and Young, 2017 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine), thousands of studies on the ipRGC → SCN pathway, and well-characterized dose-response relationships. The neural mechanisms are established. The link to health outcomes has strong epidemiological support. This is our most mature template.

**Muckli**: L1 (luminance PE) has strong neuroscience support for the basic mechanism — predictive processing in early visual cortex, luminance contrast processing via magnocellular pathways — but the specific architectural predictions (which contrast ratios produce which aesthetic responses) are less well-characterized. The Goldilocks zone for luminance PE is not as precisely mapped as, say, the dose-response curve for melatonin suppression. The mechanism is established; the architectural parameterization is preliminary.

**Steidle**: L4 (CCT as ecological signal) has a more mixed evidence base. The core finding — that warm CCT "feels" like evening and cool CCT "feels" like daytime — is robust. The cognitive consequences (dim/warm → creativity; bright/cool → analytical focus) have been replicated in my lab and others, but there are failures to replicate as well, and the effect sizes are modest. Veitch and colleagues have challenged some of the CCT-mood associations (Veitch & Newsham, 1998, ~600 citations). The ecological framing is theoretically compelling but not yet decisively demonstrated over alternative accounts (simple arousal, aesthetic preference for warm light). I would rate the ecological-prediction mechanism as "supported" with legitimate dissent, not "established."

**de Kort**: L5 (dynamic light) is our most preliminary template. The theoretical argument is strong — static environments produce habituation, dynamic environments sustain PE — and it is consistent with the broader PE framework. But the direct experimental evidence for the specific claim that LIGHT dynamics (as opposed to visual complexity more generally, or thermal variation, or acoustic variation) sustain engagement is thin. The occupant-satisfaction literature consistently favors daylit over artificially-lit spaces, and the dynamic character of daylight is a plausible contributing factor, but it has not been cleanly isolated. This template is where we are most clearly extrapolating from theory rather than reporting from evidence.

**Heschong**: L3 (daylight convergence) is in an interesting position. The FIELD evidence for daylight's superiority is very strong — multiple large-sample studies, consistent positive effects on occupant satisfaction, cognitive performance, mood, and health. But the MECHANISTIC evidence for the specific convergence model — the claim that the effect is multiplicative across multiple independent channels — is harder to establish because the channels are naturally correlated. No study has independently manipulated all six channels simultaneously. The template is our best theoretical account of why daylight works, but it is a convergence HYPOTHESIS, not a convergence PROOF.

**Panel consensus on maturity**:
- **L2 (Circadian Regulation)**: **Established**. Neural pathway, molecular mechanisms, dose-response, and health consequences all well-characterized. Architectural translation (design variables → circadian outcomes) is moderate — the CS metric exists but is not yet standard practice.
- **L1 (Luminance PE)**: **Supported**. Neural mechanisms established. Architectural parameterization (which contrast ratios, which configurations) preliminary. Plummer's taxonomy provides the experiential catalog; quantitative mapping to PE magnitudes not yet done.
- **L4 (CCT Ecological Signal)**: **Supported with dissent**. Core CCT-mood and CCT-cognition findings replicated but with inconsistencies. The ecological-prediction framing is theoretically motivated but not uniquely supported over arousal accounts.
- **L3 (Daylight Convergence)**: **Supported**. Strong field evidence for the overall daylight effect. The multi-channel convergence model is the best available explanation but has not been rigorously tested against single-mechanism alternatives.
- **L5 (Dynamic Light)**: **Preliminary**. Theoretically compelling, consistent with the PE framework, consistent with occupant-satisfaction patterns, but direct experimental evidence for light dynamics specifically is limited.

## Testable Predictions and Critical Experiments

### Panel Discussion: What Should We Predict, and What Would Falsify Us?

**Foster**: Every template should generate predictions that are testable and, crucially, that differ from what rival accounts would predict. Otherwise we are merely redescribing known phenomena in PE language.

**Prediction 1: The Dual-Pathway Dissociation**

**Brainard**: L1 and L2 make a joint prediction that can be tested by architectural manipulation. Consider two offices:
- **Office A**: Warm-white (2700K) lighting at 300 lux, dramatically sculpted with directional fixtures creating high luminance contrast — pools of light on work surfaces, shadow gradients on walls.
- **Office B**: Cool-white (6500K) lighting at 500 lux, uniformly diffuse from ceiling panels, no directional contrast.

L1 predicts Office A produces higher aesthetic ratings and spatial pleasure (high luminance PE in the Goldilocks zone). L2 predicts Office B produces better circadian outcomes — more stable sleep, higher morning alertness, lower evening melatonin suppression latency. The critical test: do these dissociate as predicted? If both aesthetic and circadian outcomes favor the SAME office, then the dual-pathway model is wrong and a simpler account (e.g., total illuminance drives everything) may suffice.

**Steidle**: And there is a third prediction embedded here. My cognitive-processing work (feeding into L4) predicts that Office A (warm, dim) facilitates creative tasks while Office B (cool, bright) facilitates analytical tasks. If we run a creativity battery in both offices, the dim/warm office should outperform — even though it is circadianly inferior.

**Figueiro**: This is a genuinely informative experiment because the three predictions can conflict. A space could be aesthetically pleasing (L1), circadianly harmful (L2), and cognitively optimal for specific tasks (L4) simultaneously. The template system predicts this multi-dimensional optimization landscape; a single-factor theory of "good lighting" does not.

**Prediction 2: The Convergence Decrement**

**Heschong**: L3 makes a specific quantitative prediction. If daylight's effect operates through multiple converging channels, then removing any single channel should produce a measurable but partial decrement, and removing multiple channels should produce a larger decrement (roughly additive or super-additive).

**Test design**: Start with a fully daylit classroom (baseline). Then systematically degrade:
- Block the view while maintaining daylight levels (opaque diffusing glass) → expect 15–25% reduction in the overall daylight performance advantage
- Maintain view but reduce daylight to below circadian threshold (heavy tint, deep room) → expect 20–30% reduction
- Block dynamics while maintaining average illuminance (frosted glazing that diffuses temporal variation) → expect 10–15% reduction
- Block all three simultaneously (windowless with matched average illuminance) → expect > 50% reduction, i.e., more than the sum of individual degradations if the convergence is super-additive

**de Kort**: The critical falsification condition: if removing a single channel eliminates the ENTIRE daylight advantage, then that channel is dominant, not convergent, and L3 is wrong. For example, if providing adequate circadian stimulus in a windowless room (high-CS electric lighting) recovers the full daylight effect, then L2 alone explains the finding and L3's multi-channel model is unnecessary.

**Reinhart**: Architecturally, this experiment requires spaces where the channels can be independently manipulated, which is technically challenging but feasible with modern glazing technologies (electrochromic glass for tint control, patterned frits for view control, automated shading for dynamic control).

**Prediction 3: The Ecological CCT Violation**

**Hurlbert**: L4 predicts that lighting whose CCT is ecologically incongruent with the time of day produces measurable discomfort and cognitive costs. The strong test:

- **Congruent condition**: 6500K lighting in the morning, transitioning to 3000K by evening (mimics natural daylight CCT trajectory)
- **Incongruent condition**: 3000K in the morning, transitioning to 6500K by evening (reverses the natural cycle)

L4 predicts: the incongruent condition produces (a) higher self-reported discomfort, (b) poorer circadian entrainment (L2 interaction — the warm morning light has lower melanopic content), (c) lower evening melatonin (the cool evening light suppresses melatonin), and (d) — this is the distinctive ecological prediction — a subjective sense of temporal disorientation, of "something being wrong with the time," that goes beyond the circadian effects and reflects the violation of the ecological time-of-day prediction.

**Conway**: The control that distinguishes L4 from simple circadian effects: include a condition with CONSTANT 4500K (neutral, no ecological time signal either way). If L4 is correct, the incongruent condition should be WORSE than the constant condition, even though the constant condition is also ecologically impoverished. The incongruent condition actively violates the prediction; the constant condition merely fails to confirm it. Violation should produce more PE than absence.

**Steidle**: And the cognitive predictions should be crossed: creative task performance should be better in the warm-morning condition (dim/warm → relational processing) even though this condition is circadianly and ecologically wrong. If so, this demonstrates that the cognitive-processing and ecological-prediction mechanisms are genuinely independent, as L4 claims.

**Prediction 4: The Sacred Light Specificity Test**

**Plummer**: The synthesis concluded that sacred light experiences are modeled as high-magnitude luminance PE engaging the awe pathway (AX3) plus ecological conditioning. This predicts that the specific light configurations in my taxonomy — shaft, glow, aureole, chiaroscuro — should produce awe-related responses (small-self, accommodation need, contemplative affect) at higher rates than equivalent luminance contrast delivered in non-taxonomic configurations.

**Test design**: Create three conditions in a controlled laboratory space, matched for overall luminance ratio:
- **Shaft**: single directional beam entering from above into a dark volume (Plummer taxonomy, sacred type)
- **Uniform high contrast**: one bright wall, one dark wall, matched average luminance ratio (same contrast, different spatial configuration)
- **Random patches**: multiple random bright and dark patches, matched average luminance ratio (same contrast, no coherent spatial structure)

L1 predicts all three produce luminance PE proportional to contrast ratio — similar aesthetic response. AX3 + L1 jointly predict: the shaft produces significantly higher awe ratings than the other two conditions, because the shaft configuration is a SPECIFIC trigger for schema accommodation (light becomes visible substance, categorical boundary is violated), not just high PE magnitude. If all three conditions produce equivalent awe, then sacred light is just high contrast and Plummer's taxonomy has no mechanistic significance.

**Turrell**: In the Skyspace, the critical variable is the interaction between interior surround and sky aperture. If you change the surround luminance (by adjusting the LED wash), the same sky produces radically different percepts — from transparent void to opaque colored surface. This suggests the sacred-light effect depends on context-dependent prediction, not absolute contrast. The test should include a surround-manipulation condition.

**Prediction 5: The Dynamic Engagement Decay Function**

**Holthuijsen**: L5 is our most preliminary template. It needs a direct test. The prediction is that static lighting produces a decay in perceptual engagement (measured by self-report, physiological markers like EEG alpha power, or secondary task performance) over extended occupancy, while dynamic lighting that mimics natural daylight variation sustains engagement.

**Test design**: Four-hour occupancy study in identical rooms:
- **Static**: fixed 4000K, 500 lux throughout
- **Step-change**: 4000K/500 lux for 2 hours, then abrupt change to 3500K/400 lux
- **Gradual natural**: CCT and intensity follow a compressed daylight trajectory (6500K→4000K→3000K) over the 4 hours
- **Gradual random**: CCT and intensity change at the same RATE as the natural trajectory but in a random walk with no ecological pattern

L5 predicts: static shows engagement decay; step-change shows a recovery spike at the transition then renewed decay; gradual natural shows sustained engagement; gradual random shows sustained engagement but with lower subjective satisfaction than gradual natural (because the ecological prediction from L4 is violated even though the PE is sustained).

**de Kort**: The gradual-natural vs. gradual-random comparison is the critical dissociation. If both produce equal engagement and equal satisfaction, then dynamics alone explain the effect and the ecological component (L4) is unnecessary. If gradual-natural produces higher satisfaction despite equal PE, then the ecological prediction adds something beyond mere temporal variation.

## Open Questions and Recommendations for Future Panels

**Foster**: Three gaps remain that this panel has identified but cannot fill.

**1. Melanopic metrics and standards**: The field is currently transitioning from photopic illuminance (lux) to melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance (m-EDI) as the standard for circadian-relevant light measurement (CIE S 026/E:2018). L2 should eventually reference specific m-EDI thresholds as scope conditions, but the dose-response curves are still being refined. When they stabilize, L2's scope conditions should be updated.

**2. Individual differences**: Chronotype (morning vs. evening types), age (older adults have less blue-light transmission through the lens), seasonal sensitivity (seasonal affective disorder), and prior light history all moderate the templates' predictions. None of our five templates adequately captures individual-difference moderation. This is a general limitation of the template framework — the templates describe population-level mechanisms — but it is especially acute for circadian effects where individual variation is large.

**3. LED spectral engineering**: The rapid shift from broadband sources (incandescent, daylight) to narrow-band sources (LEDs) creates new PE phenomena that are not yet well-characterized. Metameric failure under LEDs (two surfaces that match under one LED but differ under another) is a form of chromatic PE that L4 partially addresses but that may warrant a dedicated template if the evidence grows. The interaction between LED spectral characteristics and color constancy mechanisms (Hurlbert's concern) is an active research frontier.

**Plummer**: And I would note that the phenomenological dimension — HOW light is experienced, not just what it does to neural circuits — deserves further development. The taxonomy of light types (shaft, wash, glow, dapple, aureole, chiaroscuro) is incorporated into L1 as scope conditions, but a future panel might explore whether these types map onto distinct experiential categories that have their own psychological profiles, beyond what luminance PE alone predicts.

**Holthuijsen**: From the design-practice perspective, the biggest gap is the interaction between light and materials. How light reveals (or conceals) material properties — grain, texture, color, reflectance — is the core of architectural lighting design, and it spans our templates (L1 luminance on surfaces, L4 color rendering of materials) AND the forthcoming Materials panel (A1). I recommend that the Materials panel explicitly address the light-material interaction.

**Panel consensus**: The Light panel has successfully filled the project's largest gap, lifting A4 from ★ to ★★★. The remaining work is empirical calibration (L1 parameterization, L3 convergence testing, L5 direct evidence) and cross-domain integration (light-materials interaction, individual differences). These are refinements of a solid mechanistic foundation, not structural omissions.
