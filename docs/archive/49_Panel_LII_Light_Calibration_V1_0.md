# PANEL L-II: LIGHT CALIBRATION — PARAMETERIZING THE FIVE LIGHT TEMPLATES
## Empirical Calibration, Dissent Resolution, and Architectural Translation for L1–L5
## February 16, 2026 — Document 49

---

## Panel Charge

Panel L-I (Doc 34) produced five mechanistic templates covering luminance contrast PE (L1), circadian architectural regulation (L2), daylight multi-channel convergence (L3), color temperature as ecological signal (L4), and dynamic light temporal PE (L5). These templates provide Article Eater with a causal vocabulary for light — the system can now identify which mechanism a light-related finding invokes and check scope conditions. But the panel's own closing assessment was blunt about what was NOT delivered: empirical calibration. The templates are structurally complete but parametrically empty.

Without calibration, Article Eater's evaluations of light findings remain qualitative — "this finding is consistent with L1" rather than "this finding's effect size falls within the predicted range for these contrast ratios." The difference is the difference between a useful tool and a hand-waving machine, as Foster put it at the close of L-I.

Panel L-II is therefore a different kind of panel from L-I. Where L-I built the framework, L-II's charge is to TUNE it. Specifically:

1. **Calibrate L1** — Establish quantitative boundary estimates for the luminance-PE Goldilocks zone: what contrast ratios produce comfort, aesthetic pleasure, dramatic intensity, and glare discomfort? In which contexts (task environments vs. experiential spaces)?

2. **Test L3** — Assess whether the daylight multi-channel convergence model survives scrutiny. Do the channels contribute independently? Can channel weights be estimated? Does removing a single channel eliminate the entire daylight advantage, or does each channel contribute a partial and additive benefit?

3. **Resolve L4** — The CCT-cognition findings that motivate L4's ecological-signal framing have been challenged. This panel brings in the dissenters. Does the ecological-prediction mechanism survive, or does a simpler arousal account suffice?

4. **Establish or downgrade L5** — L5 (dynamic light) is L-I's most preliminary template. Does direct experimental evidence support the specific claim that light DYNAMICS (not just illuminance level or view) sustain perceptual engagement? Or should L5 be downgraded to a sub-component of L3?

5. **Extend to artificial lighting design** — L-I's templates are anchored to daylight. A broadening effort should formalize the PE implications of ARTIFICIAL lighting design decisions — the variables that practitioners actually control.

6. **Formalize aging moderators** — L2's dose-response parameters are systematically age-dependent due to progressive lens yellowing. This needs quantitative specification, not just a moderator note.

The panel is NOT charged with producing entirely new templates. It should produce: (a) calibrated parameter estimates for L1, L3, and L5; (b) a verdict on L4's ecological framing; (c) possibly one new template (L6: artificial lighting PE specification) if the broadening discussion warrants it; and (d) formalized age-dependent moderators for L2.

## Position Statements

### DEEPENING L1: THE LUMINANCE GOLDILOCKS ZONE

**Position Statement 1: Muckli and Rockcastle — Toward Quantitative Boundaries for Luminance Contrast PE**

**Muckli**: L-I established that luminance contrast processing is fundamentally predictive — V1 and V2 generate expectations about light distributions, and violations of those expectations produce visual prediction error. What L-I did NOT specify is where the Goldilocks boundaries fall: what contrast ratios produce pleasant processing fluency, what ratios produce peak aesthetic response, and what ratios produce discomfort.

The neuroscience provides some constraint. My laboratory's work on contextual modulation of V1 responses (Muckli & Petro, 2013, ~300 citations) demonstrates that contextual predictions are strongest when the visual scene is statistically regular — when the brain has reliable priors. A uniformly lit room provides high regularity and low PE; a dramatically lit space with strong directional shadows provides moderate regularity (the light direction is predictable, but the shadow boundaries create local contrast) and moderate PE; a randomly patched light environment provides low regularity and high PE that likely exceeds the Goldilocks zone.

The critical missing piece is the MAPPING between physical contrast metrics and neural PE magnitude. We know that magnocellular pathway neurons respond to luminance contrast with a contrast-gain function that saturates above roughly 10:1 ratio. We know that higher visual areas show inverted-U response profiles to complexity (consistent with the Goldilocks prediction). But the specific contrast ratios that produce the transition from "comfortable" to "interesting" to "dramatic" to "painful" in ARCHITECTURAL viewing conditions have not been measured using PE-compatible paradigms.

**Rockcastle**: My work provides a partial bridge. We have measured occupant aesthetic and comfort responses to daylit spaces with quantified luminance distributions (Rockcastle & Andersen, 2014, ~100 citations). The metrics that best predict occupant response are NOT simple contrast ratio (max:min luminance) but rather the spatial distribution of contrast — what I call luminance variability metrics. A space with high overall contrast concentrated in one dramatic sunlight patch produces different responses than a space with the same average contrast distributed in many small patches.

Our data suggest the following preliminary boundaries — these are the first quantitative anchors the panel can work with:

- **Coefficient of variation of luminance < 0.5**: Occupants rate the space as "uniform" — low visual interest, moderate-to-high comfort. This is the low-PE comfort zone for task environments. Standard office lighting (diffuse ceiling panels) typically falls here.
- **CV 0.5–1.5**: The aesthetic zone. Occupants rate the space as "interesting" and "pleasant." This is where well-designed daylit spaces typically fall — directional light creating readable shadow patterns, some sunlight patches, visible gradients from window to room depth.
- **CV 1.5–3.0**: The dramatic zone. Occupants use words like "powerful," "impressive," and "dramatic." Museums, churches, theatrical spaces. Comfort ratings begin to decline for sustained occupancy but aesthetic ratings can be very high.
- **CV > 3.0**: The discomfort zone. Unmanaged glare, extreme contrast between bright windows and dark interior surfaces. Occupants avert gaze, report visual fatigue, reduce time in the space.

I must emphasize that these boundaries are PRELIMINARY. They derive from a limited number of studies with predominantly Western participants in specific building types. The boundaries almost certainly shift with adaptation state, task demands, cultural familiarity, and individual photosensitivity. But they are a starting point.

**Chamilothori**: My sunlight-patch studies provide additional granularity within the aesthetic zone. We systematically varied the size, shape, edge sharpness, and contrast of sunlight patches on interior surfaces and measured occupant preference, interest, and comfort (Chamilothori et al., 2019, ~60 citations). The key findings:

- **Sunlight patches with soft edges** (as produced by diffuse glazing or deep window reveals) are preferred over hard-edged patches. In PE terms: soft edges create gradual luminance gradients that the visual system can predict smoothly; hard edges create step-function contrast that produces localized high PE.
- **Moderate-size patches** (covering 10–30% of a visible surface) are preferred over small (< 5%, barely noticeable) or large (> 50%, dominating). This is directly consistent with the Goldilocks prediction for spatial extent of contrast.
- **Moving sunlight patches** (which shift as clouds pass) are rated as more interesting and pleasant than static patches of the same contrast — direct evidence for L5's dynamic engagement claim.

**Wilkins**: I want to inject a caution about individual variation. The boundaries Rockcastle describes apply to the MEDIAN occupant. But the distribution has a long tail. In our studies on visual stress, approximately 15–20% of the general population reports significant discomfort from spatial luminance patterns that the median occupant finds perfectly comfortable (Wilkins, 1995, ~600 citations). For clinical populations — migraine sufferers (affecting ~12% of adults), photosensitive individuals — the discomfort threshold can be 50–70% lower than the median.

This means the L1 Goldilocks zone is not a fixed zone — it is a DISTRIBUTION of zones across the population. A lighting designer cannot assume a single comfort threshold. The practical implication: architectural spaces must provide luminance variability CONTROL (operable shading, adjustable artificial lighting, choice of position relative to windows) so that occupants can self-select their PE level.

### DEEPENING L3: CAN WE SEPARATE THE DAYLIGHT CHANNELS?

**Position Statement 2: Heschong and de Kort — Testing the Convergence Model**

**Heschong**: L3 claims that daylight's consistent superiority over artificial light is explained by multi-channel convergence — daylight simultaneously engages circadian entrainment (L2), luminance contrast aesthetics (L1), ecological color rendering (L4), dynamic temporal variation (L5), and view. Each channel contributes a partial benefit; the convergent total exceeds the sum.

The evidence for the OVERALL daylight effect is very strong. My daylighting-in-schools studies (Heschong et al., 2002, ~500 citations), the subsequent workplace productivity literature, and the healthcare outcomes research consistently show that daylit spaces outperform artificially lit ones on cognitive performance, mood, satisfaction, and health outcomes. Effect sizes are moderate but consistent across building types and populations.

What we do NOT have is channel-separation evidence. The channels are naturally correlated in daylit buildings: a window that provides daylight also provides a view, dynamic variation, and ecological CCT. No existing study has independently manipulated all channels. But we have PARTIAL separation evidence:

1. **View separable from light**: My own finding that view content predicts performance independently of illumination level (Heschong et al., 2002) separates one channel. This led to VIEW1 being a separate template.
2. **Circadian separable from visual**: Figueiro's work showing that circadian-optimized electric lighting (high melanopic content) improves sleep and alertness in windowless environments demonstrates that the L2 channel can operate WITHOUT the other daylight channels. But the effect sizes are smaller than the full-daylight studies, consistent with the convergence model.
3. **Dynamics separable from average illuminance**: Chamilothori's finding that moving sunlight patches are preferred over static ones at the same average contrast separates L5 from L1. But we do not know the magnitude of L5's independent contribution.

**de Kort**: The critical experiment that L-II needs to design — or identify in the existing literature — is a FACTORIAL channel-separation study. The ideal design:

- **Full daylight** (all channels active) — the ceiling
- **Daylight minus view** (opaque diffusing glass — maintains light level, spectrum, dynamics; eliminates view) — subtracts VIEW1 and partially subtracts L5 dynamics
- **Daylight minus dynamics** (fixed-tint electrochromic glass that maintains average transmittance but blocks temporal variation) — subtracts L5
- **Daylight minus circadian** (orange-tinted glass that blocks short wavelengths while maintaining illuminance) — subtracts L2
- **Matched artificial light** (electric lighting calibrated to match daylight average illuminance, CCT, and spatial distribution) — subtracts L1 contrast patterns, L5 dynamics, and view

The dependent measures need to span the channels: circadian markers (melatonin, cortisol) for L2; aesthetic and comfort ratings for L1; attention measures for VIEW1/L5; task performance for the overall bundle.

I must be honest: this experiment is technically demanding. It requires architectural glazing technologies that can independently manipulate spectral transmission, view clarity, and dynamic variation. And it requires sustained occupancy (days, not hours) to capture L2 circadian effects. We may need to piece together the answer from multiple partial studies rather than a single definitive factorial.

**Foster**: The circadian community has relevant partial evidence. Several studies have compared: (a) daylight through windows vs. (b) circadian-optimized electric lighting (high melanopic content, timed delivery) in healthcare and workplace settings. The consistent finding: circadian-optimized electric lighting improves sleep quality and alertness relative to standard electric lighting, BUT daylight still produces better outcomes than the optimized electric alternative. This supports L3: there is a residual daylight benefit beyond the circadian channel that the electric system cannot replicate. The residual is plausibly the view + dynamics + ecological CCT channels.

**Reinhart**: From the building-science side, I want to note that the channel-separation experiment de Kort describes is becoming technically feasible. Electrochromic glazing systems (SageGlass, View Inc.) can dynamically tint windows, controlling light transmission independently of view. Combined with automated venetian blind systems (for dynamic variation control) and spectral-selective films (for circadian-content manipulation), a factorial design is within reach. I would estimate 3–5 purpose-built test chambers could be outfitted for roughly $500K — expensive but not prohibitive for a well-funded laboratory.

### DEEPENING L4: THE CCT DEBATE

**Position Statement 3: Steidle vs. Veitch — Is CCT an Ecological Signal or Just Arousal?**

**Steidle**: The findings from my laboratory and others: dim, warm-white lighting (≤ 150 lux, ≤ 3000K) promotes relational and creative processing, while bright, cool-white lighting (≥ 500 lux, ≥ 5000K) promotes analytical and detail-oriented processing (Steidle & Werth, 2013, ~350 citations; Steidle et al., 2011, ~100 citations). The ecological framing explains why: dim/warm is the light of evening, campfire, relaxation — conditions that historically favored social bonding, storytelling, and open-ended thinking. Bright/cool is the light of midday, full sun, active engagement — conditions that favored focused task performance. The brain uses CCT and illuminance as ecological TIME-OF-DAY signals that trigger corresponding processing modes.

This is L4's central claim, and I stand by the ecological framing because: (a) the dim → creativity effect has been replicated in multiple laboratories; (b) the effect operates through construal level (dim light promotes abstract, high-level construal — Steidle et al., 2011), which is exactly what the ecological model predicts; (c) the effect interacts with light COLOR, not just intensity — warm CCT at moderate illuminance promotes creativity more than cool CCT at the same illuminance, suggesting it is NOT merely arousal.

**Veitch**: With respect, the evidence for an ecological CCT-cognition pathway is weaker than Anna presents. My concerns:

First, the REPLICATION LANDSCAPE is uneven. The dim → creativity effect has been replicated, yes, but with variable effect sizes and several published failures to replicate (Veitch & Newsham, 1998, ~600 citations; Souman et al., 2018, ~100 citations). The inconsistency is what you would expect if the effect is real but smaller than initially reported and highly dependent on task type, population, and experimental context — NOT what you would expect from a strong ecological signal.

Second, the AROUSAL ALTERNATIVE has not been ruled out. Cool bright light is more arousing than warm dim light — this is uncontroversial. Arousal facilitates focused attention and analytical performance. Reduced arousal facilitates mind-wandering and associative processing. The dim → creativity finding follows directly from this arousal pathway WITHOUT any need for an ecological-prediction mechanism. The CCT → cognition pathway may simply be: CCT → arousal → processing mode.

Third, the ECOLOGICAL framing requires that the brain specifically encodes CCT as a time-of-day signal. But color constancy mechanisms compensate for illuminant changes — the visual system is designed to DISCOUNT illuminant color so that object colors appear stable. If the system discounts CCT, it cannot simultaneously use CCT as an ecological time-of-day signal. This is a genuine theoretical problem.

**Steidle**: The color constancy objection has an answer. Color constancy operates for OBJECT color perception — it stabilizes the appearance of surfaces under varying illumination. But the OVERALL ambient light level and spectral composition ARE perceived, precisely because they are not attributed to any object — they are properties of the illumination itself. Kwallek et al. (1996, ~200 citations) demonstrated that ambient color affects mood and performance even when participants are not consciously aware of the color of the light. This is consistent with a subcortical or pre-attentive ecological signal, not a conscious color-constancy-mediated percept.

**Veitch**: That is plausible but not proven. And I have a third concern that is methodological: most CCT-cognition studies confound CCT with illuminance. Warm light is typically dimmer; cool light is typically brighter. The studies that control for illuminance while varying CCT alone generally find smaller or null effects (Souman et al., 2018). This suggests illuminance (arousal) is doing more of the work than CCT (ecological signal).

**Foster**: I want both positions on the record because this is exactly the kind of legitimate scientific disagreement that Article Eater must handle. The panel's task is not to resolve this debate definitively — that requires the ecological-violation experiment described in L-I's predictions section — but to characterize the uncertainty so that Article Eater can evaluate CCT-cognition findings with appropriate caveats.

### DEEPENING L5: DOES DYNAMIC LIGHT HAVE INDEPENDENT EFFECTS?

**Position Statement 4: de Kort and Chamilothori — The Evidence for Light Dynamics**

**de Kort**: L5 is our most preliminary template, and L-II must decide whether it deserves to remain independent or should be absorbed into L3 as one of daylight's convergent channels. The theoretical argument is compelling: static environments produce habituation (the nervous system down-regulates responses to unchanging stimuli), dynamic environments sustain PE (continuous small changes prevent complete habituation). But this argument applies to ALL sensory modalities, not specifically to light. The question for L5 is: do LIGHT dynamics specifically sustain visual engagement, independently of other dynamics (view, temperature, sound)?

The evidence is limited but suggestive. First, the occupant-satisfaction literature consistently favors daylit over artificially lit spaces, and daylight is inherently dynamic (changing intensity, spectral composition, and shadow patterns through the day) while most artificial lighting is static. But this correlation does not establish causation — daylit spaces also provide view, higher illuminance, and better color rendering.

Second, Chamilothori's finding that moving sunlight patches are preferred over static ones provides the most direct evidence. But this is a preference rating in a controlled setting, not a sustained-engagement measure.

Third, there is an emerging literature on dynamic artificial lighting — lighting systems that change CCT and intensity through the day, mimicking the natural daylight trajectory. Studies by Magielse et al. (2019, ~30 citations) and others suggest that dynamic artificial lighting produces higher satisfaction than static lighting, but the effects are confounded with circadian optimization (L2): the dynamic systems typically provide higher melanopic content in the morning and lower in the evening, which is a circadian intervention, not purely a dynamics intervention.

**Chamilothori**: I can add one important piece: in our studies, we included a condition where sunlight patches moved at RANDOM (same rate of change, same average contrast, but no ecological pattern — patches drifting in random directions rather than tracking sun position). Occupants rated the random-dynamic condition as more interesting than static but LESS pleasant than the natural-tracking dynamic condition. This suggests that dynamics sustain interest (supporting L5) but that the PATTERN of dynamics matters (supporting L4's ecological-signal framing — even for temporal patterns, ecological regularity produces higher satisfaction than arbitrary variation).

**de Kort**: Panel recommendation: L5 should be RETAINED as an independent template, but marked as "preliminary — requires direct testing." The evidence for dynamic engagement per se is consistent but not definitive. The critical experiment — static vs. gradual-natural vs. gradual-random vs. step-change, over a 4-hour sustained-occupancy period, measuring engagement decay — was described in L-I's predictions and should be conducted.

### BROADENING: AGING AND PHOTOSENSITIVITY

**Position Statement 5: Figueiro and Wilkins — Why the Templates Need Age-Dependent Parameters**

**Figueiro**: The circadian system loses sensitivity with age. The mechanism is well-characterized: progressive yellowing and thickening of the crystalline lens attenuates short-wavelength light reaching the retina. By age 65, the lens transmits roughly 50% of the blue light it transmitted at age 25 (Turner & Mainster, 2008, ~300 citations). Since the circadian system's peak sensitivity is at ~480nm (in the blue range), older adults receive approximately HALF the circadian stimulus from the same lighting environment as younger adults.

In my studies of older adults in care facilities (Figueiro et al., 2014, ~200 citations; Figueiro et al., 2017, ~100 citations), we found that circadian-optimized lighting interventions — high melanopic content at eye level during daytime hours — significantly improved sleep quality, reduced nighttime awakenings, and reduced agitation in dementia patients. But the required melanopic irradiance to achieve CS ≥ 0.3 was roughly DOUBLE what younger adults need. This means L2's dose-response parameters need an age correction factor: the CS threshold for architectural design should be specified as a function of expected occupant age.

The architectural implication is profound. Senior living facilities, hospitals with elderly patients, and any building with an aging workforce need HIGHER melanopic irradiance than standard recommendations provide. This can be delivered through: larger windows (more daylight), higher-CCT electric lighting (more blue content), or light at eye level (task lights, illuminated vertical surfaces) rather than overhead (ceiling fixtures illuminate horizontal surfaces, which are less effective for circadian photoreception because pupil aperture reduces the solid angle).

**Wilkins**: The photosensitivity side of the distribution is equally important and architecturally underserved. Approximately 12% of adults experience migraine, and migraine sufferers have significantly lower thresholds for visual discomfort from luminance contrast (Wilkins et al., 1984, ~500 citations). Additionally, roughly 3% of epilepsy patients have photosensitive seizure triggers, and a broader population (perhaps 10–15%) reports significant visual stress — discomfort, headache, perceptual distortion — from specific spatial patterns of luminance.

The relevant architectural variables are: flicker frequency (fluorescent lamps with magnetic ballasts, now largely obsolete, were the worst offender), spatial frequency content (striped patterns at ~3 cycles/degree are maximally uncomfortable), and luminance contrast magnitude (lower thresholds in sensitive populations shift the L1 Goldilocks zone leftward). Modern LED lighting can introduce high-frequency flicker that is not consciously perceived but produces measurable cortical responses and discomfort in sensitive individuals (Wilkins et al., 2010, ~200 citations).

The practical recommendation: L1's Goldilocks zone should be specified not as a single boundary but as a DISTRIBUTION with marked confidence intervals. Design for the 85th percentile of sensitivity (accommodating the non-clinical sensitive population) rather than the 50th. This is the same design philosophy as thermal comfort (ASHRAE specifies satisfaction for 80% of occupants, acknowledging that universal satisfaction is impossible) and acoustic comfort (noise criteria accommodate the sensitive).

### BROADENING: ARTIFICIAL LIGHTING DESIGN

**Position Statement 6: Reinhart — The Templates Must Speak to Artificial Light**

**Reinhart**: Four of L-I's five templates are most naturally satisfied by daylight. L2 (circadian) — daylight provides the highest melanopic content. L3 (convergence) — daylight bundles multiple channels. L4 (ecological CCT) — daylight provides the ecologically appropriate spectral trajectory. L5 (dynamics) — daylight changes naturally through the day. Even L1 (luminance contrast) is most richly illustrated by daylight's directional shadows.

This creates a practical problem for Article Eater. A large fraction of the architecture-wellbeing literature studies ARTIFICIAL lighting — office ceiling panels, classroom fluorescents, hospital corridor luminaires. When the system encounters a finding about artificial lighting, the templates are applicable but the architectural-translation pathways are unclear. What design decisions about artificial lighting map onto L1's contrast distribution? How does luminaire selection (direct vs. indirect, recessed vs. pendant, narrow beam vs. wide) affect the PE Goldilocks zone?

I propose that L-II should produce a bridging framework — not necessarily a new template, but a systematic mapping between artificial lighting DESIGN VARIABLES and the existing template mechanisms:

- **Luminaire type and beam distribution** → L1 (contrast/luminance distribution). A recessed downlight creates a narrow cone of high illuminance surrounded by lower ambient → moderate L1 PE. A large indirect luminaire creates uniform diffuse illumination → low L1 PE. Pendant fixtures at varied heights create a complex luminance landscape → potentially high L1 PE.
- **CCT and spectral power distribution** → L2 (melanopic content) and L4 (ecological signal). Tunable-white LED systems can vary CCT from 2700K to 6500K; the design question is WHEN and HOW to deploy each setting.
- **Lighting control strategy** → L5 (dynamics). A static lighting system provides zero L5 engagement. A time-scheduled system that tracks daylight CCT trajectory provides some L5 engagement. A daylight-responsive dimming system that varies electric light to complement daylight provides the most L5 engagement from artificial sources.
- **Luminaire layout and integration with surfaces** → L1 again. Grazing light along a textured wall (emphasizing material grain) produces different L1 PE than the same light directed at a smooth ceiling. The luminaire-surface interaction determines the luminance PATTERN that the visual system processes.

The goal is not to replace daylight — L3's convergence principle ensures that artificial light will always be a partial substitute — but to give Article Eater the ability to evaluate artificial lighting findings against the same mechanistic framework, and to help designers optimize the PE contribution of electric lighting when daylight is insufficient.

## Cross-Examination and Calibration Debates

### Debate 1: Resolving the L4 Ecological Signal — Verdict

**Foster**: We have heard Steidle's defense and Veitch's challenge. The panel must now render a judgment. The question is not whether dim/warm light affects cognition — both sides agree it does. The question is whether the mechanism is ecological prediction (L4's current framing) or simple arousal modulation.

**Steidle**: My strongest evidence for the ecological framing: the interaction between CCT and illuminance. If it were ONLY arousal, then any dim light should promote creativity regardless of spectral composition. But warm-dim produces stronger creativity effects than cool-dim at matched illuminance. This implicates the spectral composition — the COLOR of the light — as a variable beyond mere intensity.

**Veitch**: I accept the interaction is real, but offer an alternative: warm CCT is experienced as more comfortable and relaxing than cool CCT at any given illuminance. Comfort reduces anxiety, reduces self-monitoring, and frees associative processing. This is still an arousal/affect pathway, just one mediated by aesthetic preference rather than ecological prediction.

**de Kort**: I want to point out that these two accounts make different predictions for one specific condition: COOL light at LOW illuminance. The arousal model predicts: low illuminance → low arousal → creativity, regardless of CCT. The ecological model predicts: cool light at low illuminance is ecologically incongruent (cool light in nature is always bright — it is daytime full-sun) → this incongruence should produce DISCOMFORT or cognitive interference, NOT creativity. Has anyone tested cool-dim specifically?

**Steidle**: My 2013 study included a dim/cool condition. The creativity effect was present but weaker than dim/warm. This is intermediate between the two predictions — consistent with arousal (dim → creativity) but attenuated by the incongruence (cool → mild discomfort).

**Veitch**: Or consistent with arousal (dim → creativity) attenuated by the stimulating quality of cool light (cool → somewhat higher arousal even at low illuminance, partially offsetting the dim effect).

**Foster**: We are at an impasse. Both accounts predict the observed data with roughly equal precision. The ecological-prediction framing is more theoretically elegant and connects to the broader PE framework, but it is not uniquely supported. The arousal account is simpler and has fewer assumptions.

**Panel verdict on L4**: **RETAIN with acknowledged dual interpretation.** L4 is modified as follows:

The template retains the ecological-signal framing as the PREFERRED interpretation because it integrates naturally with the PE framework and generates testable predictions that the arousal account does not (specifically, the ecological-violation experiment from L-I Prediction 3: cool-bright in the evening should be WORSE than constant neutral, not just different from warm-dim). BUT the template must explicitly acknowledge the arousal-mediation alternative and mark the ecological framing as "supported with legitimate dissent." Article Eater, when encountering a CCT-cognition finding, should evaluate it against BOTH interpretations and note which aspects of the study design favor one account over the other.

**Maturity revision**: L4 moves from "supported" to **"supported with acknowledged dissent — ecological and arousal interpretations not yet empirically distinguished."**

### Debate 2: L5 Status — Retain or Absorb?

**de Kort**: The evidence for L5 as an independent mechanism is suggestive but thin. Chamilothori's moving-vs-static sunlight finding is the strongest direct evidence. The dynamic-artificial-lighting literature is confounded with circadian optimization. The theoretical argument (static → habituation, dynamic → sustained engagement) is compelling but applies to all modalities, not specifically to light.

**Chamilothori**: My random-vs-natural dynamics finding is relevant here. If L5 were simply "any change prevents habituation," then random dynamics should perform as well as natural-tracking dynamics. They do not — natural-tracking produces higher satisfaction. This suggests that L5 is not merely anti-habituation but engages an ecological prediction about the PATTERN of light change (sun-tracking dynamics match a prediction about how natural light should behave).

**Heschong**: I support retaining L5 because the occupant-satisfaction gap between daylit and artificially lit spaces is LARGER than can be explained by L1 (contrast) + L2 (circadian) + L4 (CCT) alone. Something about daylight's TEMPORAL CHARACTER contributes. If we absorb L5 into L3, we lose the ability to evaluate dynamic lighting interventions separately.

**Panel verdict on L5**: **RETAIN as independent template, upgrade from "preliminary" to "supported-preliminary."** The evidence base has grown since L-I: Chamilothori's controlled studies, the emerging dynamic-lighting literature, and the residual daylight-preference gap all support an independent L5 mechanism. But the critical 4-hour engagement-decay experiment has not been conducted. L5 remains the LEAST calibrated of the five light templates.

### Calibration Session 1: L1 Luminance Contrast PE Boundaries

**Foster**: We now move to the calibration work that is L-II's primary deliverable. Rockcastle has proposed preliminary CV-of-luminance boundaries. The panel should evaluate, refine, and contextualize these boundaries.

**Rockcastle**: To recap: CV < 0.5 (uniform/comfortable), CV 0.5–1.5 (aesthetic/interesting), CV 1.5–3.0 (dramatic/powerful), CV > 3.0 (discomfort/glare). These come from our daylit-space studies.

**Muckli**: These map well onto what we know about visual cortex response. The magnocellular contrast-gain function saturates near 10:1 local contrast; above this, the neural signal plateaus and the remaining PE comes from spatial configuration rather than contrast magnitude. Rockcastle's "dramatic zone" (CV 1.5–3.0) likely corresponds to conditions where many local regions exceed the 10:1 saturation threshold, producing a rich spatial PE pattern. Her "discomfort zone" (CV > 3.0) likely corresponds to conditions where the average luminance exceeds comfortable adaptation range — glare, essentially, which Wienold's DGP metric already quantifies.

**Wilkins**: For photosensitive populations, shift ALL boundaries downward by approximately 30–50%. The discomfort threshold in migraine patients is typically 0.5–0.7x the normal threshold (Wilkins, 1995). So a migraine sufferer might experience discomfort at CV ~1.5–2.0, which the median occupant would rate as merely "dramatic."

**Plummer**: I want to connect these boundaries to my experiential taxonomy. The experiences I have documented — the shaft, the glow, the wash, the aureole — each occupy specific regions of this space:

- **The wash**: uniform, diffuse illumination from large windows or reflected daylight. CV < 0.5. Pure L1 comfort zone. Calming, spacious, but not dramatic.
- **The glow**: warm, low-level ambient illumination (candlelight, firelight, indirect uplighting). Low CV but at low absolute luminance — the Goldilocks zone at reduced intensity. This is L4 territory as much as L1.
- **The dapple**: scattered small patches of light through foliage, perforated screens, or textured glazing. CV 0.5–1.5. Classic aesthetic zone. Engaging, pleasant, suggesting nature.
- **The shaft**: a single strong beam of directional light penetrating a dark volume. CV 1.5–3.0 or higher — dramatic zone. This is the light of cathedrals, of Baroque church interiors. The emotional response includes awe (AX3 connection).
- **The aureole**: a figure or surface surrounded by light that seems to emanate from within. Extreme local contrast but within a controlled visual field. CV variable but perceptually coherent. Turrell's Skyspaces produce this.

**Panel consensus on L1 calibration**:

The panel adopts Rockcastle's CV-of-luminance boundaries as PRELIMINARY L1 calibration anchors, with the following qualifications:

| Zone | CV Range | Experiential Character | PE Level | Architectural Examples | Context Dependence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Comfort | < 0.5 | Uniform, calm, readable | Low PE (prediction confirmed) | Office ceiling panels, overcast daylight, the wash | Appropriate for sustained task environments |
| Aesthetic | 0.5–1.5 | Interesting, pleasant, engaging | Moderate PE (Goldilocks optimum) | Well-designed daylit spaces, the dapple, pendant lighting | Ideal for mixed-use, educational, residential |
| Dramatic | 1.5–3.0 | Powerful, impressive, intense | High PE (approaching accommodation) | Museums, sacred spaces, the shaft | Appropriate for experiential/cultural spaces |
| Discomfort | > 3.0 | Painful, aversive, fatiguing | Excessive PE (negative valence) | Unmanaged glare, bright window in dark room | Avoid in all sustained-occupancy settings |

**Caveats**: (1) Boundaries shift ±30–50% for photosensitive populations. (2) Boundaries are adaptation-state dependent — a dark-adapted occupant entering a daylit atrium experiences higher effective CV than a light-adapted one. (3) Spatial configuration matters: the SAME CV can feel pleasant (directional light creating readable shadows) or unpleasant (random bright patches without spatial logic). (4) These boundaries are from Western, predominantly office/educational settings. Cross-cultural calibration is needed.

### Calibration Session 2: L2 Age-Dependent Parameters

**Figueiro**: L2 is the most calibrated template in the system. The age-correction is straightforward. I propose the following formalization:

**Standard L2 dose-response** (adults 20–45):
- Circadian stimulus threshold: CS ≥ 0.3 for ≥ 2 hours during morning/early afternoon
- Melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance (M-EDI): ≥ 250 lux at eye level
- This is achievable with: window-adjacent workstation in a well-daylit office; or ceiling-mounted 6500K LED panels at 500+ lux

**Age-corrected L2 dose-response** (adults 65+):
- Required M-EDI approximately DOUBLES: ≥ 500 lux at eye level
- This typically requires: supplementary high-CCT task lighting, illuminated vertical surfaces at seated eye height, or significantly larger windows
- Healthcare/senior-care design implication: standard ceiling-only overhead lighting is INSUFFICIENT for circadian needs in elderly populations

**Age correction function** (linear approximation):
- Required M-EDI(age) ≈ M-EDI(25) × (1 + 0.015 × (age − 25))
- This gives approximately 60% increase at age 65, 100% at age 90
- Precision: ±20% individual variation even within age cohorts

**Foster**: This is exactly the kind of calibration that makes a template practically useful. When Article Eater encounters a finding about light in a senior care facility, it can now check: was the melanopic irradiance sufficient for the AGE GROUP? A study reporting null circadian effects in elderly patients at 250 lux M-EDI would be correctly flagged as below threshold, not as evidence against L2.

### Calibration Session 3: L3 Channel-Weight Estimation

**Heschong**: We cannot run the full factorial experiment in this session, but we can estimate channel weights from the existing partial-separation evidence.

**Evidence-based estimation** (rough, to be refined by VIEW-II and L-II Priority 2):

1. **Circadian channel (L2)**: Figueiro's work shows that circadian-optimized electric lighting recovers approximately 40–60% of the daylight effect on sleep and alertness. Estimated weight: ~0.35.
2. **View channel (VIEW1)**: My finding that view content predicts performance independently of illumination, plus the literature on nature views, suggests view contributes approximately 20–30% of the overall daylight effect. Estimated weight: ~0.25.
3. **Luminance contrast channel (L1)**: Daylit spaces have more varied and directional luminance distributions than most artificial lighting. Estimated contribution: ~0.15.
4. **Ecological CCT channel (L4)**: The contested mechanism. If real, estimated contribution: ~0.10. If merely arousal, this weight is absorbed into the circadian and contrast channels.
5. **Dynamic variation channel (L5)**: The preliminary mechanism. Estimated contribution: ~0.15.

**de Kort**: These weights sum to 1.0, which implies additivity. L3's CENTRAL CLAIM is super-additivity — the convergent total exceeds the sum. The super-additive bonus is estimated at 15–25% — meaning the full-daylight effect is approximately 15–25% larger than would be predicted by adding the individual channels. This is consistent with the convergence principle in VIEW1 and MAT4 but has not been directly measured.

**Panel consensus**: These channel weights are VERY PRELIMINARY. They should be published as expert estimates, not empirical values. The channel-separation experiment remains the highest priority for L-III.

## Panel Synthesis: What L-II Accomplished

### Opening Remarks from the Chair

**Foster**: L-II was charged with calibrating the five templates that L-I produced. Let me assess what we delivered and what remains.

### What We Delivered

**L1 is now partially calibrated.** Rockcastle's CV-of-luminance boundaries provide the first quantitative anchors for the Goldilocks zone, refined by Muckli's neural constraints, Wilkins's photosensitivity correction, and Plummer's experiential-type mapping. When Article Eater encounters a finding about luminance contrast — "occupants preferred condition A (CV = 1.2) over condition B (CV = 0.3)" — the system can now say: "This is consistent with L1: condition A falls in the aesthetic zone, condition B in the comfort zone. The preference for moderate over uniform contrast is predicted by the inverted-U." This is a qualitative-to-semi-quantitative upgrade. Full calibration awaits psychophysical dose-response experiments.

**L2 is now age-parameterized.** Figueiro's age-correction function gives Article Eater a quantitative tool for evaluating circadian findings across populations. A study reporting null circadian effects in elderly participants at 250 lux M-EDI is now correctly interpretable as sub-threshold, not as evidence against L2. This is the most practically consequential calibration this panel has produced: it immediately changes how Article Eater evaluates the large healthcare-lighting literature.

**L3 has preliminary channel weights.** The expert-estimated weights (circadian ~0.35, view ~0.25, contrast ~0.15, CCT ~0.10, dynamics ~0.15) are explicitly preliminary but give Article Eater its first basis for assessing which channels a given study tests. A study that manipulates daylight while controlling for view (e.g., diffusing glass) should be evaluated as testing ~75% of the convergent bundle, not the full 100%.

**L4 retains its ecological framing but with acknowledged dissent.** The Steidle-Veitch debate was productive but not conclusive. L4 is revised to carry dual interpretation (ecological prediction vs. arousal mediation) and a maturity rating of "supported with dissent." This is honest and serves Article Eater well: the system will flag CCT-cognition findings as relevant to L4 while noting the interpretive ambiguity.

**L5 is retained and slightly strengthened.** Chamilothori's controlled evidence for dynamic-vs-static and natural-vs-random dynamics supports L5 as an independent mechanism. But the engagement-decay experiment remains unperformed. L5 moves from "preliminary" to "supported-preliminary."

### What We Did Not Deliver

**Reinhart**: We discussed the artificial lighting design bridge (Position Statement 6) but did not produce a formal template or mapping document. The panel ran out of time. This remains the most significant gap for architectural translation: L-I's templates speak eloquently about daylight but offer limited guidance when a paper reports a study conducted entirely under artificial lighting — which is the majority of the experimental literature.

**Wilkins**: We formalized the population-sensitivity distribution for L1 but did not produce a systematic analysis of flicker, spatial-frequency discomfort, and LED-specific visual stress triggers. These are emerging issues as LED retrofit replaces fluorescent and incandescent lighting in existing buildings. The architectural community needs guidance, and Article Eater needs parameters.

**de Kort**: The L3 channel-weight estimates are expert opinion, not empirical values. The channel-separation experiment remains the most important unfilled promise in the light-template system.

## Testable Predictions and Critical Experiments

### Prediction 1: The L1 Boundary Validation

**Rockcastle**: The CV boundaries must be validated with a purpose-built experiment. Test design: 8 lighting conditions spanning CV 0.2 to 4.0 in a single space (using movable fixtures, adjustable shading, and electrochromic glass). Dependent measures: comfort ratings, aesthetic ratings, interest ratings, task performance, and physiological markers (pupil dilation, blink rate — proxies for visual effort and discomfort). The inverted-U prediction: aesthetic ratings peak at CV 0.5–1.5 and decline symmetrically at lower (boring) and higher (uncomfortable) values. Comfort ratings decline monotonically with increasing CV. The INTERSECTION of the aesthetic and comfort curves defines the task-environment optimum.

**Wilkins**: Add a replication arm with photosensitive participants (recruited via migraine screening). The prediction: the entire inverted-U shifts leftward by 30–50% for this population. If it does not shift, the population-sensitivity correction is wrong and L1 needs revision.

### Prediction 2: The Age-Corrected Circadian Threshold

**Figueiro**: The age-correction formula can be tested directly. Design: three age groups (25–35, 50–60, 70–80) exposed to the SAME architectural lighting condition (e.g., M-EDI = 350 lux at eye level, 6500K, for 3 hours in the morning). Measure circadian response (melatonin suppression, cortisol phase, actigraphy-assessed sleep quality). L2 with age correction predicts: the 25–35 group shows robust circadian response (M-EDI exceeds their threshold); the 50–60 group shows moderate response; the 70–80 group shows weak or null response (M-EDI below their age-adjusted threshold). The falsification condition: if ALL age groups respond equivalently, the lens-yellowing mechanism is compensated by neural gain adjustment and the age correction is unnecessary.

### Prediction 3: The L4 Ecological Violation

**Foster**: This is L-I's unperformed critical experiment, and we repeat it here because it remains the decisive test.

- **Congruent**: 6500K morning → 3000K evening (mimics natural daylight trajectory)
- **Incongruent**: 3000K morning → 6500K evening (reverses natural cycle)
- **Constant**: 4500K throughout (no ecological signal either way)

L4 ecological predicts: incongruent WORSE than constant (active violation > absence). Arousal-only predicts: incongruent and constant equivalent (both lack the arousal-modulation benefit of the congruent condition, but neither produces active interference). If incongruent is WORSE than constant, the ecological mechanism is supported. If they are equivalent, the arousal account suffices and L4 should be simplified.

## Panel L-III Prospectus: Priorities for a Third Light Panel

The priorities below are organized by whether they **deepen** existing template content or **broaden** coverage.

---

#### DEEPENING — Further Calibrating L1–L5

**Deepening Priority 1: L3 Channel-Separation Experiment**

The most important remaining calibration target in the entire light-template system. The full factorial design (described in de Kort's Position Statement 2 and repeated in Prediction 2 of L-I) requires purpose-built test chambers with independently controllable glazing systems. L-III should either: (a) commission this experiment, or (b) conduct a systematic review of the partial-separation evidence accumulated since L-II and attempt a meta-analytic channel-weight estimation.

**Deepening Priority 2: L5 Engagement-Decay Direct Test**

The 4-hour engagement-decay experiment (static vs. gradual-natural vs. gradual-random vs. step-change) must be conducted to either confirm L5 as independent or absorb it into L3. This is technically simpler than the L3 channel-separation experiment and could be completed with a single well-instrumented test room.

**Deepening Priority 3: L1 Cross-Cultural Validation**

Rockcastle's CV boundaries derive from Western populations in office and educational settings. Sacred spaces (Indian temples, Islamic mosques, Gothic cathedrals) routinely produce luminance distributions at CV 2.0–4.0 that occupants experience as sublime rather than uncomfortable. Cultural calibration of the L1 boundaries — particularly the aesthetic-dramatic and dramatic-discomfort transitions — is needed.

---

#### BROADENING — Extending to New Mechanisms and Populations

**Broadening Priority 1: Artificial Lighting PE Template (L6)**

Reinhart's position statement outlined the framework: a systematic mapping between artificial lighting DESIGN VARIABLES (luminaire type, beam distribution, CCT, control strategy, luminaire-surface interaction) and the existing L1–L5 mechanisms. L-III should formalize this as L6 or as an architectural-translation appendix to L1–L5.

**Broadening Priority 2: Light in Healthcare Environments**

The healthcare-lighting literature is the largest single application domain for the light templates. A dedicated application panel would calibrate L1–L5 for hospital, clinic, and care-facility contexts — where the occupant population (sick, elderly, photosensitive) differs systematically from the office-worker populations on which most calibration data are based. This connects to Broadening Priority 2 of L-I (aging/photosensitivity) and the cross-cutting Aging & Architecture panel.

**Broadening Priority 3: LED-Specific Visual Stress**

Wilkins identified that modern LED lighting introduces new visual stress vectors (high-frequency flicker, spectral spikes) that L1 does not currently address. A dedicated assessment of LED-specific luminous hazards would extend L1's discomfort boundary to the modern lighting palette.

---

### Estimated Impact of L-III

If L-III accomplishes Deepening Priorities 1–2, A4 reaches full ★★★★ (empirically calibrated convergence model and confirmed dynamic-light mechanism). Broadening Priority 1 would significantly strengthen the architectural-translation column for artificial lighting, enabling Article Eater to evaluate the majority of experimental lighting studies that use electric lighting.

## Scope Exclusions

**Foster**: The following topics were discussed but excluded from L-II scope:

**1. Light-material interaction** — How light reveals grain, texture, and color of materials. This is a joint light-material mechanism that belongs in MAT-II or a dedicated light-material panel. L1 describes the light distribution; MAT3 describes material identity integration; the interaction between them (what a material LOOKS LIKE under different lighting) is not owned by either template alone.

**2. Lighting control interfaces** — How occupants interact with lighting controls (switches, dimmers, automated systems) and how control-system design affects satisfaction. This is a human-factors / interaction-design question, not a light-perception question.

**3. Urban-scale light pollution** — How exterior artificial light at night (ALAN) affects circadian health at the building-occupant level. L2 addresses the indoor lighting environment; outdoor light pollution is an urban-planning and public-health issue with different design levers.

**4. Therapeutic lighting interventions** — Light-therapy boxes, dawn simulation, and targeted clinical interventions for seasonal affective disorder, circadian disorders, and dementia. These are clinical interventions, not architectural design features, though they use the same L2 circadian pathway.
