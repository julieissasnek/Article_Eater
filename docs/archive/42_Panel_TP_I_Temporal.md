## Panel Charge

This panel addresses **A10 Temporal Experience & Movement** (coverage ★★ Partial in Doc 33). Unlike previous panels, which addressed specific sensory domains (light, materials, spatial configuration, visual form), this panel addresses the TEMPORAL DIMENSION that crosscuts all domains. Architecture is not experienced instantaneously — it is experienced over time, through movement, across daily and seasonal cycles, and through the long duration of a building's life. The temporal dimension is where the individual sensory templates become an integrated experience.

Multiple previous panels have already formalized temporal mechanisms within their domains: L2 (circadian architecture, hours-to-days), L5 (dynamic light, minutes-to-hours), MAT2 (seasonal thermal adaptation, weeks-to-months), SC3 (architectural promenade, seconds-to-minutes), VF3 (processing-style shifts at spatial transitions, seconds). What is NOT formalized: (1) **proprioceptive-motor prediction** — how the body predicts and responds to architectural surfaces and spatial demands during movement, (2) **the doorway effect** — how architectural thresholds create episodic boundaries in memory, (3) **aging and patina** — how buildings change over years and decades and how this temporal depth affects experience, and (4) **the temporal hierarchy** — the systematic integration of PE events across timescales from milliseconds to decades.

The panel is charged with producing 3–4 templates covering: (a) motor prediction and proprioceptive PE during architectural movement, (b) the threshold as episodic boundary (the doorway effect in architectural design), (c) material aging and temporal depth, and (d) a temporal hierarchy framework that systematizes the timescale structure already present across all templates. These must connect to SC3 (which handles the promenade compositional structure), L2/L5 (circadian and dynamic light), MAT2 (seasonal thermal), and T8 (affordance/postural readiness).

## Panel Composition

### Motor Cognition and Proprioception

**1. Daniel Wolpert** (Columbia University, Zuckerman Mind Brain Behavior Institute; formerly Cambridge University)
The leading researcher on motor prediction — the brain's use of forward models to predict the sensory consequences of its own movements before they occur (Wolpert, Ghahramani, & Jordan, 1995, ~2,500 citations; Wolpert & Flanagan, 2001, ~1,500 citations). His central claim: the brain exists primarily to MOVE, and nearly all of neural computation — including perception, learning, and decision-making — is organized around the prediction and control of movement. The "forward model" in his framework IS a prediction: the motor system predicts what it will see, hear, feel, and balance in the immediate future, and the discrepancy between prediction and actual sensory feedback (sensory prediction error) drives motor learning and adaptation.
*Role*: Establishes the motor prediction framework for architecture. Every step on a staircase, every hand on a door handle, every shift of weight in a chair involves a motor prediction: the brain predicts the forces required, the sensory feedback expected, and the postural adjustments needed. When architecture matches these predictions (standard step height, expected door weight, familiar surface compliance), the movement is fluent and unconscious. When architecture violates them (an unexpected step height, a door heavier than predicted, a surface more slippery than expected), the motor prediction error is immediate, visceral, and sometimes dangerous.

**2. Mark Latash** (Pennsylvania State University, Department of Kinesiology)
Research on the neural control of posture and movement, particularly the concept of "synergies" — coordinated patterns of muscle activation that the motor system uses to maintain stability. His work on postural sway during quiet standing demonstrates that the body is NEVER static — it continuously oscillates around an equilibrium, making thousands of micro-adjustments per minute based on proprioceptive feedback. Standing in a building is an active motor task, not a passive state.
*Role*: Provides the postural dimension. Standing in a room with a slightly tilted floor (Eisenman's Holocaust Memorial, Libeskind's Jewish Museum) produces continuous postural PE — the vestibular and proprioceptive systems detect the deviation from level and the motor system must continuously correct. This sustained postural PE is the bodily basis of the "unease" that occupants report in deliberately tilted spaces. Conversely, standing on a perfectly level floor produces minimal postural PE — the body's motor prediction (the floor is level) is continuously confirmed.

**3. William Warren** (Brown University, Department of Cognitive, Linguistic, and Psychological Sciences)
Research on visually guided locomotion — how the visual system controls walking, including the perception of affordances for passage (doorway width relative to body width), step height for stair climbing, and surface properties for gait adjustment (Warren, 1984, ~1,000 citations; Warren & Fajen, 2004, ~500 citations). His work establishes the body-scaled dimensions of architectural affordances: a doorway must be at least 1.3 times shoulder width for easy passage; a step must be within 0.88 × leg length for comfortable climbing; a slope must be below ~30° for comfortable walking.
*Role*: Provides the ecological-affordance parameters for movement through architecture. These body-scaled thresholds define when motor prediction is easy (within the affordance range) and when it becomes effortful (approaching or exceeding the limits). Architecture that stays well within the affordance ranges produces fluent, unconscious movement; architecture that pushes the limits (narrow passages, steep stairs, uneven floors) demands conscious motor control and produces proprioceptive PE.

### Memory, Context, and the Doorway Effect

**4. Gabriel Radvansky** (University of Notre Dame, Department of Psychology)
Discoverer of the "doorway effect" — the phenomenon that walking through a doorway causes a measurable decrement in memory for the preceding room's contents (Radvansky & Copeland, 2006, ~400 citations; Radvansky, Krawietz, & Tamplin, 2011, ~300 citations). His event segmentation framework proposes that the brain partitions continuous experience into discrete episodes at boundary events — and spatial transitions (doorways, corridors, building entries) are powerful episodic boundary markers. When you walk through a doorway, the hippocampus updates its context representation (T23 context memory), partially clearing the working memory of the previous context.
*Role*: Provides the memory mechanism for architectural thresholds. The doorway effect demonstrates that spatial boundaries are not merely perceptual — they are COGNITIVE, affecting how experience is organized in memory. Architecture that designs thresholds deliberately (a vestibule, a narthex, a processional entry) creates strong episodic boundaries that segment the visitor's experience into discrete chapters. Architecture that minimizes thresholds (open plan, continuous flowing space) reduces episodic segmentation, producing a more continuous but potentially less memorable experience.

**5. Jeffrey Zacks** (Washington University in St. Louis, Department of Psychological and Brain Sciences)
Developer of Event Segmentation Theory (EST) — the framework that describes how the brain automatically partitions continuous sensory input into discrete events at points of maximum change (Zacks, Speer, Swallow, Braver, & Reynolds, 2007, ~1,500 citations). His work shows that event boundaries are detected automatically, that they coincide with increases in prediction error across multiple modalities, and that memory encoding is enhanced at event boundaries. Applied to architecture: every spatial transition (entering a new room, crossing a threshold, turning a corner) is a potential event boundary.
*Role*: Provides the general theory of temporal segmentation into which the doorway effect fits. EST predicts that architectural transitions produce event boundaries WHEN they involve sufficient multi-modal change — a change in room alone may not be enough; a change in room + light + acoustics + material produces a stronger boundary. This connects directly to SC3's multi-modal threshold: the strongest thresholds (those with the most modality changes simultaneously) produce the strongest episodic boundaries and the most memorable experiences.

### Material Aging and Temporal Depth

**6. Stewart Brand** (Long Now Foundation; author, *How Buildings Learn*)
Author of the most influential book on how buildings change over time (Brand, 1994, ~2,000 citations). His "shearing layers" model decomposes a building into temporal strata with different rates of change: Site (permanent), Structure (30–300 years), Skin (20 years), Services (7–15 years), Space Plan (3–30 years), Stuff (daily). His key insight: buildings that accommodate temporal change at each layer (adaptable plans, replaceable systems, accessible structure) age gracefully; buildings that resist change (monolithic construction, integrated systems, inflexible plans) age catastrophically. The building is not a static object — it is a temporal process.
*Role*: Provides the macro-temporal framework for architectural experience. A building that is decades old carries temporal information in its materials (patina, wear, modification scars), its spatial organization (adapted layouts, added rooms, blocked-off passages), and its cultural associations (historical events, community memory). This temporal depth is perceptually available: you can SEE that the stone is old, that the floor is worn, that the layout has been modified. Temporal depth enriches architectural experience by providing episodic associations (T23), fractal complexity from weathering (T1), and cultural-material conditioning (MAT5).

**7. Mohsen Mostafavi** (Harvard Graduate School of Design; co-author, *On Weathering: The Life of Buildings in Time*)
Architect and theorist whose work on weathering explores how materials change over time and how this change can be designed for rather than merely endured (Mostafavi & Leatherbarrow, 1993, ~300 citations). His argument: modern architecture treats weathering as decay (a problem to be prevented through coatings, sealants, and replacement), while traditional architecture treated weathering as maturation (a process that enriches and deepens the building's character). The Japanese concept of *wabi-sabi* — beauty in imperfection and transience — is the aesthetic of positive temporal change.
*Role*: Provides the design perspective on material aging. Some materials age beautifully (copper patinas to green, oak darkens and develops grain, stone grows moss and lichen, brick mellows) while others age poorly (unpainted steel rusts destructively, concrete stains and cracks, plastic yellows and degrades). The distinction is partially about fractal development: natural weathering processes tend to increase surface fractal complexity (T1), moving the material toward the D ≈ 1.3 optimum. Artificial degradation (staining, cracking, yellowing) does not follow fractal statistics and produces visual disorder rather than visual richness.

### Temporal Psychology and Rhythm

**8. Hugo Spiers** (University College London, Department of Experimental Psychology)
Research on navigation, spatial memory, and real-world neuroscience using neuroimaging in naturalistic settings. His work on London taxi drivers (continuing Maguire's foundational studies) shows that hippocampal activity peaks at decision points during navigation and that the hippocampus tracks spatial goals, route progress, and proximity to destination (Spiers & Maguire, 2007, ~500 citations). His recent work uses head-mounted filming and retrospective fMRI to capture the neural dynamics of real-world navigation moment by moment.
*Role*: Provides the real-time neural dynamics of navigation. His method — capturing the moment-by-moment hippocampal activity during real-world architectural experience — provides the temporal resolution needed to understand how the brain processes spatial transitions in real time. His finding that hippocampal activity peaks at decision points directly supports SC1's prediction about navigational PE at choice points, and extends it to the temporal dimension: the PE peaks are temporally structured by the spatial sequence.

**9. Marc Wittmann** (Institute for Frontier Areas of Psychology and Mental Health, Freiburg)
Research on time perception — how the brain constructs the subjective experience of time passing (Wittmann, 2009, ~300 citations; Wittmann, 2016, ~200 citations). His work on embodied time perception proposes that the experience of time is rooted in bodily interoception: when the body is in an aroused, attentive state, time seems to slow down (more events are encoded per unit time); when the body is in a calm, habituated state, time seems to speed up (fewer events are encoded). This maps directly onto PE: high PE events (arousal, surprise, spatial transitions) are encoded as temporally "long" moments; low PE intervals (habituation, familiar environments) are encoded as temporally "compressed."
*Role*: Provides the subjective-time dimension that architecture manipulates. A visitor who moves through a rich architectural promenade (SC3) with many high-PE transitions remembers the experience as temporally long (many events were encoded). A visitor who moves through a monotonous corridor (low PE, few transitions) remembers it as temporally compressed. The architect does not merely compose spatial experience — the architect composes TEMPORAL experience, determining how long the building feels to move through regardless of physical distance or clock time.

## Position Statements

### MOTOR PREDICTION AND ARCHITECTURAL MOVEMENT

**Position Statement 1: Daniel Wolpert — The Brain Exists to Move, and Architecture Is What It Moves Through**

Every sensory experience in a building is preceded by and embedded in a motor act. You walk through a doorway, climb a staircase, push open a door, settle into a chair, lean against a railing. The motor system generates predictions for each of these actions: the forces required, the sensory feedback expected, the timing of contact. When the building matches the prediction (standard step height of 178mm ±6mm, door weight of 15–20N, floor surface friction μ > 0.5), the action is fluent — the motor system operates below conscious awareness and the person attends to visual, social, or cognitive content. When the building violates the prediction, the motor prediction error is immediate and visceral: the stumble on an unexpected step, the startle from a door that swings faster than expected, the instability on a slippery floor.

Motor PE is qualitatively different from visual or auditory PE. Visual PE involves updating a perceptual model. Motor PE involves a physical recovery action — a postural correction, a grip adjustment, a balance response. The stakes are higher: a visual prediction error is interesting; a motor prediction error can cause a fall. This is why building codes specify step heights, railing heights, and floor friction: they enforce the motor predictions that the body relies on.

But motor prediction is not only about code compliance. The QUALITY of architectural movement — the gait rhythm on stairs, the hand-railing interaction, the postural dance of sitting and rising — is a continuous proprioceptive experience that contributes to the overall quality of the building. A beautifully proportioned staircase (each step matching the motor prediction precisely, the railing at exactly the right height, the landing providing a natural gait-reset point) produces a FLOW of motor prediction confirmation that is experienced as physical grace. A poorly proportioned staircase (steps slightly too high or too deep, railing slightly out of reach, no landing at the gait-fatigue point) produces a stream of minor motor PEs that is experienced as physical awkwardness — even when no one stumbles.

**Position Statement 2: Mark Latash — Postural PE and the Feel of Being in a Space**

The body is never still. Standing in a room, the center of mass oscillates within a small envelope (typically ±1–2 cm), and this oscillation is actively controlled by the postural synergy system — coordinated patterns of ankle, hip, and trunk muscle activation that maintain balance (Latash, 2008, ~500 citations). The postural system maintains a prediction of vertical: the vestibular system provides the gravitational reference, and the proprioceptive system maintains the body's alignment with it.

Architecture affects postural prediction through three channels. First, **floor level and surface compliance**: a level, firm floor produces minimal postural PE. A floor with a slight slope (as in a raked auditorium) produces continuous, low-level postural PE that is normally unconscious but becomes conscious in extreme cases. A soft, compliant floor (thick carpet, suspended wood) produces postural PE because the surface yields under load — the body predicted a rigid support and received a compliant one. Second, **visual vertical**: when the walls, columns, and edges of a room are truly vertical, the visual system confirms the vestibular vertical and postural PE is zero. When visual verticals are tilted (as in Eisenman's or Libeskind's deliberately non-orthogonal buildings), the visual system provides a vertical reference that conflicts with the vestibular reference — a sustained visual-vestibular conflict that produces mild postural instability and the subjective feeling of "something is wrong with this space." Third, **moving visual field**: large-scale visual motion (swaying trees visible through windows, dynamic projections on walls, crowds flowing through an atrium) produces optokinetic postural responses — the body sways in the direction of visual flow, requiring postural correction.

The postural system thus provides a continuous background signal about how "stable" or "unstable" the architectural environment is. This signal feeds into the interoceptive system (T12) — the bodily sense of physical security or insecurity — and contributes to the emotional quality of the space. The secure, grounded feeling of a traditional stone building with level floors and true verticals is partly postural: the body's predictions are continuously confirmed. The unsettling quality of a deconstructivist building with tilted walls and sloping floors is partly postural: the body's predictions are continuously challenged.

**Position Statement 3: William Warren — Affordance Thresholds and the Body-Scaled Architecture**

Architecture is designed for bodies of a specific size. Doorways, stairs, corridors, ceiling heights, seat heights, counter heights — all are dimensioned relative to the human body. The ecological psychology framework specifies these relationships as body-scaled affordances: the usability of an architectural element is determined by the ratio of the element's dimensions to the user's body dimensions (Warren, 1984, ~1,000 citations).

Step height is the most precisely studied: the optimal step height is approximately 0.25 × leg length, and the maximum comfortable step height is approximately 0.88 × leg length. Doorway passage width: comfortable at ≥ 1.3 × shoulder width. Corridor width for two-person passing: ≥ 2.6 × shoulder width. Ramp slope for comfortable walking: ≤ 1:12 (8%). Each of these thresholds defines a motor prediction boundary: below the threshold, the motor system predicts successful, fluent action. At the threshold, the prediction becomes uncertain. Above the threshold, the motor system predicts failure or discomfort.

The PE relevance: architectural elements that fall well within the affordance range produce zero motor PE — the prediction is so strong that the motor system does not even generate conscious motor planning (you walk through a normal doorway without thinking about it). Elements at the affordance boundary produce moderate motor PE — the motor system generates conscious planning (you notice the doorway is narrow and adjust your posture). Elements beyond the affordance boundary produce high motor PE — the motor system predicts failure and generates avoidance or caution (you see a staircase with no railing and proceed cautiously). Architecture that consistently operates within affordance ranges produces a physically fluent, unconscious movement experience. Architecture that deliberately pushes affordance boundaries (narrow passages, steep stairs, unexpected level changes) produces a physically conscious, effortful movement experience that can be thrilling (a dramatic mountain staircase) or distressing (a confusing emergency exit) depending on context and agency.

### MEMORY, THRESHOLDS, AND EPISODIC SEGMENTATION

**Position Statement 4: Gabriel Radvansky — The Doorway Effect as Architectural Episodic Boundary**

Walking through a doorway is not just a spatial transition — it is a MEMORY event. The doorway effect demonstrates that crossing a spatial boundary causes a measurable disruption in working memory for the contents of the preceding room (Radvansky & Copeland, 2006, ~400 citations; Radvansky et al., 2011, ~300 citations). The mechanism: the hippocampus maintains a "situation model" — an active representation of the current context, including the objects, people, and goals present. When the spatial context changes (new room, new visual environment, new acoustic environment), the hippocampus updates the situation model, which partially clears the working memory of the previous context's contents.

The architectural implication is that THRESHOLDS ARE MEMORY DEVICES. A well-designed threshold (a vestibule between rooms, a narthex before the nave, a courtyard between building wings) creates a strong episodic boundary that segments the visitor's experience into discrete, memorable episodes. A poorly designed "threshold" (a door in a wall between otherwise identical rooms) creates a weak episodic boundary because the context change is minimal. No threshold at all (open-plan continuous space) creates no episodic boundaries, producing a continuous but less structured — and potentially less memorable — experience.

The critical variable is the MAGNITUDE of context change at the boundary. A doorway between two similar rooms (same size, same light, same material) produces a weak doorway effect. A threshold between dramatically different spaces (dark narrow corridor → bright tall atrium, stone → wood, reverberant → damped) produces a strong doorway effect because the hippocampal context update is comprehensive — everything in the situation model changes. This is exactly what SC3 describes as a multi-modal threshold: the more modalities that change, the stronger the episodic boundary, and the more memorable the transition.

**Position Statement 5: Jeffrey Zacks — Event Segmentation and Architectural Time**

Event Segmentation Theory (EST) proposes that the brain continuously segments experience into events at points of maximal change — moments when the prediction error across all input channels peaks simultaneously (Zacks et al., 2007, ~1,500 citations; Zacks & Swallow, 2007, ~600 citations). These event boundaries are detected automatically, without conscious effort, and they have three consequences: (a) working memory is partially reset (Radvansky's doorway effect), (b) memory encoding is ENHANCED at boundaries (the transition itself is remembered better than the steady-state periods between transitions), and (c) the subjective experience of time is structured — events feel like discrete units with beginnings and endings.

For architecture: every spatial transition is a potential event boundary. BUT the strength of the boundary depends on the magnitude of the multi-modal change. EST predicts that architectural experience is segmented into discrete episodes at strong spatial transitions (entering a new room that differs in space, light, material, sound) and experienced as continuous during steady-state occupancy of a single space. The TEMPORAL STRUCTURE of architectural experience — how it feels to move through a building over time — is determined by the DENSITY and STRENGTH of event boundaries along the movement path.

This connects directly to SC3 (promenade) but adds the MEMORY dimension: the promenade is not only an emotional trajectory (SC3's PE orchestration) but a MEMORY trajectory. The moments that the visitor will REMEMBER are the event boundaries — the thresholds, the spatial transitions, the moments of revelation. The steady-state periods between transitions (walking down a long corridor, sitting in a room) are temporally compressed in memory. The architect who designs strong thresholds at key moments produces a building that is remembered as a sequence of vivid episodes. The architect who designs no thresholds produces a building that is remembered as a vague, undifferentiated blur.

This also explains Bermudez's EAE data (from SC-I): the most-cited extraordinary architectural experiences are all EVENT BOUNDARIES — the moment of entering the Pantheon, the moment of emerging onto the terrace, the moment of seeing the light through the oculus. These are transitions, not steady states. Architecture's most powerful moments are temporal inflection points where prediction error peaks.

### AGING, PATINA, AND TEMPORAL DEPTH

**Position Statement 6: Stewart Brand — Buildings Are Temporal Processes**

A building is not an object. It is a PROCESS — a series of changes happening at different rates in different layers (Brand, 1994, ~2,000 citations). The site is essentially permanent. The structure changes over centuries. The skin is replaced every generation. The services are updated every decade. The space plan shifts every few years. The stuff (furniture, objects) changes daily. When a building's layers can change independently (adaptable design), the building evolves gracefully — each layer adapts to changing needs while the other layers provide continuity. When the layers are fused (the services run through the structure, the skin is the structure), change at one layer disrupts all the others, and the building resists adaptation until it is demolished.

For the PE framework: temporal depth is perceptually available. An occupant of a building reads temporal information from material wear (smooth stone where thousands of hands have touched, worn stair treads, sun-faded paint), from spatial adaptation (a wall added here, a doorway widened there, a window blocked off and bricked up), and from cultural layering (a medieval structure with Georgian windows and Victorian plumbing and 21st-century lighting). Each of these temporal signals activates different mechanisms: material wear engages T1 (increased fractal complexity from natural processes) and MAT5 (cultural associations with age, continuity, permanence). Spatial adaptation engages T3 (cognitive map complexity — the building has an "illogical" plan because it has been modified) and SC1 (low intelligibility from historical modifications). Cultural layering engages the knowledge-meaning system (Chatterjee's aesthetic triad) — recognizing that a building has survived and adapted through centuries produces a distinctive emotional response (reverence, rootedness, temporal humility).

**Position Statement 7: Mohsen Mostafavi — Designing for Temporal Change**

The architectural tradition of working WITH time rather than against it goes back millennia. Japanese shrines at Ise are rebuilt every 20 years in an identical form — the BUILDING ages but the DESIGN is renewed. European cathedrals were built over centuries by generations of masons, each generation adapting the design while maintaining the structural and liturgical logic. Islamic gardens are designed with plant species that will mature over decades, transforming the garden experience on a generational timescale. In each case, temporal change is not a problem — it is the MEDIUM.

Modern architecture has largely abandoned this temporal orientation. The dominant aesthetic is the "finished" building — clean, pristine, as-designed. Weathering is a defect to be prevented. Adaptation is a betrayal of the original design intent. The result is buildings that look best on the day they are completed and progressively worse thereafter — the opposite of traditional buildings, which look adequate when new and magnificent when old.

For the PE framework: a building that improves with age provides a continuously positive temporal PE trajectory. Each visit reveals new details that weathering has produced — a new color in the oxidized copper, a new texture in the worn stone, a new spatial quality from a recent adaptation. A building that degrades with age provides a continuously negative temporal PE trajectory — each visit reveals new damage, new staining, new dysfunction. The material template MAT4 (natural material convergence) suggests WHY natural materials age positively: their weathering processes are fractal (T1), producing increasing visual complexity over time, while synthetic materials' degradation processes are often non-fractal (uniform yellowing, random cracking, systematic staining), producing decreasing visual quality.

### TEMPORAL PSYCHOLOGY

**Position Statement 8: Hugo Spiers — Real-Time Neural Dynamics of Architectural Navigation**

Navigation through architecture produces a characteristic temporal signature in hippocampal and prefrontal activity. Using retrospective fMRI with video recordings of real-world navigation, we have shown that hippocampal activity peaks at decision points and at moments of spatial reorientation — exactly the locations where SC1 predicts maximum navigational PE (Spiers & Maguire, 2007, ~500 citations). Prefrontal activity peaks when route plans are generated or updated. The temporal profile is punctuated, not continuous: long stretches of low activity (the navigator is on a known route, predictions are confirmed) interrupted by sharp peaks (decision points, unexpected spatial features, reorientation events).

This temporal signature maps onto the promenade framework (SC3) at the neural level: the promenade is experienced as a sequence of hippocampal-prefrontal peaks (at thresholds and transitions) separated by relatively quiescent intervals (within steady-state spaces). The density and magnitude of peaks constitute the neural "score" of the architectural experience.

**Position Statement 9: Marc Wittmann — Architecture Shapes Subjective Time**

Time perception is not a clock reading. It is a construction based on the density of encoded events (Wittmann, 2016, ~200 citations; Eagleman, 2008, ~600 citations). When many events are encoded per unit time (high arousal, novel environment, frequent PE events), subjective time EXPANDS — the experience feels long. When few events are encoded (low arousal, familiar environment, steady-state), subjective time CONTRACTS — the experience feels short. This "time dilation" effect has been documented in controlled experiments and explains why novel experiences feel temporally rich while routine experiences feel temporally compressed.

Architecture manipulates subjective time through PE density. A building with many spatial transitions, material changes, light shifts, and acoustic events (a complex museum, a winding medieval town) produces high event-encoding density → expanded subjective time → the building feels "large" and "timeful" even if physically compact. A building with few transitions (a long uniform corridor, an undifferentiated open-plan office) produces low event-encoding density → contracted subjective time → the building feels "quick" and "forgettable" even if physically large.

This time-perception effect interacts with memory: experiences that felt temporally long during experience are also recalled as temporally rich — "we spent hours in that building, it felt like a whole day" versus "we were there for hours but I can barely remember it." The architect who designs PE-rich environments produces buildings that are both experientially and retrospectively temporally expansive. The architect who designs PE-poor environments produces buildings that vanish from both experience and memory.

## Cross-Examination and Debate

### Debate 1: Is Motor PE Architecturally Important or Just a Code Compliance Issue?

**Challenge**: Building codes already specify step heights, railing heights, and floor friction. Does the motor PE template add anything beyond what codes require?

**Wolpert**: Codes specify SAFETY minimums — the thresholds above which falls and injuries occur. Motor PE operates over the entire range of architectural movement, not just at the safety boundary. A staircase can be code-compliant (safe) and still produce a continuous stream of minor motor PEs that makes climbing feel awkward and exhausting. The PE framework adds the QUALITY dimension that codes do not capture: not just "will people fall?" but "will people move beautifully?"

**Warren**: The affordance threshold research provides body-scaled parameters that are more specific than code requirements. Codes specify a maximum step height of ~210mm. The optimal step height for gait fluency is 170–180mm (for an average adult). The difference between "compliant" and "optimal" is the difference between absence of danger and presence of motor grace. The PE template parameterizes this quality dimension.

**Latash**: And the postural PE dimension is entirely outside codes. No building code addresses visual-vertical conflict, floor compliance, or the sustained postural effects of non-orthogonal geometry. Yet these affect the bodily experience of every occupant. The Eisenman/Libeskind case demonstrates that deliberate postural PE can be architecturally powerful — but it needs to be recognized as a design variable, not an accident.

**Panel consensus**: **Motor PE adds a quality dimension that codes do not capture.** Codes ensure safety; motor PE templates parameterize physical grace. The template should note the distinction between code compliance (binary: safe/unsafe) and motor prediction confirmation (continuous: awkward → fluent → graceful).

### Debate 2: Does the Doorway Effect Mean Open Plans Are Better for Memory?

**Challenge**: If doorways cause memory disruption (Radvansky), wouldn't open-plan environments (no doorways, no episodic boundaries) preserve memory better?

**Radvansky**: No — this confuses working memory disruption at a SINGLE boundary with overall memory STRUCTURE. The doorway effect disrupts the active contents of working memory at the boundary, but it simultaneously ENHANCES encoding of the transition itself. Event boundaries are remembered better than steady states. A building with strong thresholds produces a richly structured memory: a sequence of vivid episodes separated by clear boundaries. An open-plan building produces an unstructured memory: a continuous, undifferentiated blur.

**Zacks**: The analogy to text is instructive. A document with no paragraph breaks or section headers is harder to remember than one with clear structure, even though the paragraph breaks themselves cause a tiny disruption in reading flow. The episodic boundaries that doorways create are like paragraph breaks in experience — they organize memory into retrievable units.

**Brand**: And over longer timescales, the spatial boundaries that thresholds create enable ADAPTATION — rooms can be repurposed independently when they are separated by clear boundaries. An open plan that resists episodic segmentation also resists functional adaptation. The temporal and spatial dimensions converge: good thresholds serve both immediate memory and long-term adaptability.

**Panel consensus**: **The doorway effect does NOT argue against thresholds — it argues FOR designing thresholds deliberately.** Strong thresholds create strong episodic boundaries → better-organized, more vivid memory → more memorable buildings. The key design insight is that the MAGNITUDE of context change at the threshold determines boundary strength. A doorway between identical rooms creates a weak boundary. A transition between dramatically different spaces creates a strong boundary. The designer controls memory structure by controlling threshold magnitude.

## Synthesis: The Temporal Constitution of Architectural Experience

The four templates produced by this panel reveal that time is not a secondary dimension of architecture — it is the dimension in which all other architectural properties become experience. Space, light, material, and sound are perceived instantaneously at any given moment, but they are EXPERIENCED over time, through movement, across daily and seasonal cycles, and through the long duration of a building's life.

**TP1 (Motor Prediction)** establishes that the body is the first temporal instrument: every step, every grasp, every postural adjustment is a prediction-and-test cycle operating at the T-2 timescale. Architecture that respects motor predictions produces physical fluency — the body moves unconsciously and attention is free for engagement with the building's visual, social, and cognitive content. Architecture that violates motor predictions demands conscious bodily attention, which may serve an architectural purpose (Eisenman's deliberate disorientation, Ando's steep stairs as spiritual preparation) or may simply indicate bad design (an unexpected step, a door that sticks). The distinction between deliberate and accidental motor PE is the distinction between architecture and building failure.

**TP2 (Episodic Boundary)** establishes that architectural thresholds are simultaneously spatial events and memory events. The doorway effect demonstrates that spatial boundaries trigger hippocampal context updating, segmenting continuous experience into discrete episodes. The strength of the episodic boundary depends on the magnitude of multi-modal context change at the threshold — the same variable that SC3 identifies as the determinant of emotional impact. This convergence is significant: the moments of greatest emotional impact (SC3) are also the moments of greatest memorial salience (TP2). Architecture's most powerful moments are simultaneously its most memorable moments. The promenade is both an emotional score and a memorial structure.

**TP3 (Temporal Depth)** establishes that buildings carry time in their surfaces. Natural weathering processes tend to increase fractal complexity (T1), moving material surfaces toward the perceptual optimum over decades and centuries. This is why old buildings often possess a visual richness that new buildings lack — their surfaces have been sculpted by time into fractal patterns that the perceptual system finds rewarding. Brand's shearing-layer model adds the spatial dimension: buildings that accommodate change develop an accumulated spatial complexity that carries narrative information about human adaptation. The emotional response to temporal depth — reverence, rootedness, connection to continuity — is architecture's equivalent of the deep time that geologists read in rock strata.

**TP4 (Temporal Hierarchy)** provides the integrative framework: architectural experience is constituted by PE events spanning seven orders of temporal magnitude, from millisecond perceptual processing to centennial material aging. The quality of an architectural experience depends on PE management at each timescale and on the coherence of PE across timescales. This hierarchy reveals why certain buildings produce profound experiences: they succeed at EVERY timescale simultaneously. A great cathedral produces rewarding contour PE (T-1), motor grace in its processional dimensions (T-2), powerful episodic boundaries at its threshold sequence (T-2/T-3), a composed promenade through its spatial sequence (T-3), dynamic light that changes through the day and seasons (T-4/T-6), and material surfaces enriched by centuries of weathering (T-7). The full hierarchy operates in concert.

The relationship between this panel and SC-I is particularly important. SC3 (the promenade template) describes the multi-modal PE trajectory of movement through architecture — the emotional composition. TP-I adds three dimensions that SC3 does not address: the motor-proprioceptive channel (TP1), the memorial structure (TP2), and the temporal hierarchy that situates the promenade within the full range of architectural timescales (TP4). Together, SC3 and the TP templates provide a comprehensive account of how architecture orchestrates experience over time.

## Testable Predictions

### Prediction 1: Motor PE Quality Correlates with Movement Satisfaction

**Hypothesis**: Occupants will report higher satisfaction with movement quality in buildings where step heights, railing heights, door weights, and floor surfaces are within the body-scaled optimal range (Warren, 1984), compared to buildings that are code-compliant but outside the optimal range.

**Critical experiment**: Select 10 staircases that vary in step height from 150mm (below optimal) through 178mm (optimal) to 210mm (code maximum). Measure gait kinematics (stride regularity, vertical acceleration variability, grip force on railing) and subjective ratings of movement quality. Predict: inverted-U relationship with peak at 0.25 × average leg length, and a continuous motor PE metric from gait kinematics that correlates with subjective ratings.

**Templates tested**: TP1.

### Prediction 2: Threshold Multi-Modal Magnitude Predicts Memory Structure

**Hypothesis**: Participants who walk through a sequence of spaces with strong multi-modal thresholds (4+ simultaneous channel changes) will recall the sequence as more vivid, more temporally extended, and better segmented into discrete episodes than participants who walk through a sequence with weak thresholds (1–2 channel changes), even when the spaces themselves are equated for visual interest.

**Critical experiment**: Construct two versions of the same spatial sequence (using partitions, lighting, materials, and acoustic treatment in a laboratory setting). Version A: dramatic thresholds (light, material, acoustic, and spatial changes at each boundary). Version B: minimal thresholds (same spaces, uniform transitions). Test free recall, cued recall, temporal duration estimation, and subjective vividness. Predict: Version A produces richer episodic structure, longer estimated duration, and higher vividness.

**Templates tested**: TP2, SC3, and Wittmann's subjective-time hypothesis.

### Prediction 3: Natural Material Aging Increases Fractal Dimension Toward Optimum

**Hypothesis**: Natural building materials (stone, wood, brick, copper) measured at 0, 10, 25, 50, and 100+ years of age will show a systematic increase in surface fractal dimension, with the rate and trajectory depending on material type, converging toward D ≈ 1.3.

**Critical experiment**: Measure surface fractal dimension (using box-counting method on high-resolution photographs) of matched samples of stone, wood, brick, and copper at varying ages. Sample from buildings of known construction dates. Control for maintenance regime, climate exposure, and orientation. Predict: monotonic increase in D for natural materials; no systematic increase (or decrease) for synthetic controls (vinyl, composite panel).

**Templates tested**: TP3, T1.

### Prediction 4: Subjective Building Duration Correlates with PE Event Density

**Hypothesis**: Visitors to buildings with high PE event density (many spatial transitions, material changes, light shifts per unit walking distance) will estimate their visit as longer in duration than visitors to buildings with low PE event density, controlling for actual clock time and walking distance.

**Critical experiment**: Select pairs of buildings matched for total path length but differing in PE event density (e.g., a winding museum with many room transitions vs. a long gallery with few). Fit visitors with eye-tracking and movement sensors to measure PE events (fixation changes, gait adjustments, spatial transitions). After the visit, collect prospective time estimates, retrospective time estimates, and number-of-events estimates. Predict: PE event density correlates with retrospective duration estimation (Wittmann, 2016) and with number-of-events recalled.

**Templates tested**: TP4, TP2.

### Prediction 5: Cross-Timescale Coherence Predicts Overall Architectural Satisfaction

**Hypothesis**: Buildings that score well on PE management at multiple timescales simultaneously (motor fluency at T-2, good threshold design at T-3, appropriate circadian light at T-5, positive material aging at T-7) will receive higher overall architectural quality ratings than buildings that score well at some timescales but poorly at others.

**Critical experiment**: Rate 20 well-known buildings on TP4's seven timescale dimensions using expert panels and objective measures where possible. Correlate multi-timescale PE profiles with published architectural quality assessments, occupant satisfaction surveys, and Bermudez-type extraordinary-experience ratings. Predict: no single timescale is sufficient to predict overall quality; a multi-timescale composite is a better predictor than any individual timescale score.

**Templates tested**: TP4.

## Panel TP-II Prospectus: Priorities for a Follow-Up Temporal Panel

### Priority 1: Empirical Calibration of the Doorway Effect Gradient

TP2 predicts that episodic boundary strength varies continuously with the magnitude of multi-modal context change at the threshold. This prediction needs experimental testing with graded stimuli: thresholds involving 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5+ simultaneous channel changes, with memory structure, subjective vividness, and temporal estimation as dependent measures. A VR-based experiment could systematically manipulate individual channels while controlling others, providing the parametric data that TP2 currently lacks.

### Priority 2: Fractal Dimension Tracking of Aged Building Surfaces

TP3's central claim — that natural weathering increases surface fractal dimension toward the D ≈ 1.3 optimum — requires measurement. A cross-sectional study of building surfaces at varying ages, controlling for material type, climate exposure, and maintenance regime, would provide the first empirical test. If confirmed, this would connect the temporal dimension to the most quantitatively specific mechanism in the template inventory (T1).

### Priority 3: Motor PE Instrumentation

TP1 needs a validated measure of continuous motor PE quality during architectural movement. Wearable inertial measurement units (IMUs) can capture gait kinematics with high temporal resolution; the question is whether kinematic variability maps onto subjective movement quality in a way that provides a design-relevant metric. A study correlating IMU-derived gait metrics with subjective movement quality ratings across a range of staircases, corridors, and ramp conditions would provide this.

### Priority 4: Real-Time Multi-Timescale PE Measurement

TP4 claims that architectural experience integrates PE across multiple timescales simultaneously. Testing this requires measuring PE markers at different timescales during a single building visit — physiological measures (EDA, heart rate variability for T-2/T-3), eye-tracking (for T-1), movement sensors (for T-2), and circadian markers (cortisol, melatonin for T-5) — and demonstrating that they contribute independently to overall experience ratings. This is methodologically ambitious but within reach of current ambulatory neuroscience methods (Spiers's real-world fMRI paradigm, Ellard's psychophysiological measurement approach).

### Priority 5: Circadian Architecture Design Parameters

A10.4 remains at ★★★ rather than ★★★★ because the circadian mechanisms are formalized (L2) but the design parameters for circadian architecture — what spectral content, at what intensity, through what architectural means, at what time of day — require dose-response calibration that overlaps with the L-II prospectus. A joint TP-II/L-II effort on circadian architecture design parameters would advance both domains simultaneously.

## Scope Exclusions

The following topics were identified during the panel as relevant to temporal experience but belonging in other panels:

**1. Urban-scale temporal dynamics** — how cities change over time, urban renewal, neighborhood succession, seasonal patterns of urban use. These operate at the urban morphology scale and belong in a future Urban-Scale panel (flagged by SC-I as well). TP-I addresses the building scale.

**2. Aging occupants** — how the temporal dimension of architecture interacts with age-related changes in motor capacity, sensory acuity, cognitive mapping, and thermal regulation. A dedicated Aging & Architecture panel (flagged in transfer context) would address this.

**3. Child development** — how developing spatial cognition, motor capacity, and memory systems interact with architectural experience at different developmental stages. A dedicated Child Development & Architecture panel (flagged in transfer context) would address this.

**4. Digital and virtual temporality** — how screen-based information, responsive environments, and interactive architectural systems create new temporal structures. This is an emerging domain that may warrant future attention but lacks the mature scientific literature that TP-I's templates require.

**5. Ritual and liturgical time** — how architecture supports repetitive temporal structures (daily prayer, weekly worship, seasonal festivals). This overlaps with cultural-meaning systems and would be better addressed through the knowledge-meaning dimension of Chatterjee's aesthetic triad than through the PE framework directly.
