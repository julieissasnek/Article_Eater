# Beyond Implicit Processing: How Activity Space Bridges the Explicit–Implicit Divide in the Goldilocks Framework

## Topic Page — Goldilocks Principle in Architectural Design

### Version 1.0 — 2026-02-20

## 1. The Problem: Goldilocks Without Activity

The Goldilocks Principle, as developed in the current deck, rests primarily on *implicit* processing channels. Prediction error (PE) from predictive processing drives the inverted-U: too little stimulation yields boredom (low PE, disengagement), too much yields overwhelm (high PE, cognitive overload), and intermediate levels yield the "just right" zone of optimal cognitive functioning. This story is elegant and well-supported for low-level sensory channels — visual complexity, acoustic intensity, thermal comfort, wood coverage, fractal dimension, ceiling height, and lighting.

But there is a conspicuous gap. The entire framework, if left at the implicit level, cannot explain why the *same physical space* produces radically different psychological responses depending on what people are *doing* in it. A reading room in a library that is "just right" for quiet study may be oppressively understimulating for a collaborative design charrette. A bustling open-plan café that energizes creative freelancers may be catastrophic for someone drafting a legal brief. The physical stimulus profile has not changed — what changed is the *activity*.

This is the problem that Activity Space (Kirsh, 2026) was designed to address, and it is the single most important theoretical addition the Goldilocks framework needs.

---

## 2. What Is Activity Space?

Activity Space, as formalized in Kirsh (2026), reconceives the unit of analysis for human–environment interaction. The argument is that architectural space is not merely geometric volume containing physical features to which occupants passively respond. It is, rather, a structured field of interpreted relevance — shaped by occupants' goals, expectations, skills, and action possibilities.

The concept rests on a three-level analytical framework:

### 2.1 The Logical Level (Goal–Subgoal Structure)

Every human activity has an implicit formal structure: a hierarchy of goals and subgoals, success conditions, legal transitions, and dependencies. Cooking dinner decomposes into preparation, cooking, plating, and serving; each of these further decomposes into finer-grained actions. Chess has its state space of legal moves. Even casual conversation has implicit turn-taking structure and social goals.

This logical level is *topological* — it specifies connectivity and dependency without reference to physical implementation. The same cooking task could be performed in kitchens of wildly different layouts, just as the same chess game could be played on boards of radically different physical form (Kirsh uses the vivid example of chess projected onto a sphere versus a flat board — formally identical, cognitively worlds apart).

For architecture, the crucial insight is that the logical structure of an activity *constrains but does not determine* the physical and cognitive requirements of the space. A designer who understands only the physical environment without understanding the activities it must support is working with incomplete specifications — analogous to designing a chip layout without knowing the circuit topology.

### 2.2 The Physical Level (Metric Implementation)

The physical level concerns how the abstract structure of activities maps onto material, spatial, geometric reality: distances between workstations, tool placement, circulation paths, sight lines, HVAC, lighting, acoustics. This is the level at which conventional architectural programming and requirements gathering operate — bubble diagrams, adjacency matrices, spatial optimization.

Kirsh makes the critical point that the relationship between logical and physical levels is one of topology-to-metric mapping: the same formal activity structure admits indefinitely many physical implementations, and the quality of that mapping profoundly affects performance. *Mise-en-place* in French cooking is a canonical example — it does not change the formal task of cooking (one is no closer to a finished dish after arranging ingredients), but it dramatically reduces the cognitive cost of execution by encoding sequential and timing information in spatial layout (Kirsh, 1995).

### 2.3 The Cognitive Level (Representational and Processing Consequences)

This is where the concept achieves its distinctive power. The cognitive level concerns how the design of activity space affects what people *represent*, *attend to*, *remember*, and *compute* during task performance. Two physically identical kitchens — same layout, same equipment — can differ enormously in cognitive congeniality (Kirsh, 1996; Chandrasekharan, 2005) depending on whether tools, cues, and state information are arranged to support the occupant's cognitive workflow.

Cognitive congeniality is measurable: speed-accuracy tradeoffs, interruption recovery time, error rates, and worst-case error costs all provide behavioral metrics for how well an environment supports the cognitive demands of the activities performed within it.

## 3. The Epistemic Bubble: Why the Room Is Not the Unit of Analysis

The most radical implication of Activity Space theory is the concept of the *epistemic bubble* (or *enactive envelope*). When a person is engaged in an activity, they do not perceive the full geometric space around them. They perceive and respond to a *personally constructed field of interpreted relevance* — a subset of the environment filtered, weighted, and in some cases augmented by their current goals, expectations, and action possibilities.

Kirsh illustrates this vividly with the Paddington Station example. A traveler rushing to catch a train inhabits one enactive space — focused on gate numbers, platform signs, crowd flow. When the train is delayed and the traveler decides to find a café, they enter a *different* enactive space in the *same physical station*. Different features become salient, different affordances are perceived, different navigation strategies engage. The physical environment is unchanged; the experienced environment has shifted dramatically.

The empirical grounding for this claim is strong. Simons and Chabris (1999) demonstrated that 70% of participants focused on a basketball-counting task failed to notice a person in a gorilla suit walking through the scene — inattentional blindness driven entirely by task framing. Uncapher, Hutchinson, and Wagner (2011) showed neuroimaging evidence that both top-down (goal-driven) and bottom-up (stimulus-driven) attention modulate what gets encoded into memory during navigation and spatial experience. The task-positive network (Cole, Bassett, Power, Braver, & Petersen, 2014) and its anticorrelation with the default mode network (Fox et al., 2005; Raichle et al., 2001) provide the neural architecture for this activity-gated perception.

The upshot for architecture: **the room is not the unit of analysis — the activity space is**. A single room may contain multiple overlapping activity spaces, as Kirsh demonstrates with the Florentine restaurant where servers, bussers, diners, and cleaners all occupy the same physical space but inhabit entirely different enactive envelopes, perceiving different features, following different paths, and operating under different cognitive constraints.

## 4. Why the Goldilocks Framework Needs Activity Space

### 4.1 The Moderation Problem

Without Activity Space, the Goldilocks Principle must treat a given physical environment as having a *fixed* stimulus profile that either falls within or outside the optimal zone. But this is empirically false. The same physical space is "just right" for some activities and wrong for others. The inverted-U is not anchored to the room — it is anchored to the *activity-in-the-room*.

Activity Space provides the missing moderating variable. The Goldilocks optimum for any sensory channel is *conditional on the activity frame*:

- **Visual complexity**: Moderate fractal complexity (D ≈ 1.3–1.5) may be optimal for restorative contemplation but suboptimal for focused analytical work that requires minimal visual distraction.
- **Acoustic intensity**: The creative-task sweet spot (~50–70 dB ambient) identified in the literature presupposes a specific activity class; the optimal acoustic profile for concentrated reading is markedly different.
- **Thermal comfort**: The narrow thermal band for cognitive performance shifts depending on activity metabolic rate — sedentary desk work versus physical therapy versus collaborative discussion.

In each case, the activity frame — the logical structure of what the person is trying to accomplish — sets the *boundary conditions* within which implicit processing operates.

### 4.2 The Bridge Between Explicit and Implicit Processing

This is the conceptual heart of the matter. Slides 25–28 of the current deck establish that the Goldilocks framework has an advantage over predictive processing alone because it can integrate *explicit* processing — aesthetic judgments, semantic meaning, cultural norms, deliberate evaluation — alongside the implicit inverted-U. The Kidd and Hayden (2015) challenge is that their inverted-U evidence comes from infant and animal studies and cannot straightforwardly generalize to adult explicit cognition.

Activity Space provides the *mechanism* for this integration:

1. **Activity selection is partly explicit.** Choosing to read, to converse, to sketch, to meditate — these are deliberate, goal-directed decisions involving prefrontal executive function. The person *decides* (explicitly) what they are going to do.

2. **Once set, the activity frame reconfigures the affordance ecology.** This is the critical move. When a person commits to an activity, their epistemic bubble narrows and reshapes: different features become salient, different affordances are perceived, different prediction models engage. This reconfiguration is largely *implicit* — it happens automatically through the task-positive network's modulation of attention and salience.

3. **The implicit processing system now operates over a *different* effective stimulus.** The physical environment has not changed, but the effective stimulus — what the predictive processing system is actually generating predictions about and computing prediction errors over — has been transformed by the activity frame. Same room, same physical features, different PE profile.

4. **Therefore, the Goldilocks optimum shifts.** The inverted-U for each channel is now defined *relative to the activity*, not relative to the room's absolute physical properties.

This is the bridge: explicit processing (activity selection) *gates* implicit processing (PE-based inverted-U) by reconfiguring the affordance ecology through which the implicit system encounters the physical environment. Activity Space is the mechanism by which deliberate, top-down, goal-directed cognition reshapes the landscape over which bottom-up, automatic, PE-driven optimization operates.

### 4.3 Cognitive Niche Engineering

The Activity Space concept also connects the Goldilocks framework to the extended mind thesis (Clark & Chalmers, 1998) and the broader program of cognitive niche engineering. If architectural design is understood as the construction of cognitive niches — environments engineered to support specific patterns of thought and action (Kirsh, 1996, 2019) — then the Goldilocks Principle becomes a *design optimization criterion* for niche construction.

The niche must be tuned not merely to human sensory preferences in the abstract, but to the *activities* occupants will pursue. This is why the Dokk1 library in Aarhus is so instructive: it contains zones that are cognitively congenial for quiet reading (low acoustic intensity, moderate visual complexity, controlled lighting) and zones designed for children's active exploration (higher stimulation, more visual complexity, flexible spatial configuration). The *same building* implements *different Goldilocks profiles* for *different activity spaces*.

## 5. Formal Template Specifications

The following templates formalize how Activity Space moderates Goldilocks predictions:

### T_AS_001: Cognitive Congeniality Modulation
**Claim:** Environments designed with high cognitive congeniality for a target activity will show narrower, more peaked inverted-U curves (i.e., stronger Goldilocks effects) than environments with low cognitive congeniality.
**Mechanism:** Congenial environments reduce extraneous cognitive load, freeing processing resources to be sensitive to the stimulus channel under investigation. Incongenial environments introduce noise that flattens the inverted-U.
**Predicted interaction:** Congeniality × stimulus_level → performance | well-being.

### T_AS_002: Epistemic Bubble Gating
**Claim:** Features of the physical environment that fall outside the current epistemic bubble do not contribute to the effective Goldilocks stimulus level.
**Mechanism:** Activity-driven attentional filtering (inattentional blindness, task-positive network suppression of irrelevant features) means that only activity-relevant features generate prediction error.
**Predicted dissociation:** Same physical feature → different PE contribution depending on whether the feature is within or outside the current activity's epistemic bubble.

### T_AS_003: Multi-Activity Interference
**Claim:** Spaces that must support multiple simultaneous activity spaces (e.g., open-plan offices, mixed-use libraries) will show degraded Goldilocks optimization because the optimal stimulus profile for one activity may fall outside the optimal zone for concurrent activities.
**Mechanism:** Overlapping epistemic bubbles create cross-talk; features that are "just right" for one activity generate prediction errors (too much or too little) for another.
**Design implication:** Spatial, acoustic, and visual partitioning that respects activity-space boundaries improves multi-activity Goldilocks performance.

### T_AS_004: Cognitive Congeniality as Distinct from Physical Optimization
**Claim:** Cognitive congeniality is not reducible to physical ergonomic optimization. Two physically identical environments can differ in cognitive congeniality if one encodes task-relevant state information in spatial layout (e.g., *mise-en-place*) while the other does not.
**Mechanism:** Cognitive congeniality operates at the representational level (Kirsh, 2026, cognitive perspective), reducing computational demands through external representation and environmental scaffolding, while physical optimization operates at the metric level.
**Predicted independence:** Physical optimization (e.g., minimal walking distance) and cognitive optimization (e.g., maximal state visibility) are partially independent dimensions; improving one does not necessarily improve the other.

## 6. Implications for the Goldilocks Deck

### 6.1 Structural Role in the Argument
Activity Space is not merely another empirical domain to be covered (like temperature or ceiling height). It is a *meta-framework* — the only Tier 1 framework in the current system that operates at the logical level and therefore provides *moderating predictions* about when and how all other frameworks apply. It belongs early in the deck, before the empirical channel slides, because it sets up the interpretive frame: "when we say a sensory channel has an optimal zone, we mean optimal *for this activity*."

### 6.2 Narrative Arc
The recommended narrative arc is:

1. **Slide 8 (Activity Space theory):** Introduce the three-level framework, the epistemic bubble concept, and the claim that the room is not the unit of analysis.
2. **Slide 9 (Dokk1 demonstration):** Show how one building implements multiple activity spaces with different Goldilocks profiles — the theoretical claim made concrete.
3. **Slides 10–14 (empirical channels):** Now each channel slide carries the implicit qualification that its inverted-U is moderated by activity frame.
4. **Slides 25–28 (explicit processing):** Activity Space provides the *mechanism* for the explicit–implicit bridge — the slide 8 theory pays off here.
5. **Slide 28 (cognitive niche engineering):** Activity Space connects the Goldilocks framework to extended mind and niche construction, citing Kirsh (2026) directly.

### 6.3 Connection to Indian Fieldwork
The Activity Space framework also enriches the cross-cultural analysis. If Indian architectural traditions operate at systematically elevated sensory set-points across multiple channels, this may reflect not merely different implicit preferences but different *activity norms* — the activities typical of Indian domestic and commercial spaces (multi-generational gathering, devotional practice, commercial bazaar navigation) may *require* higher stimulation levels for cognitive congeniality. The Goldilocks optimum shifts because the typical activity space shifts.

## 7. Key Claims (Summary)

1. **The room is not the unit of analysis — the activity space is.** Human response to architectural environments is mediated by the activity frame, which determines the epistemic bubble through which physical features are perceived and processed.

2. **Activity Space operates at three levels (logical, physical, cognitive)** that are analytically distinct and provide different types of design constraint. Only the Activity Space framework among Tier 1 theories operates at the logical level.

3. **Activity selection is explicit; its downstream effects are implicit.** Choosing an activity is a deliberate, top-down act. But once the activity frame is set, it reconfigures the affordance ecology through which the implicit PE-based processing system encounters the environment. This is the bridge between explicit and implicit channels.

4. **Cognitive congeniality is measurable and designable.** Speed-accuracy profiles, interruption recovery, error rates, and related behavioral metrics provide quantitative assessment of how well an environment supports the cognitive demands of target activities.

5. **The Goldilocks optimum is activity-relative.** Every inverted-U in the framework should be understood as conditional on the activity frame. Multi-activity spaces face inherent Goldilocks conflicts that require spatial, acoustic, and visual partitioning.

6. **Activity Space connects Goldilocks to niche construction.** Architectural design, understood as cognitive niche engineering, must be tuned not merely to human sensory preferences but to the activities occupants will pursue (Kirsh, 2026; Clark & Chalmers, 1998).
