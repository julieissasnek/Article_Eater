# PANEL CREA-I: CREATIVE COGNITION AND THE BUILT ENVIRONMENT
## Mechanistic Templates for How Architectural Features Modulate Divergent Thinking, Insight, and the Incubation–Elaboration Cycle
## February 16, 2026 — Document 55

## Panel Charge

This panel addresses the gap identified in Document 53 for **A9 Task & Cognition — Creativity** (coverage ★★+, the lowest sub-rating in the entire system). The attention dimension of A9 is well served (★★★★) by T4 (attentional demand), T25 (directed attention fatigue), T36 (working memory load), T38 (cognitive load / metabolic cost), and T45 (proactive/reactive control). But the creative cognition dimension has no dedicated template. The current library touches creativity obliquely — VF3 (ceiling height → relational processing, Doc 54), T27 (DMN/TPN dynamics), and the ART reduction (Doc 21, soft fascination → directed attention restoration) — yet nowhere does the system formalize the specific neural and cognitive mechanisms by which built environments facilitate or inhibit creative thought.

This is a consequential gap. The claim that certain architectural environments promote creativity is pervasive in design practice — open-plan "innovation labs," high-ceilinged lofts, biophilic workspaces, walking paths — yet the mechanistic basis for these claims ranges from well-supported to folklore. A panel of creativity researchers and environmental psychologists is needed to separate signal from noise and produce templates with the same mechanistic rigor as the rest of the library.

The panel is charged with producing 2–3 mechanistic templates that formalize: (1) how environmental features modulate the dynamic coupling between default-mode and executive-control networks that underlies creative ideation, (2) how multiple architectural channels (spatial proportion, ambient sound, illumination, visual complexity) jointly shift cognitive processing style between divergent and convergent modes, and (3) how transitional and movement-supporting spaces facilitate the incubation phase of creative problem-solving through motor-cognitive coupling and spontaneous thought.

These templates must connect to the existing infrastructure — particularly T27 (DMN/TPN dynamics), T45 (proactive/reactive control), T25 (directed attention fatigue), VF3 (ceiling height / cognitive mode), T1 (fractal fluency), T2 (complexity Goldilocks zone), L4 (CCT as ecological prediction signal), MAT4 (natural material convergence), and VIEW1 (nature view multi-channel convergence). The panel must also address the relationship between creativity and the ART reduction (Doc 21): the restored attentional state produced by soft fascination environments is a precondition for creative ideation, but restoration and creation are not the same process.

### Key Questions

1. **Network dynamics**: Is creative cognition best modeled as a state of flexible DMN–executive coupling, as the suppression of executive inhibition, or as an alternation between generative (DMN-dominant) and evaluative (executive-dominant) phases? What environmental features support each?

2. **Multi-channel modulation**: Do ceiling height (VF3), ambient noise level (Mehta et al., 2012), illumination level (Steidle & Werth, 2013), and nature exposure (ART/VIEW1) affect creativity through a single shared mechanism (processing disfluency → abstract processing) or through multiple independent pathways?

3. **Incubation architecture**: Can the incubation effect — the well-documented finding that creative problem-solving improves after a period of non-focused activity — be deliberately supported by architectural features such as walking paths, transitional spaces, and environments that promote mind-wandering?

4. **Boundary conditions**: Under what conditions do "creativity-promoting" features become counterproductive? Does the divergent-thinking benefit of open, unconstrained environments trade off against the focused-execution demands of actually producing creative work?

5. **Individual differences and populations**: Do the same environmental features promote creativity across populations, or do novice creators, expert creators, and different cultural groups respond differently?

## Panel Composition

### Creative Cognition Neuroscience

**1. Roger Beaty** (Pennsylvania State University, Department of Psychology)
The leading researcher on the neural basis of creative cognition, particularly the dynamic interplay between default-mode and executive-control networks during creative ideation. His landmark study (Beaty, Benedek, Silvia, & Schacter, 2016, ~1,200 citations) demonstrated that highly creative individuals show stronger functional connectivity between DMN nodes (medial PFC, posterior cingulate) and executive regions (dorsolateral PFC, inferior frontal gyrus) during divergent thinking tasks. This coupling — not DMN activation alone — predicts individual differences in creative ability.
*Role*: Establishes the neural architecture that environmental features must modulate. The central claim is that creativity requires *cooperation* between networks that are typically anti-correlated. Any environmental manipulation that shifts DMN–executive coupling should affect creative output. Beaty's connectome data provide the most precise neural targets for template specification.

**2. Jonathan Schooler** (University of California, Santa Barbara, Department of Psychological and Brain Sciences)
Pioneer of research on mind-wandering, meta-awareness, and the incubation effect in creative problem-solving. His work established that mind-wandering — spontaneous, task-unrelated thought — is not merely attentional failure but a functional cognitive mode that supports creative incubation (Schooler, Smallwood, Christoff, Handy, Reichle, & Sayette, 2011, ~1,500 citations). His "intermittent conscious awareness" framework distinguishes between mind-wandering with and without meta-awareness, with only the former supporting creative insight.
*Role*: Provides the cognitive process model for incubation. The key architectural question: which environmental features promote the specific kind of mind-wandering (meta-aware, loosely goal-directed) that supports creative incubation, versus the kind (zoning out without awareness) that does not? Walking, nature exposure, and low-demand sensory environments are candidates, but the mechanism must be specified.

**3. Mathias Benedek** (University of Graz, Institute of Psychology)
Research on the neuroscience of divergent thinking, particularly the cognitive control processes involved in creative idea generation. His work (Benedek, Jauk, Sommer, Arendasy, & Neubauer, 2014, ~500 citations; Benedek & Fink, 2019, ~300 citations) demonstrates that creative ideation is not simply "unleashed" by relaxing control — it requires a specific form of controlled retrieval from long-term memory, guided by internal goals rather than external stimuli. Alpha-band oscillations over prefrontal cortex during creative tasks index this internally directed attention.
*Role*: Provides the critical corrective to the naive "creativity = relaxation" model. Benedek's data show that the most creative moments involve *more* prefrontal alpha (internal focus) and *more* executive engagement, not less. This constrains the architectural claim: environments that merely reduce demands do not automatically promote creativity. They must support the specific mode of internally directed, goal-guided but loosely constrained cognition.

**4. Kalina Christoff** (University of British Columbia, Department of Psychology)
Research on spontaneous thought and its neural substrates. Her "dynamic framework of thought" (Christoff, Irving, Fox, Spreng, & Andrews-Hanna, 2016, ~1,800 citations) proposes that thoughts vary along two dimensions: deliberate constraints (executive control) and automatic constraints (salience, habit). Creative thought occupies the region of low-to-moderate deliberate constraint with moderate automatic constraint — it is neither fully free-associative nor fully controlled.
*Role*: Provides the theoretical framework for mapping environmental features onto the thought-space that supports creativity. Her two-dimensional model predicts that environments should be assessed not by a single "stimulation" axis but by how they position the mind in deliberate × automatic constraint space. The creative sweet spot requires environments that relax deliberate constraints without eliminating them entirely.

### Environmental Modulation of Creativity

**5. Ravi Mehta** (University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, Gies College of Business)
Author of the landmark finding that moderate ambient noise (~70 dB, similar to a coffee shop) enhances creative performance relative to both low noise (~50 dB) and high noise (~85 dB), through a processing-disfluency mechanism: moderate noise slightly impairs focused processing, which triggers abstract, higher-level construal that facilitates divergent thinking (Mehta, Zhu, & Cheema, 2012, ~1,100 citations). This study generated the "coffee shop effect" narrative and directly implicates acoustic environment in creative cognition.
*Role*: Provides the most cited single finding on environmental modulation of creativity. The panel must evaluate: is the mechanism truly processing disfluency → abstract construal, or is it better described as moderate arousal optimization (inverted-U)? If disfluency, the mechanism shares structure with VF3 (ceiling height → freedom → abstract processing) and may reflect a general pathway. If arousal, it connects to T7 (noradrenergic arousal modulation).

**6. Anna Steidle** (Ludwigsburg University of Applied Sciences, Department of Business Psychology)
Research on how illumination affects creativity and cognitive processing style. Her key finding: dim lighting (150 lux) promotes creative performance relative to bright lighting (1,500 lux), mediated by a sense of being free from constraints — darkness creates a psychological "safe harbor" for unconventional thinking (Steidle & Werth, 2013, ~300 citations). The proposed mechanism — perceived freedom → reduced inhibition → divergent thinking — parallels the ceiling height effect and the moderate noise effect.
*Role*: Provides the illumination–creativity evidence and connects to the L-series templates. The critical question: is the dim-lighting benefit operating through the same freedom/disfluency pathway as ceiling height and moderate noise (suggesting a common mechanism), or through a distinct route — perhaps reduced visual salience of the immediate environment, which shifts attention inward toward default-mode processing? L4 (CCT as ecological prediction signal) predicts warm, low-intensity light → evening → relaxation; the creativity-specific mechanism may ride on this circadian ecological cue.

**7. Joan Meyers-Levy** (University of Minnesota, Carlson School of Management)
*Returning panelist from VF-I.* Author of the ceiling height effect (Meyers-Levy & Zhu, 2007, ~1,200 citations). High ceilings activate relational (abstract, creative) processing; low ceilings activate item-specific (detail-focused) processing. VF3 (Doc 54) parameterized the proportional ratio but flagged the creativity dimension as needing dedicated treatment.
*Role*: Provides the spatial-proportion evidence and the freedom-confinement priming account. The creativity panel must integrate this with the noise and lighting evidence to determine whether there is a single "processing style" pathway or multiple independent routes to divergent thinking.

**8. Marily Oppezzo** (Santa Clara University, Department of Psychology)
Author of the finding that walking — indoors or outdoors — substantially boosts divergent thinking performance (Oppezzo & Schwartz, 2014, ~800 citations). The effect was specific to divergent thinking (convergent thinking was not improved and was sometimes impaired by walking). The study could not isolate whether the mechanism was motor-proprioceptive stimulation, cardiovascular arousal, environmental change, or attentional disengagement from the task.
*Role*: Provides the motor-cognition evidence critical for Template CREA3 (incubation architecture). The key question: if walking promotes divergent thinking regardless of whether it occurs indoors (on a treadmill) or outdoors, then the mechanism is at least partly motor/proprioceptive, not purely environmental. This has direct implications for architectural corridor, stairway, and garden design.

### Integration and Synthesis

**9. Rex Jung** (University of New Mexico, Department of Neurosurgery and Psychology)
Research on the neuroscience of creativity with a structural and network-level perspective. His "transient hypofrontality" hypothesis (Dietrich & Kanso, 2010, ~600 citations, which Jung has refined in subsequent work) proposes that creative insight involves temporary downregulation of dorsolateral PFC, allowing unusual associative combinations to emerge from posterior cortical regions and DMN. The resolution likely involves temporal dynamics — initial generative phases may benefit from reduced filtering, while evaluation and elaboration phases require increased control. This has direct architectural implications: different spaces for different phases.

## Part 1: Individual Position Statements

### 1.1 Roger Beaty — Creativity Is a Network Dynamic, Not a Brain Region

I want to correct a misconception that has persisted in both the neuroscience and architecture literatures: the idea that creativity lives in the right hemisphere, or in the default-mode network, or in the prefrontal cortex. It lives in none of these places individually. Creativity emerges from the *dynamic coupling* between networks that typically work in opposition.

Our connectome work (Beaty et al., 2016; Beaty, Kenett, Christensen, et al., 2018, ~900 citations) shows that during divergent thinking tasks, highly creative individuals exhibit stronger functional connectivity between three networks simultaneously: (1) the default-mode network (medial PFC, posterior cingulate, angular gyrus), which generates candidate ideas through spontaneous associative processes; (2) the executive-control network (dorsolateral PFC, inferior frontal gyrus, anterior inferior parietal lobule), which evaluates and selects among these candidates; and (3) the salience network (anterior insula, dorsal ACC), which orchestrates the switching between generative and evaluative modes.

The critical finding is that this three-network coupling is not a static state — it fluctuates dynamically over the course of a single creative task (Beaty, Seli, & Schacter, 2019, ~300 citations). Early in ideation, DMN dominates with weaker executive coupling (generating diverse candidates). As the task progresses and the person converges on a response, executive coupling strengthens (evaluating and refining). The salience network gates the transition. *Creativity is the fluid management of this temporal sequence, not the activation of any single component.*

For the architectural question, this means we should ask not "which environments activate the DMN?" but rather "which environments support flexible, rapid alternation between generative and evaluative modes?" An environment that locks the brain into either pure DMN mode (total relaxation, no evaluation) or pure executive mode (high demand, no generation) would be anti-creative. The sweet spot is an environment that provides enough cognitive freedom for DMN-driven generation but enough structure to engage evaluation when needed.

My prediction for this panel: environments with *moderate* sensory richness — not understimulating (which fails to engage the salience network switching mechanism) and not overstimulating (which locks executive control into reactive mode) — will optimally support creative coupling. This is a Goldilocks prediction, but it is more specific than the general complexity Goldilocks (T2) because it specifies the NETWORK mechanism: moderate complexity engages the salience network at a switching rate compatible with DMN–executive alternation.

**Position**: Template CREA1 should formalize DMN–executive coupling dynamics as the core neural mechanism. Environmental parameters should be specified in terms of how they shift coupling strength, switching rate, and phase dominance (generative vs. evaluative).

### 1.2 Jonathan Schooler — Mind-Wandering Is Not All Created Equal

I need the panel to understand a distinction that is often lost in the "mind-wandering promotes creativity" narrative. Not all mind-wandering is creative, and the conditions that promote creative mind-wandering are surprisingly specific.

Our taxonomy (Schooler et al., 2011; Seli, Kane, Smallwood, Schacter, et al., 2018, ~1,100 citations) distinguishes at minimum: (1) deliberate mind-wandering (intentionally letting the mind roam — "let me think about this loosely"); (2) spontaneous mind-wandering (the mind drifts without intention); (3) meta-aware mind-wandering (you notice that your mind has wandered); and (4) mind-wandering without meta-awareness (you are unaware that you have drifted). The evidence strongly suggests that the creative benefit comes primarily from spontaneous mind-wandering *with meta-awareness* — the state where the mind generates novel associations freely but the person retains enough reflective capacity to notice when something interesting has emerged.

The architectural implication is non-obvious. An environment that demands so little attention that the person completely zones out (mind-wandering without meta-awareness) is NOT optimally creative — the person may generate associative chains but lacks the meta-cognitive monitoring to catch the useful ones. An environment that provides gentle, intermittent stimulation — what Kaplan called "soft fascination" — maintains just enough external engagement to keep the meta-awareness channel open while allowing spontaneous thought to proceed. This is why nature environments appear to support creative incubation: the fractal visual patterns, birdsong, water movement provide intermittent bottom-up engagement that anchors partial awareness without capturing it fully.

The incubation effect (Sio & Ormerod, 2009, ~700 citations) — the finding that taking a break from a problem and returning to it later improves solution rates — is most effective when the incubation period is filled with *low-demand* activity rather than rest or high-demand activity. Walking, gentle gardening, showering, gazing at landscapes — these are exactly the activities that promote spontaneous meta-aware mind-wandering. And they are all activities that architecture can support or impede.

**Position**: Template CREA3 should formalize the incubation mechanism and specify the environmental conditions (low-demand activity, intermittent soft fascination, movement) that support creative mind-wandering with meta-awareness. It must distinguish this from mere relaxation or passive rest.

### 1.3 Mathias Benedek — Creativity Requires MORE Control, Not Less

I must respectfully push back against a narrative I expect to hear on this panel: the idea that creativity flows when executive control is reduced. The data do not support this.

Our EEG studies of divergent thinking (Benedek, Jauk, et al., 2014; Benedek & Fink, 2019; Benedek, Schickel, Jauk, Fink, & Neubauer, 2014, ~400 citations) consistently show that the most creative responses — those rated as both original and appropriate — are associated with *increased* upper alpha power (10–12 Hz) over prefrontal cortex. Alpha power in this context indexes internally directed attention — the deliberate suppression of external sensory input to focus on internal representations. This is not executive *relaxation*; it is executive *redirection*. The control system is working hard, but it is pointed inward rather than outward.

What changes during creative ideation is not the AMOUNT of control but its TARGET. In routine cognition, executive control manages the interface between the person and the external environment — monitoring input, selecting responses, inhibiting prepotent actions. During creative ideation, the same control system manages the interface between the person and their own memory — directing retrieval toward distant associations, inhibiting dominant but unoriginal responses, evaluating the novelty and appropriateness of internally generated candidates.

The architectural implication: environments that reduce EXTERNAL executive demands (quiet, uncluttered, thermally comfortable) free up control resources for INTERNAL creative processing. This is not the same as claiming that low-demand environments directly promote creativity. A perfectly silent, featureless room frees executive resources but provides no content for creative combination. The optimal creative environment reduces irrelevant external demand while providing sufficient sensory input to serve as fuel for associative processes — what I would call "structured freedom."

This connects to Beaty's coupling dynamics: the increased prefrontal alpha during creativity IS the neural signature of the executive network participating in DMN-generated ideation, not opposing it. The executive system is not suppressed — it is recruited into the service of internally directed thought.

**Position**: CREA1 must not model creativity as executive-control reduction. It should model the REDIRECTION of executive resources from external monitoring to internal-search management, with environmental parameters specified in terms of how they shift the external/internal balance.

### 1.4 Kalina Christoff — The Two-Dimensional Thought Space

I want to provide a framework that can reconcile what may seem like contradictions between my colleagues. The deliberate-constraints × automatic-constraints model (Christoff et al., 2016) provides a two-dimensional space in which different modes of thought, and the environments that support them, can be mapped:

**High deliberate / Low automatic**: Goal-directed analytical thinking. The person is actively controlling their train of thought and screening out habitual or stimulus-driven intrusions. This is the mode of focused work — writing, calculation, careful reading. Architectural support: enclosed, quiet, minimal distraction.

**Low deliberate / High automatic**: Habitual, stimulus-driven behavior. The person is not exercising top-down control and their thought follows habitual or environmentally cued paths. Architectural support (or induction): highly familiar, routine environments that trigger autopilot.

**Low deliberate / Low automatic**: Free-associative dreaming, the loosest form of mind-wandering. Few constraints of any kind. May produce occasional creative sparks but mostly produces incoherent, quickly forgotten associations. Architectural support: sensory deprivation, rest.

**Moderate deliberate / Low-to-moderate automatic**: THE CREATIVE SWEET SPOT. The person maintains a loose goal ("come up with ideas for...") that provides directional constraint without rigid control, while automatic associations are allowed to flow with partial monitoring. This is where creative incubation, divergent thinking, and insight preparation occur.

This mapping resolves the Benedek-vs-Jung debate. Benedek's "more control" findings emerge because creative tasks require more deliberate constraint than free association — the person must direct their associative processes toward a goal. Jung's "transient hypofrontality" describes a *relative* reduction compared to focused analytical work, not an absolute reduction. Both are correct — creativity requires LESS deliberate constraint than analysis but MORE than daydreaming. The optimal zone is intermediate.

For architecture, this predicts that environments should be classified along two axes: (1) how much deliberate/executive constraint they impose or support (from high-demand complex wayfinding to zero-demand lounging), and (2) how much automatic/stimulus-driven constraint they impose (from highly salient, attention-capturing features to sensory minimalism). The creative sweet spot maps to environments with LOW imposed executive demand but MODERATE sensory richness — environments that leave deliberate control available for internal direction while providing enough sensory content to fuel associative processes without capturing attention fully.

**Position**: CREA2 should use the deliberate × automatic constraint framework to map environmental features onto the thought-space and identify the architectural conditions that position cognition in the creative zone.

### 1.5 Ravi Mehta — The Disfluency Pathway and Its Limits

My finding that ~70 dB ambient noise promotes creativity (Mehta et al., 2012) has been widely cited but often oversimplified. Let me specify what the data actually show and where the limits are.

The mechanism we proposed is processing disfluency → construal level shift → abstract processing → enhanced divergent thinking. Moderate ambient noise slightly impairs the processing of focal information, and this impairment triggers a compensatory shift to higher-level, more abstract construal — the mind "zooms out" when it cannot process at the fine-grained level. This abstract construal promotes the kind of broad, category-spanning associative thinking that supports divergent creativity.

Three boundary conditions matter for the architectural application:

First, the effect is specific to *divergent* thinking. Convergent thinking (solving problems with a single correct answer), detail-oriented work, and analytical reasoning are IMPAIRED by moderate noise, as our data showed. An architectural space optimized for creative ideation via the disfluency pathway would be suboptimal for the convergent phases of creative work. This immediately suggests that the creative process needs different environments for different phases — a finding with obvious implications for workspace design.

Second, the noise type matters. We tested relatively undifferentiated ambient noise (simulated coffee shop). Speech noise with intelligible content would be expected to be MORE disruptive because it engages linguistic processing systems involuntarily (T31, auditory scene analysis; T47, gamma/beta WM dynamics). The creativity benefit requires noise that is present but not semantically engaging. Natural sounds (wind, water, birdsong) may be ideal because they provide acoustic richness without semantic content — connecting to the soft fascination pathway from ART.

Third, the disfluency interpretation has been challenged. An alternative account holds that the effect is simply arousal optimization — an inverted-U relationship between arousal and creative performance, with moderate noise at the peak. Mehta and Zhu (2016, ~200 citations) provided additional evidence favoring the disfluency account over pure arousal, but the debate is not fully resolved. The distinction matters architecturally: if disfluency, then any source of mild processing difficulty (moderate noise, dim lighting, slight thermal deviation) should work through the same pathway; if arousal, then different sensory channels would produce additive arousal effects rather than converging on a single mechanism.

**Position**: The disfluency–construal shift mechanism is real but domain-limited. CREA2 should model it as one of several channels through which environment modulates processing style, with clear scope conditions marking where it helps (divergent thinking) and where it hurts (convergent execution).

### 1.6 Anna Steidle — Darkness as Psychological Freedom

Our finding that dim lighting (~150 lux) enhances creative performance relative to bright lighting (~1,500 lux) was mediated by a perceived sense of freedom (Steidle & Werth, 2013). We proposed that dim lighting creates a feeling of being unconstrained — partly through reduced visual scrutiny (feeling less observed), partly through the association of darkness with fewer rules and more permissiveness (nighttime, after hours), and partly through reduced visual salience of the immediate environment, which shifts attention inward.

There is a piece that connects to Mehta's work that I want to make explicit: both moderate noise and dim lighting may operate through the SAME higher-level mechanism — a shift from external to internal attention that supports abstract processing. In our studies, dim lighting specifically enhanced performance on tasks requiring unconventional associations and creative elaboration. The mediator was perceived freedom, but the underlying mechanism may be what Benedek describes: a reallocation of executive resources from external monitoring (which bright light supports by making the environment highly salient) to internal search (which dim light supports by reducing environmental salience).

The critical connection to the L-series templates: L4 (CCT as ecological prediction signal) predicts that warm, dim light sends an "evening" ecological signal — relaxation, social intimacy, reduced threat vigilance. This ecological signal may be the distal cause of the creativity benefit: the brain interprets dim warm light as a low-threat, low-demand situation, which licenses the reallocation of executive resources from external monitoring to internal creative processing. If this is correct, the pathway is: dim warm light → ecological evening signal (L4) → reduced threat vigilance (T5 pathway downregulated) → executive resources freed from external monitoring → available for internally directed creative search (CREA1).

However, I must acknowledge boundary conditions. Dim lighting impairs reading, fine motor work, and any task requiring high-resolution visual processing. The creative benefit comes at a visual-performance cost. Additionally, some individuals report feeling sleepy or unmotivated in dim conditions, suggesting that the circadian relaxation signal can overshoot into drowsiness. The optimal creative lighting may be dim AMBIENT light with task-specific supplementary light available — a layered lighting approach that provides the global "freedom" signal while preserving task capability.

**Position**: The dim-lighting creativity effect connects to L4 (ecological CCT) and shares a mechanism with the moderate-noise effect. CREA2 should model both as inputs to a common processing-style shift pathway, but must specify the boundary conditions (drowsiness, visual task impairment) and the architectural solution (layered lighting).

### 1.7 Joan Meyers-Levy — Ceiling Height Revisited for Creativity

I want to bring specific data to this panel that goes beyond what was covered in VF-I (Doc 39) and the VF3 parameterization in Doc 54. Our original finding (Meyers-Levy & Zhu, 2007) demonstrated that 10-foot ceilings activated "relational" processing (making connections across categories) while 8-foot ceilings activated "item-specific" processing (focusing on individual features). The mechanism we proposed was freedom-confinement priming — high ceilings activate the concept of freedom, which through spreading activation promotes unconstrained relational processing.

What the panel may not know is the dose-response evidence that has accumulated since 2007. Vartanian et al. (2015, ~200 citations) found that the effect is not linear — extremely high ceilings (cathedral-scale) can produce a sense of awe or insignificance that shifts processing in a different direction entirely, toward self-transcendent rather than creative ideation. The awe response (AX3 in the template library) may override the freedom-priming pathway at very high ceiling heights. The VF3 parameterization (Doc 54) captured this with the "overwhelming > 0.80" proportional ratio category, but the specific interaction with creative processing was flagged as needing this panel's treatment.

For this panel I want to propose that the ceiling height effect on creativity is best understood as operating through TWO pathways: (1) the freedom-priming → unconstrained association pathway, which is dominant at moderately high proportional ratios (0.35–0.50 in VF3 terms); and (2) the spatial-prediction → processing-style pathway, which is the PE account sketched in Doc 33 and formalized in VF3 — high ceilings expand the spatial prediction envelope, and the brain responds to this expanded prediction range by adopting a more abstract, relational processing mode. These two pathways converge: freedom priming is the cognitive appraisal; spatial prediction expansion is the PE mechanism.

**Position**: VF3 already captures the basic parametric structure. CREA2 should model ceiling height as one channel in a multi-channel processing-style modulation system, connecting to VF3 for the parameterization and adding the specific creativity-relevant prediction that the "liberating" zone (0.35–0.50 R_h) is optimal for divergent thinking, while the "impressive" zone (0.50–0.80) begins transitioning from creative to awe-related processing.

### 1.8 Marily Oppezzo — Walking, Movement, and the Body–Mind Connection

Our finding (Oppezzo & Schwartz, 2014) was striking in its specificity: walking doubled divergent thinking output on the Guilford Alternative Uses Task, but this effect persisted even on a treadmill facing a blank wall. Outdoor walking was slightly superior to indoor treadmill walking, but the indoor treadmill effect was itself very large (d ≈ 0.8). This means the primary mechanism is motor/proprioceptive, not environmental.

Several candidate mechanisms deserve consideration:

First, the *bilateral rhythmic stimulation* hypothesis: walking involves rhythmic alternating activation of the motor cortex, which through cortico-cortical coupling may entrain prefrontal oscillatory dynamics. There is evidence that bilateral alternating movement promotes interhemispheric communication (Oppezzo & Schwartz, 2014, supplementary analysis; see also Chaddock-Heyman et al., 2014, ~350 citations on exercise and brain connectivity). If interhemispheric communication supports the kind of broad, category-crossing associative processes that underlie divergent thinking, then rhythmic walking would enhance this directly.

Second, the *cardiovascular arousal* hypothesis: walking modestly elevates heart rate and cerebral blood flow, which could enhance cognitive function through improved neural metabolic support. However, this predicts a general cognitive benefit, not the specific divergent-thinking benefit we observed. And it does not explain why convergent thinking was sometimes impaired.

Third, the *executive-offloading* hypothesis: walking requires some allocation of motor-executive resources (balance, navigation, obstacle avoidance), which draws executive control away from the internal task. This mild executive distraction may serve the same function as moderate noise and dim lighting — a disfluency-like effect that triggers abstract processing. But the treadmill condition requires minimal executive allocation for balance or navigation, yet still produced a large effect, which weakens this account.

Fourth, the *proprioceptive–cognitive coupling* hypothesis: the rhythmic motor prediction errors generated by each step (the small PE at each foot-ground contact) maintain the predictive processing system in an active but non-focused state. This is analogous to Schooler's "meta-aware mind-wandering" — the body is gently occupied, keeping the system alert, while the mind is free to wander. The continuous low-level PE from walking may prevent the system from settling into either full DMN dominance (passive rest) or full executive dominance (focused work), instead maintaining it in the creative coupling zone that Beaty describes.

I favor the fourth account — proprioceptive–cognitive coupling — because it explains both the treadmill effect (proprioceptive input is present) and the slight outdoor superiority (outdoor walking adds environmental novelty, which supplements the proprioceptive input with visual PE). It also connects to the PE framework more naturally than the alternatives.

The architectural implications are direct. Buildings that incorporate walking into the daily routine — long, gently curving corridors, indoor-outdoor circulation paths, walking meeting routes, garden labyrinths — should incidentally support creative incubation. Crucially, this is NOT the same as exercise: the creative benefit was observed at normal walking pace, not vigorous exercise. The architecture of gentle movement, not the architecture of fitness.

**Position**: CREA3 should model the motor-proprioceptive pathway as the primary mechanism for walking-creativity coupling, with environmental enrichment (nature, visual interest) as a secondary, additive channel. The template should specify that walking-pace movement is sufficient — the mechanism is rhythmic proprioceptive PE, not cardiovascular arousal.

### 1.9 Rex Jung — Transient Hypofrontality and the Phase Model

I want to reconcile my position with Benedek's, because we are actually closer than it appears. The transient hypofrontality thesis (Dietrich, 2004, ~1,000 citations; Dietrich & Kanso, 2010, ~600 citations) does NOT claim that all creativity involves reduced prefrontal function. It claims that the GENERATIVE phase of creative thinking — the initial production of novel ideas, analogies, and combinations — benefits from reduced DLPFC-mediated inhibition of unusual associations. The EVALUATIVE phase — assessing whether a novel idea is actually useful, appropriate, or valuable — requires full executive engagement.

Benedek's data showing increased prefrontal alpha during creative tasks reflect, in my interpretation, a *weighted average* across both phases within a single trial. The divergent thinking tasks used in EEG studies (alternative uses, creative writing prompts) inherently mix generation and evaluation in each response. If generation involves reduced DLPFC inhibition and evaluation involves increased executive engagement, the net EEG signal depends on the relative duration and intensity of each phase. The increased alpha Benedek observes may reflect the EVALUATIVE overlay on DMN-generated content — the executive system is working hard precisely because it is receiving unusual input from the generative process.

This phase model has direct architectural implications. If generation and evaluation are computationally distinct phases with different neural requirements, then the optimal creative workspace should support temporal alternation between environments conducive to each: generative spaces (open, moderately stimulating, movement-permitting, dim lighting, natural materials) and evaluative spaces (enclosed, quiet, well-lit, supportive of focused analytical work). The physical transition between these spaces may itself serve an incubation function.

**Position**: CREA1 should incorporate the generative-evaluative phase distinction as a temporal dimension. Environmental features should be mapped onto their differential effects on each phase, and the architectural recommendation should be PHASE-APPROPRIATE environments, not a single "creativity room."

### 1.10 Colin Ellard — Ecological Measurement and the Reality Check

My role is to keep this panel honest about what can actually be measured in real buildings. Let me identify the measurement challenges for each proposed template mechanism:

For DMN–executive coupling (CREA1): This can be measured with mobile EEG or fNIRS in principle, but the signal quality in ambulatory conditions is substantially degraded compared to laboratory settings. Source-localized network connectivity from mobile EEG is feasible with high-density montages (64+ channels) but not with consumer-grade systems (4–8 channels). My recommendation: design the template so that it makes predictions about proxy measures (alpha power over frontal cortex, prefrontal hemodynamic response via fNIRS) that are measurable with current ambulatory technology, even if full network-connectivity analysis requires laboratory validation.

For processing-style shift (CREA2): This is MORE measurable ecologically because the outcome — creative performance — can be assessed with standard divergent thinking tasks administered in different environmental conditions. The Guilford Alternative Uses Task, the Remote Associates Test (RAT), and the Wallach-Kogan similarity tasks can all be administered on a tablet while the participant is in the target environment. The challenge is controlling for order effects and expectation effects (people know they're being tested in a "creative space").

For incubation via walking (CREA3): Oppezzo already demonstrated the paradigm — alternate between creative tasks and walking periods, measure output before and after. The architectural extension would involve comparing different walking environments (featureless corridor vs. garden path vs. urban streetscape). I have conducted analogous studies in my lab using mobile psychophysiology during urban walks (Ellard, 2015, ~200 citations) and can confirm the approach is feasible.

My strongest recommendation: all three templates should include a "minimal ecological paradigm" — the simplest study design that would test the template's core prediction in a real building, using currently available technology. This was established as best practice in the earlier panels and I want to maintain it.

**Position**: Ecological measurement is feasible for all three proposed templates. The main challenge is DMN–executive coupling dynamics (CREA1), which requires either high-density mobile EEG or proxy measures. Processing-style (CREA2) and incubation (CREA3) are straightforwardly measurable.

## Part 2: Panel Discussion

### Thread 1: Resolving the Control Debate

**Opus** (moderating): We have an apparent disagreement between Benedek (creativity requires MORE executive control, redirected inward) and Jung (creativity benefits from transient hypofrontality, reduced prefrontal inhibition). Beaty's coupling framework and Christoff's two-dimensional model suggest a resolution. Can we reach consensus?

**Benedek**: To be precise, my claim is that the most creative outcomes — those scoring highest on both originality AND appropriateness — show increased prefrontal alpha. Ideas that are merely bizarre (original but inappropriate) do not. The executive system is required to make novel associations USEFUL, not just novel.

**Jung**: And my claim is that the initial generation of novel associations — the raw material for creative thought — benefits from relaxed inhibition. We are talking about different phases. The generative phase needs low filtering; the evaluative phase needs high filtering. The Benedek signal is the composite.

**Beaty**: I can adjudicate this with our time-resolved connectivity data (Beaty et al., 2019). In the first 5–10 seconds of a divergent thinking trial, we see stronger DMN activation with *weaker* executive coupling — consistent with Rex's generative phase. In the final seconds, as the person prepares their response, executive coupling increases sharply — consistent with Mathias's evaluative data. The temporal dynamics are the resolution.

**Christoff**: Which maps perfectly onto the deliberate × automatic space. Early generation = low deliberate, moderate automatic (associative flow with loose direction). Late evaluation = high deliberate, low automatic (executive screening of candidates). The creative PROCESS traverses the thought space; it does not occupy a single point.

**Schooler**: And the meta-awareness finding fits here too. The transition from generation to evaluation requires that the person NOTICE they have generated something worth evaluating. That is precisely what meta-awareness provides. Without it, generative wandering continues indefinitely without productive output.

**Opus**: Do I have consensus on the following formulation? Creative cognition involves a temporal sequence: (1) a generative phase characterized by DMN dominance with relaxed executive coupling, producing novel associations; (2) a salience-network-mediated transition triggered by meta-awareness of a promising candidate; (3) an evaluative phase characterized by strengthened executive coupling, assessing the novelty and appropriateness of the candidate. Environmental features can differentially support each phase.

**Beaty**: Yes, with the caveat that these phases can cycle rapidly — the sequence may repeat multiple times within a single creative episode, with generation and evaluation interleaving rather than occurring in a single long sequence.

**Benedek**: Agreed, and the cycling rate may itself be a creativity parameter — highly creative individuals may cycle faster.

**Jung**: Accepted. My hypofrontality claim applies specifically to phase 1.

**Ellard**: The phase cycling is important for measurement. We will not resolve sub-second phase dynamics with ambulatory EEG. What we CAN measure is whether different environments shift the *overall balance* toward generative or evaluative dominance — the time-averaged phase weighting.

### Thread 2: The Common Mechanism Question

**Opus**: Mehta's moderate noise, Steidle's dim lighting, and Meyers-Levy's high ceilings all appear to promote divergent thinking through mechanisms described as "processing disfluency," "perceived freedom," and "freedom-confinement priming" respectively. Are these three descriptions of the same underlying mechanism?

**Mehta**: The disfluency account predicts that ANY source of mild processing difficulty should shift construal to a more abstract level. Dim lighting and high ceilings are not sources of processing disfluency in the same way that noise is — noise directly impairs focal processing, while dim lighting and high ceilings do not impair processing in the same manner.

**Steidle**: I disagree slightly. Dim lighting does reduce the resolution of visual processing, which IS a form of disfluency. And the "freedom" mediator I identified may be the subjective EXPERIENCE of the disfluency-driven construal shift: when the brain zooms out to a more abstract processing level, the person experiences this as a sense of freedom or unconstrained thinking.

**Meyers-Levy**: The ceiling height effect is harder to explain as disfluency. High ceilings do not impair any processing. The mechanism is more naturally described as spatial prediction range — a wide prediction envelope invites abstract, relational processing, while a narrow envelope invites concrete, item-specific processing. This is a PE account, not a disfluency account.

**Christoff**: I think we need to distinguish two pathways. **Pathway A**: Processing disfluency → construal shift → abstract processing → divergent thinking. This is Mehta's mechanism, and it may operate through noise, visual clutter, and other sources of mild processing difficulty. **Pathway B**: Environmental spaciousness → expanded prediction envelope → abstract processing mode → divergent thinking. This is the ceiling height mechanism, operating through spatial PE. Both pathways converge on the same downstream effect (abstract processing facilitating divergent thinking) but through different upstream causes.

**Steidle**: Where does dim lighting fall?

**Christoff**: Dim lighting may operate through Pathway B primarily. By reducing the salience of the immediate visual environment, dim lighting effectively EXPANDS the psychological space — the mind is less tethered to specific visual objects and more free to range over internal representations. The reduced visual salience is functionally similar to increased spatial openness — both reduce the "pull" of the immediate environment.

**Beaty**: I want to add a **Pathway C** that is distinct from both. **Pathway C**: Reduced external salience → executive resources freed from external monitoring → redirected to internal creative search. This is the mechanism Benedek described, and it operates not through construal shift but through resource reallocation. Dim lighting, natural materials (MAT4 — preattentive processing that does not demand explicit monitoring), and thermal comfort all reduce the executive resources consumed by environmental management. These freed resources become available for internally directed creative processing.

**Opus**: So we may have three pathways converging on creativity:
- **Pathway A** (Disfluency): Noise, clutter → impaired focal processing → abstract construal → divergent thinking
- **Pathway B** (Spaciousness): High ceilings, open spaces, dim light → expanded prediction envelope → abstract processing mode → divergent thinking
- **Pathway C** (Resource Reallocation): Comfort, natural materials, reduced external salience → freed executive resources → internally directed creative search → divergent thinking

**Mehta**: These are not mutually exclusive. A coffee shop may engage all three: moderate noise (Pathway A), high ceilings if it is a spacious café (Pathway B), and comfortable seating with natural materials (Pathway C).

**Ellard**: This is testable. If the three pathways are independent, their effects should be additive — a space with ALL three features should produce a larger creativity benefit than a space with only one. If they converge on a single bottleneck (say, abstract construal), then combining them should show diminishing returns once the construal shift is maxed out. A factorial design manipulating noise level × ceiling height × lighting level × material naturalness could decompose this.

### Thread 3: The Divergent-Convergent Tradeoff

**Opus**: Several panelists have flagged that features promoting divergent thinking may impair convergent thinking. Is this a genuine tradeoff, and if so, what are the architectural implications?

**Mehta**: It is a genuine tradeoff in my data. Moderate noise enhanced divergent thinking (Alternative Uses Task, d ≈ 0.4–0.6) but slightly impaired convergent thinking (Remote Associates Test in some conditions, not others). High noise impaired both.

**Oppezzo**: Same pattern for walking. Divergent thinking doubled; convergent thinking on the Compound Remote Associates task did not improve and showed a trend toward impairment.

**Benedek**: This is predicted by the resource reallocation model. If creativity involves redirecting executive resources from external monitoring to internal search, then the SAME reallocation that helps divergent thinking (which benefits from broad, loosely directed internal search) would hurt convergent thinking (which requires focused, constrained problem-solving). You cannot optimize for both simultaneously.

**Jung**: Which is exactly why I proposed the phase model with DIFFERENT environments for different phases. The creative process is not all divergent thinking. A real creative project — designing a building, writing a paper, developing a product — alternates between divergent ideation (what are the possibilities?), convergent selection (which possibility is best?), elaboration (developing the selected idea), and evaluation (does it work?). Each phase has different cognitive demands and different optimal environments.

**Meyers-Levy**: This suggests that the TRANSITION between environments is itself important. Walking from a generative space to an evaluative space provides both the physical transition (incubation) and the environmental shift (from divergent-promoting to convergent-promoting features). The building's circulation becomes part of the creative process.

**Schooler**: And the transition spaces should be designed for incubation — low-demand, movement-permitting, soft-fascination environments that support the meta-aware mind-wandering that bridges generation and evaluation.

**Opus**: This yields a three-zone model of creative workspace:
1. **Generative zones**: Open, moderate sensory richness, dim ambient light, high ceilings, natural materials, optional ambient sound. Supports DMN-dominant ideation.
2. **Evaluative zones**: Enclosed, quiet, well-lit, uncluttered. Supports executive-dominant analysis and refinement.
3. **Transitional zones**: Walking paths, gardens, informal gathering spaces. Supports incubation, meta-aware mind-wandering, and the generative-to-evaluative transition.

### Thread 4: Individual Differences and Populations

**Opus**: Do these environmental effects operate the same way across populations?

**Jung**: The structural brain data suggest not. Individuals with thicker prefrontal cortex and stronger white matter integrity in the frontal-temporal tracts (arcuate fasciculus, uncinate fasciculus) show higher baseline creativity. For these individuals, environmental modulation may be less critical — they carry their creative infrastructure with them. For individuals with less robust creative brain architecture, environmental support may matter MORE.

**Beaty**: Our connectome data show the same pattern for connectivity. High-creative individuals already have strong DMN–executive coupling at rest. Low-creative individuals show weaker coupling that is more sensitive to environmental manipulation. The environmental effects reported by Mehta, Steidle, and Meyers-Levy may be driven disproportionately by the less-creative participants in their samples, for whom the environmental intervention tips the balance.

**Mehta**: This is plausible but not directly tested in our studies. We did not measure individual creative ability as a moderator. It is an important prediction: the noise × creativity interaction should show a larger noise benefit for less creative individuals.

**Christoff**: Cultural differences in the deliberate-constraint dimension are also worth noting. Cultures that emphasize obedience and rule-following (high "tightness" in Gelfand's framework; Gelfand et al., 2011, ~2,500 citations) may produce individuals whose default deliberate-constraint level is higher. For these individuals, environmental features that reduce deliberate constraint (perceived freedom, spaciousness) may have a LARGER creativity benefit, because they have further to move along the deliberate-constraint axis to reach the creative sweet spot.

**Ellard**: Age is another moderator. Frontal lobe development continues into the mid-20s, and frontal decline begins in the 50s. Children and older adults have less executive control to redirect, which might mean they benefit less from the resource-reallocation pathway (Pathway C) but potentially more from the spaciousness pathway (Pathway B), which operates through a more basic spatial-perceptual mechanism.

**Opus**: I note this parallels the population sensitivity finding in T45 (proactive/reactive control): populations with reduced executive capacity (elderly, stressed, ADHD) show different environmental sensitivity patterns. CREA templates should specify population moderators with the same rigor.

## Part 5: Testable Predictions

The panel identified six high-priority testable predictions that follow from the CREA templates and that were not derivable from the existing template library:

### Prediction 1 (from CREA1 + CREA2): The Creativity Goldilocks

**Claim**: Environments with moderate sensory richness produce higher creative output than either understimulating or overstimulating environments, through optimal calibration of salience-network switching rate. This is DISTINCT from the aesthetic Goldilocks (T2), which predicts preference rather than creative output.

**Protocol**: Participants complete divergent thinking tasks in three environments varying in multi-modal complexity: LOW (bare white room, silence, 1500 lux), MODERATE (natural materials, ambient nature sounds ~65 dB, dim light 200 lux, moderate visual detail), HIGH (complex urban scene, multi-source noise ~80 dB, bright varied lighting, high visual clutter).

**Predicted pattern**: Inverted-U for divergent thinking peaking at MODERATE. Linear DECREASE for convergent thinking (LOW best). The dissociation between the two tasks in the same environments is the critical test.

**Measurable via**: AUT + RAT scores; mobile EEG alpha variability as neural proxy for DMN–ECN coupling flexibility.

### Prediction 2 (from CREA2): Three-Pathway Independence Test

**Claim**: The disfluency pathway (A), spaciousness pathway (B), and resource-reallocation pathway (C) are partially independent and produce additive effects on divergent thinking.

**Protocol**: 2×2×2 factorial — ambient noise (quiet vs. 70 dB non-semantic) × ceiling height (2.5m vs. 4m) × lighting (1500 lux vs. 150 lux). All eight conditions.

**Predicted pattern**: If pathways are independent, main effects should be approximately additive with no significant three-way interaction. The "all divergent-promoting" condition (moderate noise + high ceiling + dim light) should produce approximately the sum of the three individual effects. If pathways share a bottleneck, the three-way interaction should be significant with diminishing returns when multiple pathways converge.

**Statistical note**: Minimum N ≈ 160 for adequate power on the three-way interaction (α = .05, power = .80, expected small interaction effect).

### Prediction 3 (from CREA3): Proprioceptive Primacy in Walking-Creativity

**Claim**: The walking-creativity benefit is primarily motor-proprioceptive, with environmental enrichment providing an additive but secondary contribution.

**Protocol**: Within-subject, four break conditions after creative task impasse: (a) seated rest in garden, (b) treadmill in blank room, (c) walking in featureless corridor, (d) walking in garden. Measure creative output improvement on return to task.

**Predicted rank order**: (d) garden walk > (c) corridor walk ≈ (b) treadmill > (a) seated garden. The CRITICAL comparison is (b) vs. (a): if treadmill > seated-in-garden, then movement trumps environment. The (c) vs. (b) comparison tests whether actual locomotion through space adds to treadmill-only proprioception.

### Prediction 4 (from CREA1): Phase-Appropriate Architecture

**Claim**: Matching environmental features to creative phase (generative vs. evaluative) produces better creative output than either environment alone sustained throughout.

**Protocol**: Participants complete a creative design task over 60 minutes. Three conditions: (a) generative-promoting environment throughout (open, dim, moderate noise); (b) evaluative-promoting environment throughout (enclosed, bright, quiet); (c) PHASE-MATCHED — first 20 min in generative environment, 10 min walking transition (CREA3 incubation), 20 min in evaluative environment, 10 min review.

**Predicted pattern**: Phase-matched (c) produces creative products rated higher on BOTH originality AND appropriateness than either (a) or (b). Condition (a) should produce more original but less refined products; (b) should produce more refined but less original products.

### Prediction 5 (from CREA2 + individual differences): Creativity × Environment Interaction

**Claim**: The environmental modulation of creative performance is larger for individuals with lower baseline creativity.

**Protocol**: Pre-screen participants on creative ability (Remote Associates Test, biographical creativity inventory). Administer divergent thinking task in divergent-promoting vs. neutral environment. Compute environment × baseline-creativity interaction.

**Predicted pattern**: Significant interaction with larger environmental benefit for lower-creativity individuals. High-creativity individuals show a ceiling effect. The environmental manipulation is an "equalizer" that provides external support for the network dynamics that high-creative individuals generate internally.

### Prediction 6 (from CREA3 + TP2): Threshold-Segmented Incubation

**Claim**: Walking paths with periodic threshold transitions (doorways, material changes, spatial compressions/expansions) produce more effective incubation than uniform paths of the same length, because thresholds create episodic boundaries (TP2) that segment the incubation period into distinct associative contexts.

**Protocol**: Two walking conditions of equal duration and distance: (a) uniform corridor, (b) corridor with 3–4 distinct spatial episodes (compression → expansion → compression → garden). Post-walk creative task performance compared.

**Predicted pattern**: Episodic walk (b) > uniform walk (a) for creative improvement. The mechanism: each threshold transition (TP2) clears the working memory context, allowing the associative system to explore a fresh region of the solution space in each segment.

**Priority D1: Multi-Channel Interaction Study**
The central unresolved question is whether Pathways A, B, and C are independent or share a bottleneck. The 2×2×2 factorial (Prediction 2) would resolve this and provide the interaction parameters needed for CREA2 calibration. This is the highest priority for CREA-II because it determines whether the multi-channel model is additive or sub-additive.
*Lead panelists*: Mehta, Steidle, Meyers-Levy
*Required measures*: Divergent and convergent task performance across 8 environmental conditions
*Expected deliverable*: Interaction coefficients for the three pathways; confirmation or refutation of shared-bottleneck hypothesis

**Priority D2: Phase-Resolved Environmental Modulation**
The generative-evaluative phase model (CREA1) predicts that different environments optimally support different phases, but the temporal dynamics have not been mapped onto environmental parameters. CREA-II should use time-resolved EEG during creative tasks in different environments to measure whether generative-promoting environments increase the duration of DMN-dominant phases and whether evaluative-promoting environments increase the duration of ECN-dominant phases.
*Lead panelists*: Beaty, Benedek, Ellard
*Required measures*: High-density mobile EEG with temporal connectivity analysis during creative tasks in different environments
*Expected deliverable*: Environmental modulation functions mapping features to phase duration and switching rate

**Priority D3: Walking Path Parameters**
CREA3 specifies that walking promotes incubation but does not parameterize the path. What is the optimal walking duration (5 min? 15 min? 30 min?), the optimal path complexity (straight, gently curved, labyrinthine?), the optimal threshold spacing (TP2), and the interaction with soft-fascination density? These parameters require systematic manipulation.
*Lead panelists*: Oppezzo, Schooler, Ellard
*Required measures*: Post-walk creative improvement as function of walk duration × path features
*Expected deliverable*: Dose-response curves for incubation walk parameters

**Priority D4: Individual Difference Moderators**
The prediction that low-creativity individuals benefit more from environmental support (Prediction 5) needs direct testing. Identifying which populations gain the most from creativity-supporting architecture would have immediate practical implications for educational and workplace design.
*Lead panelists*: Jung, Beaty, Christoff
*Required measures*: Baseline creativity assessment × environment interaction in large sample
*Expected deliverable*: Moderation coefficients for baseline creativity, age, cultural background

### BROADENING (Extending to New Domains)

**Priority B1: Collaborative Creativity**
All three CREA templates are specified for INDIVIDUAL creative cognition. Real creative work in architecture, science, and business is often collaborative. How do the environmental features modulate GROUP creative dynamics? The social facilitation and evaluation-apprehension literatures suggest that the presence of others may bias toward evaluative processing, which would conflict with generative-phase requirements. Conversely, social stimulation may provide the associative raw material (hearing others' ideas) that fuels individual generation.
*Required integration*: SOC1–SOC3 (social configuration), T48–T52 (social brain templates)
*New template target*: CREA4 — Collaborative Creative Environments

**Priority B2: Developmental and Aging Moderators**
Children and older adults have different prefrontal capacity, different baseline DMN–ECN coupling patterns, and potentially different sensitivity to environmental modulation of creativity. CREA templates should be calibrated for these populations, connecting to the planned Aging & Architecture and Child Development panels.
*Required integration*: Planned cross-cutting panels (Aging, Development)
*New template target*: Age-moderated parameters for CREA1–CREA3

**Priority B3: Digital and Virtual Creative Environments**
The remote-work revolution has shifted much creative work to virtual environments (video calls, shared digital whiteboards, VR collaboration spaces). Do the same environmental principles (spaciousness, moderate stimulation, movement) apply in virtual settings? Can a virtual environment provide the incubation support (CREA3) that a physical garden walk provides?
*Required new expertise*: VR researchers, human-computer interaction
*New template target*: Virtual environment parameters for CREA1–CREA2

## Part 9: Scope Exclusions

The following topics were discussed but explicitly excluded from the CREA-I templates:

1. **Artistic creativity vs. scientific creativity vs. everyday creativity**: The templates model the domain-general cognitive mechanisms (divergent thinking, incubation, network coupling) without distinguishing between creative domains. Domain-specific expertise effects are acknowledged as moderators but not formalized. A future panel might address whether architectural creativity (designing buildings) engages different environmental sensitivities than verbal creativity (writing), visual creativity (painting), or scientific creativity (experimental design).

2. **Flow states**: The Csikszentmihalyi flow state is related to but distinct from the creativity mechanisms modeled here. Flow involves sustained focused engagement with an intrinsically rewarding task — it is more aligned with the evaluative/elaboration phase than the generative phase. Flow states were discussed but deferred to a potential FLOW-I panel.

3. **Pharmacological and substance effects**: Caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, and psychedelics all modulate creative cognition, and their environmental interactions (coffee shop culture, bar culture) are architecturally relevant. Excluded because the neuromodulatory complexity is beyond the panel's scope.

4. **Musical and auditory creativity**: M-series templates (M1–M17) handle musical cognition. The interaction between ambient sound for creativity (CREA2 Pathway A) and musical creativity (composing, improvising) was noted but excluded — the optimal acoustic environment for creative listening is different from the optimal environment for creative thinking-in-general.

5. **Organizational and management factors**: Whether a workspace "promotes creativity" depends not only on physical features but on organizational culture, management style, and team dynamics. These social-organizational factors are beyond the scope of the mechanistic template library, which models individual and small-group cognition in physical environments.

6. **Long-term creative development**: The templates model acute effects of environmental features on creative processing. Whether sustained exposure to certain environments promotes long-term creative ability development (plasticity effects) is an important but separate question requiring longitudinal evidence that does not yet exist.
