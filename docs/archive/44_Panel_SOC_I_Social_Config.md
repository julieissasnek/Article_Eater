# PANEL SOC-I: SOCIAL CONFIGURATION AND THE ARCHITECTURE OF INTERPERSONAL SPACE
## Mechanistic Templates for How Architecture Shapes Social Prediction, Privacy, and Territory
## February 16, 2026 — Document 44

---

## Panel Charge

This panel addresses **A8 Social Configuration** (coverage ★★+ after SC-I). SC4 (Spatial Configuration and Social Encounter PE) already formalizes how spatial integration predicts encounter frequency and how visual connectivity determines social monitoring load. What SC4 does NOT formalize: (1) **proxemics and interpersonal distance** — how architecture mediates the distances at which people interact and the PE consequences of distance violations, (2) **privacy as a regulatory mechanism** — how architecture provides the gradient of social exposure from full public visibility to complete solitude and how occupants use this gradient to regulate social PE load, (3) **territorial affordance** — how spatial boundaries, furniture arrangement, and environmental markers create zones of behavioral ownership that reduce social unpredictability, and (4) **density perception and crowding** — how architecture modulates the relationship between physical density and perceived crowding through spatial design variables.

The panel is charged with producing 3–4 templates covering: (a) proxemic PE — how architecture mediates interpersonal distances and the prediction errors that arise from distance violations, (b) architectural privacy gradient — how buildings provide a spectrum of social exposure settings that enable privacy regulation, (c) territorial affordance — how spatial and material features create defensible zones that reduce social PE, and (d) density-to-crowding modulation — how architectural variables transform neutral physical density into either positive social energy or negative crowding stress. These must connect to SC4 (spatial-social encounter), T5 (threat/HPA), T29 (allostatic regulation), T48–T52 (social brain), T25 (directed attention), and T31 (auditory scene analysis).

## Panel Composition

### Proxemics and Interpersonal Space

**1. Edward T. Hall** (posthumous; formerly Northwestern University)
The founder of proxemics — the study of human spatial behavior and interpersonal distance (Hall, 1966, ~10,000 citations). His four-zone model — intimate (0–45cm), personal (45–120cm), social (1.2–3.6m), public (>3.6m) — remains the foundational framework for understanding how humans use physical distance to regulate social interaction. His central insight: interpersonal distance is not arbitrary but is a COMMUNICATION CHANNEL that conveys information about relationship type, interaction intensity, and emotional valence. Architecture determines which interpersonal distances are available, comfortable, and forced.
*Role*: Establishes the proxemic framework. Architecture creates the spatial conditions that constrain interpersonal distances: a narrow corridor forces intimate-zone proximity between strangers; a large atrium allows public-zone separation; a conference table fixes social-zone distance; a bench invites personal-zone proximity. Each of these architectural conditions generates a proxemic prediction — the brain anticipates the appropriate distance for the social context — and the match or mismatch between predicted and actual distance produces proxemic PE.

**2. Robert Sommer** (University of California, Davis)
Pioneer of personal space research in architectural settings (Sommer, 1969, ~3,000 citations). His studies of seating arrangement, furniture placement, and spatial behavior in institutional settings (libraries, hospitals, waiting rooms) demonstrated that the physical arrangement of furniture is a primary determinant of social interaction patterns. His key contribution: the distinction between SOCIOFUGAL design (arrangements that discourage interaction — rows of chairs facing forward) and SOCIOPETAL design (arrangements that encourage interaction — chairs arranged in circles, tables for small groups). Architecture does not merely permit social interaction; it actively channels, encourages, or suppresses it through furniture geometry.
*Role*: Provides the furniture-scale proxemic analysis. Hall's zones describe the interpersonal distances at which different interaction types occur. Sommer's research specifies how architectural and furniture design determines which zones are available. A waiting room with chairs bolted to the floor in rows is sociofugal — it forces parallel orientation and public-zone distance, discouraging conversation. The same room with moveable chairs around small tables is sociopetal — it enables face-to-face orientation at personal-social zone distance, facilitating interaction. The designer controls the social prediction landscape by controlling furniture geometry.

**3. Dacher Keltner** (University of California, Berkeley)
Research on social emotion, power dynamics, and nonverbal communication (Keltner & Haidt, 1999, ~2,000 citations; Keltner, 2009, ~500 citations). His work on approach and inhibition in social contexts demonstrates that social behavior is regulated by predictions about social safety — approach systems engage when social interaction is predicted to be rewarding; inhibition systems engage when it is predicted to be threatening. Physical environment modulates these predictions: open, well-lit, legible spaces reduce social threat prediction and increase approach; enclosed, dark, illegible spaces increase social threat prediction and increase inhibition.
*Role*: Provides the social-emotional mechanism. Hall and Sommer describe the SPATIAL variables; Keltner describes the EMOTIONAL responses. Proxemic violations (someone too close, or an architecture that forces too-close proximity) activate the behavioral inhibition system — the same threat-detection system that T5 formalizes at the individual level. Conversely, architecture that permits comfortable social distances and provides choice in social exposure activates the approach system — the prediction that social interaction will be manageable and potentially rewarding.

### Privacy Regulation

**4. Irwin Altman** (posthumous; formerly University of Utah)
The theorist of privacy regulation — the process by which individuals control their accessibility to others (Altman, 1975, ~3,000 citations). His central framework: privacy is not a state (private vs. public) but a DIALECTICAL PROCESS of continuously adjusting the degree of social accessibility to match the desired level. When actual accessibility exceeds desired accessibility (too much social exposure), the person experiences crowding. When actual accessibility falls below desired accessibility (too much isolation), the person experiences loneliness. Architecture provides the mechanisms for privacy regulation: doors, walls, acoustic barriers, visual screens, spatial distance, and social-convention markers (a closed door, a turned back, headphones).
*Role*: Establishes privacy as a PE phenomenon. The brain maintains a prediction of desired social accessibility level — a target state that varies with mood, task, personality, and context. Architecture provides the REGULATORY MECHANISMS by which the person adjusts actual accessibility toward the desired level. When architectural privacy mechanisms are adequate (the person can close a door, move to a quiet corner, adjust a partition), regulation succeeds and social PE is minimal. When they are inadequate (open-plan office with no privacy options, shared dormitory room, glass-walled meeting room), regulation fails and the discrepancy between desired and actual accessibility produces sustained social PE → crowding stress even at moderate physical density.

**5. Gary Evans** (Cornell University, Department of Design and Environmental Analysis)
Environmental psychologist specializing in the health effects of environmental stressors, including density, noise, and lack of privacy (Evans & Cohen, 1987, ~1,500 citations; Evans & McCoy, 1998, ~800 citations). His work demonstrates that lack of architectural control over social exposure is a chronic stressor that produces measurable cortisol elevation, attentional fatigue, and reduced helpfulness. His key finding: the relationship between density and stress is mediated by PERCEIVED CONTROL — high density with high control (choice of where to go, ability to withdraw) produces minimal stress, while moderate density with low control (no escape, no privacy, no withdrawal option) produces significant stress.
*Role*: Provides the stress-physiology evidence. Evans's work establishes that privacy failure is not merely uncomfortable — it is a chronic physiological stressor that operates through the HPA axis (T5) and produces allostatic load (T29). The architectural variables that mediate this are specific: visual access (can others see you?), acoustic separation (can others hear you?), territorial markers (do you have a defined personal zone?), and withdrawal options (can you leave?). Each of these is a design variable under the architect's control.

**6. Jungsoo Kim and Richard de Dear** (University of Sydney, Indoor Environmental Quality Laboratory)
Researchers on indoor environmental quality and occupant satisfaction, with extensive work on open-plan offices (Kim & de Dear, 2013, ~1,000 citations). Their large-sample study of the CBE database demonstrated that open-plan offices consistently produce LOWER satisfaction than private offices, and that the primary driver of dissatisfaction is lack of acoustic and visual privacy — not temperature, lighting, or spatial quality. This finding directly challenges the social-encounter hypothesis that motivates open-plan design: whatever gains in casual encounter SC4 predicts, they are overwhelmed by the privacy losses that occupants experience.
*Role*: Provides the canonical case study. The open-plan office is the architectural setting where social configuration most dramatically affects occupant wellbeing — and where it most dramatically fails. Kim and de Dear's data demonstrate that the social-encounter benefits of spatial integration (SC4) must be balanced against the privacy costs of visual and acoustic exposure. The template system needs to formalize this trade-off: SC4 describes the encounter benefit of integration; SOC-I must describe the privacy cost.

### Territory and Defensible Space

**7. Oscar Newman** (posthumous; formerly Columbia University)
Architect and planner who developed the theory of defensible space — the idea that physical design of residential environments can reduce crime by creating zones of territorial influence (Newman, 1972, ~4,000 citations). His framework identifies four design elements that create defensible space: (a) territorial definition (clear demarcation of private, semi-private, semi-public, and public zones), (b) natural surveillance (sightlines from private spaces to shared spaces), (c) image (the building's appearance signals care and occupancy), and (d) milieu (the surrounding neighborhood context). Although Newman's primary focus was crime reduction, his territorial hierarchy applies broadly: clear territorial definition reduces social unpredictability and social PE at all scales.
*Role*: Provides the territorial gradient framework. Newman's four-zone territorial hierarchy (private → semi-private → semi-public → public) is a spatial instantiation of Altman's privacy gradient. Architecture that provides clear territorial transitions — a front door, a porch, a shared landing, a common courtyard, a public street — creates a predictable gradient of social density and behavioral expectation. At each level, the occupant can predict who will be present, what behavior is appropriate, and how much social monitoring is required. Architecture that collapses the territorial hierarchy (a front door opening directly onto a public sidewalk, a private bedroom opening onto a shared corridor) forces abrupt transitions between social PE regimes → unpredictable social encounters → sustained vigilance.

**8. Erving Goffman** (posthumous; formerly University of Pennsylvania)
Sociologist whose dramaturgical framework describes social life as performance in defined settings (Goffman, 1959, ~80,000 citations; Goffman, 1963, ~15,000 citations). His concepts of "front stage" (where public performance occurs), "back stage" (where people prepare, rest, and drop the performance), and "social frame" (the shared definition of what kind of interaction is appropriate in a given setting) are directly architectural: architecture creates the stages, defines the transitions between front and back, and signals through material and spatial cues what social frame is in effect.
*Role*: Provides the social-performance framework. Architecture determines where front-stage performance is required (lobby, meeting room, reception desk) and where back-stage recovery is available (private office, break room, restroom). The brain's social prediction system (T48–T52) generates different predictions in front-stage vs. back-stage settings: front-stage requires sustained impression management (high social PE load); back-stage permits relaxation of impression management (low social PE load). Buildings that provide clear front/back transitions and adequate back-stage space enable social performance cycling. Buildings that provide only front-stage space (all-glass office, open-plan workspace, surveillance-designed hospital) deny the recovery that back-stage provides → sustained social PE → Goffman's "presentation fatigue."

### Social Neuroscience of Shared Space

**9. Chris Frith** (University College London, Wellcome Centre for Human Neuroimaging)
Neuroscientist whose work on the social brain demonstrates that the presence of others fundamentally changes neural processing — the brain in a social context operates differently from the brain in isolation (Frith & Frith, 2007, ~3,000 citations). His "we-mode" hypothesis proposes that the brain shifts into a qualitatively different processing mode when co-presence is detected: mentalizing systems activate, mirror neuron systems engage, and social prediction circuits generate continuous models of others' intentions, beliefs, and actions. This social processing load is automatic and unavoidable in co-present situations.
*Role*: Provides the neural cost of co-presence. The brain's social processing is NOT optional — it engages automatically whenever others are detected. In a private office, social processing circuits are quiescent. In an open-plan office with 30 visible co-workers, these circuits run continuously, generating predictions about 30 agents' actions, intentions, and attention. This is a COGNITIVE COST that architecture imposes by determining visual and acoustic access to other people. The social brain templates (T48–T52) describe the mechanisms; Frith quantifies the neural cost of co-presence, which provides the mechanistic basis for privacy as attention preservation.

**10. Robin Dunbar** (University of Oxford, Department of Experimental Psychology)
Evolutionary anthropologist who proposed that human social group size is constrained by neocortical processing capacity — the "social brain hypothesis" (Dunbar, 1993, ~7,000 citations; Dunbar, 1998, ~3,000 citations). His finding that humans can maintain approximately 150 stable social relationships (Dunbar's number) reflects the cognitive cost of social tracking. His layered model of social networks — 5 intimate, 15 close, 50 friends, 150 acquaintances — maps onto architectural scales: a household (5), a floor or wing (15), a department (50), a building (150).
*Role*: Provides the social-scale framework. Architecture operates at specific scales within Dunbar's hierarchy: a private office supports the 5 (intimate team); a shared floor supports the 15 (close colleagues); a building supports the 150 (acquaintance network). When architecture forces social exposure beyond the occupant's current relational layer — a first-year employee in an open-plan office visually exposed to 200 strangers — the social prediction system is overwhelmed: there are more agents than the social brain can model, producing diffuse social PE and the experience of anonymous crowding. Architecture that scales social exposure to relational layer reduces social PE to manageable levels.

## Position Statements

### PROXEMICS AND INTERPERSONAL DISTANCE

**Position Statement 1: Edward T. Hall — The Hidden Dimension of Architecture**

Every architectural setting establishes a proxemic regime — a range of interpersonal distances that it makes comfortable, possible, or unavoidable (Hall, 1966, ~10,000 citations). The four-zone model is not arbitrary; each zone corresponds to a distinct sensory regime. In the intimate zone (0–45cm), thermal radiation is detectable, breathing is audible, body odor is present, and fine facial detail is visible — a full multi-sensory social signal. In the personal zone (45–120cm), thermal cues are lost but speech at normal volume and facial expression are clear. In the social zone (1.2–3.6m), speech must be slightly raised and facial microexpressions are difficult to read. In the public zone (>3.6m), only gross body posture and loud speech are available.

Architecture determines which zones are available in any setting. A library carrel enforces personal-zone isolation. A lecture hall establishes public-zone separation between speaker and audience. An elevator forces intimate-zone proximity among strangers — a proxemic violation that produces the characteristic elevator behavior: gaze aversion, body stiffening, minimized movement. The social prediction system generates an expected proxemic zone based on context (relationship type, setting type, cultural norms), and when architecture forces a different zone, proxemic PE results.

The PE is bidirectional. Too-close proximity (elevator, crowded subway, narrow corridor) produces threat-related PE — the intimate zone is normally reserved for trusted intimates, and its invasion by strangers activates the behavioral inhibition system (Keltner). Too-distant separation when closeness is desired (a meeting room so large that participants are pushed to social-zone distance, a hospital room where the visitor's chair is far from the bed) produces approach-frustration PE — the social system predicts intimate or personal zone interaction and the architecture prevents it. Architecture mediates interpersonal distance, and the quality of the mediation determines the quality of social experience.

**Position Statement 2: Robert Sommer — Furniture as Social Architecture**

The spatial variables that most directly affect social interaction operate at the furniture scale, not the building scale (Sommer, 1969, ~3,000 citations). The orientation of chairs determines whether occupants face each other (conversation-facilitating), sit side by side (companion arrangement), sit at angles (casual-flexible arrangement), or face away (isolation arrangement). The distance between seats determines which proxemic zone the interaction occupies. The height of partitions determines visual access. The fixity of furniture determines whether occupants can adjust the spatial arrangement to match their social needs.

The sociofugal-sociopetal dimension is a continuous variable under design control. At the sociofugal extreme: rows of fixed forward-facing seats (airport gate, lecture hall, waiting room) — these minimize face-to-face contact, enforce parallel orientation, and produce social isolation even at moderate density. At the sociopetal extreme: round tables with moveable chairs, conversation pits, L-shaped seating arrangements — these maximize face-to-face orientation, enable personal-zone proximity, and facilitate the turn-taking rhythms of conversation.

The PE framework applies: the brain generates a social prediction based on context (I am in a café; I expect to be able to sit across from my companion at personal-zone distance). When furniture matches this prediction (a small table with two facing chairs), social PE is zero and the social interaction proceeds fluently. When furniture violates it (a long counter with side-by-side stools, too far apart for comfortable conversation), social PE results — the interaction is awkward, strained, or abandoned. Furniture is the finest-grained instrument of architectural social design.

**Position Statement 3: Dacher Keltner — Approach and Inhibition in Architectural Space**

The social brain operates two competing systems: approach (mediated by left prefrontal activation, dopaminergic reward circuits) and inhibition (mediated by right prefrontal activation, amygdala, HPA axis) (Keltner et al., 2003, ~1,500 citations). Approach is activated when social interaction is predicted to be safe and potentially rewarding. Inhibition is activated when social interaction is predicted to be threatening or uncontrollable. Architecture tilts the balance between these systems through spatial variables that modulate social safety prediction.

Variables that promote approach: adequate interpersonal distance (personal or social zone for the relationship type), multiple exit routes (the occupant can withdraw), visual legibility (the occupant can see who else is present), territorial markers (the occupant has a defined personal zone), and acoustic privacy (conversations cannot be overheard by non-participants). Variables that promote inhibition: forced intimate-zone proximity with strangers, no exit options, visual illegibility (others can see you but you cannot see them — the panopticon condition), territorial ambiguity (no clear "your space"), and acoustic transparency (everything is overheard).

The open-plan office activates the inhibition system on almost every variable: forced social-zone proximity with dozens of semi-strangers, no withdrawal option during work hours, full visual transparency (everyone can see your screen, your facial expression, your posture), no territorial definition (hot-desking removes even desk ownership), and full acoustic transparency (every phone call and conversation is shared). This is the architectural embodiment of chronic social inhibition — and it explains why occupant satisfaction data (Kim & de Dear, 2013) consistently shows open-plan dissatisfaction despite the social encounter benefits that SC4 describes.

### PRIVACY REGULATION

**Position Statement 4: Irwin Altman — Privacy as Dynamic Regulation, Not Static State**

Privacy is not a binary condition. It is a continuous regulatory process — the ongoing adjustment of actual social accessibility toward a desired level (Altman, 1975, ~3,000 citations). The desired level fluctuates: after a period of intense social interaction, the desired level drops (the person wants solitude); after a period of isolation, it rises (the person wants company). This fluctuation operates on multiple timescales: minutes (needing a break after a long meeting), hours (the daily rhythm of social energy), days (introvert recharge cycles), and seasonally (festive seasons increase social appetite).

Architecture provides the REGULATORY MECHANISMS. Doors (can be opened or closed), partitions (can be adjusted), spatial distance (can be increased by moving), acoustic barriers (walls, white noise), visual screens (curtains, frosted glass, vegetation), and social conventions (a closed door means "do not disturb") — all are architectural instruments for privacy regulation. When the regulatory mechanisms are adequate, the occupant can match actual accessibility to desired accessibility, and social PE stays near zero. When they are inadequate — and the canonical case is the open-plan office, where NONE of the regulatory mechanisms are available — the occupant cannot regulate, and the chronic discrepancy between desired and actual accessibility produces sustained social PE → crowding stress → allostatic load (T29).

The critical insight for the PE framework: CROWDING IS NOT DENSITY. Crowding is the DISCREPANCY between desired and actual social accessibility. A busy market square can be high-density without feeling crowded because the occupant CHOSE to be there and can LEAVE (high control, high choice → adequate regulation). An open-plan office can feel crowded at moderate density because the occupant cannot regulate visual or acoustic exposure (low control, no choice → regulation failure). Density is a physical property of the space; crowding is a PE property of the person-space relationship.

**Position Statement 5: Gary Evans — Privacy Failure as Chronic Stressor**

The health consequences of architectural privacy failure are measurable and significant (Evans & Cohen, 1987, ~1,500 citations; Evans, 2003, ~1,000 citations). In studies of residential density, dormitory room sharing, and open-plan office work, sustained privacy failure produces: elevated cortisol (HPA axis activation, T5), reduced perceived control, decreased helpfulness and prosocial behavior, impaired task performance on complex cognitive tasks, and increased social withdrawal (paradoxically — the person copes with too much social exposure by psychologically disengaging from social interaction entirely).

The architectural variables that mediate density → stress are specific and designable. Visual access: can others see you? (Partition height, office layout, glass vs. opaque walls.) Acoustic separation: can others hear you? (Wall Sound Transmission Class, background masking, distance.) Territorial definition: do you have a space that is recognizably "yours"? (Assigned desk, personal storage, boundary markers.) Withdrawal options: can you leave? (Break rooms, outdoor access, quiet rooms, phone booths.) Each variable independently contributes to perceived control, and perceived control mediates the density-stress relationship.

The design implication is that social wellbeing in buildings is not primarily about reducing density — it is about providing the architectural mechanisms that enable privacy regulation at whatever density the program requires. A hospital ward at high density can function well if patients have curtains (visual privacy), acoustic separation between beds, personal bedside territory, and access to a day room (withdrawal option). The same ward without these features produces the same density-stress that Evans's research documents.

### TERRITORY AND DEFENSIBLE SPACE

**Position Statement 6: Oscar Newman — The Territorial Gradient**

Crime and social disorder in residential environments are strongly predicted by the clarity of the territorial hierarchy — the gradient from fully private to fully public space (Newman, 1972, ~4,000 citations). Buildings with clear territorial transitions (front door → private hallway → shared landing → common stairway → lobby → street) create zones of increasing social density and decreasing personal control, with each zone clearly demarcated and each transition signaled by architectural cues (a door, a change of material, a change of level, a change of light). In these buildings, residents take ownership of the semi-private and semi-public zones (maintaining them, monitoring them, personalizing them), and social behavior in each zone is predictable because the territorial norms are clear.

Buildings that collapse the territorial hierarchy (a front door opening directly onto a public corridor shared by 200 units, no intermediate semi-private zones) produce territorial ambiguity — no one takes ownership of the shared space, social norms are unclear, and behavior is unpredictable. The social prediction system (T48–T52) cannot generate accurate predictions when territorial cues are absent: Who belongs in this space? What behavior is appropriate? Who is responsible for maintenance and monitoring? The result is diffuse social PE, social withdrawal from shared spaces, and the deterioration of the commons.

For the PE framework: the territorial gradient is a SOCIAL PREDICTION GRADIENT. In your private room, you predict zero uninvited social encounters — and this prediction is confirmed if you have a lockable door. On your shared landing (4–6 units), you predict encountering neighbors you know — and this prediction is confirmed if the landing is clearly demarcated and exclusively accessed by those units. In the lobby, you predict encountering building residents — confirmed if the lobby is access-controlled. At the street, you predict encountering strangers — and adjust your social prediction system accordingly. Each territorial level carries a different social PE regime, and clear territorial design enables the social brain to calibrate its predictions accurately at each level.

**Position Statement 7: Erving Goffman — Front Stage, Back Stage, and Architectural Performance**

All social interaction is performance, and all performance requires staging (Goffman, 1959, ~80,000 citations). Architecture provides the stages: a courtroom has a clear front stage (the bench, the bar, the witness stand) and a clear back stage (chambers, the jury room, the holding area). A restaurant has front stage (the dining room) and back stage (the kitchen). A home has front stage (the living room, the dining room — where guests are received) and back stage (the bedroom, the bathroom — where performance is not required).

The distinction is cognitively fundamental. Front-stage behavior requires continuous impression management — monitoring one's own appearance, speech, and behavior while simultaneously monitoring others' reactions. This is high social PE load: the brain generates predictions about social reception and continuously tests them against feedback. Back-stage behavior permits dropping the performance — relaxing posture, unmonitoring speech, engaging in behaviors that would be inappropriate in public. This is low social PE load: the brain reduces social prediction demands to a minimum.

Architecture that provides clear front/back transitions and adequate back-stage space enables a sustainable performance cycle. Architecture that eliminates back stage — the all-glass office, the open-plan workspace, the dormitory room that doubles as social space, the hospital room with no curtain — forces sustained front-stage performance with no recovery. Goffman's "presentation fatigue" is the exhaustion of sustained social PE without back-stage relief. This connects directly to Altman's privacy regulation: back stage IS the private setting where the desired accessibility level can drop to zero.

### SOCIAL NEUROSCIENCE OF SHARED SPACE

**Position Statement 8: Chris Frith — The Automatic Cost of Co-Presence**

The social brain is not a voluntary system. When other people are detected — through vision, hearing, or even knowledge of their presence — social processing circuits activate automatically (Frith & Frith, 2007, ~3,000 citations). The medial prefrontal cortex generates models of others' mental states (mentalizing). The mirror neuron system simulates others' actions. The temporal-parietal junction tracks others' perspectives. The amygdala evaluates social threat. These processes run in parallel, continuously, and without conscious initiation.

The implication for architecture is profound: every person visible or audible in the occupant's environment imposes an automatic cognitive cost — the social prediction system must generate and maintain a model for each detected agent. In a private office (zero visible others), social processing load is near zero. In an open-plan office with 30 visible colleagues, the social brain maintains 30 agent models simultaneously — tracking their locations, actions, gaze directions, and (when audible) conversational content. This is not a choice; it is the architecture of the social brain.

This automatic social processing competes with non-social cognitive resources. The executive function system (T25, directed attention) has limited capacity. Social processing diverts capacity from task-relevant processing. This is the mechanistic explanation for the well-documented productivity costs of open-plan offices: the social brain, activated by ubiquitous co-presence, consumes attentional resources that would otherwise support work.

**Position Statement 9: Robin Dunbar — Social Scale and Architectural Scale**

The human social brain evolved to manage social groups of approximately 150 individuals (Dunbar, 1993, ~7,000 citations). But this 150 is not a single undifferentiated group — it is organized in concentric layers: ~5 intimate contacts (support clique), ~15 close friends (sympathy group), ~50 friends (affinity group), ~150 acquaintances (active network). Each layer requires different cognitive resources: maintaining an intimate relationship requires frequent, extended, emotionally rich interaction; maintaining an acquaintance requires only periodic recognition and minimal social prediction updating.

Architecture maps onto these layers at specific scales. A private office or small meeting room supports the 5 (intimate team): face-to-face interaction at personal zone, full sensory access, extended collaborative work. A shared pod or open bay supports the 15 (close colleagues): visual access, casual conversation, shared awareness of activity. A floor or wing supports the 50 (broader team): periodic encounter in shared spaces, recognition, light social tracking. A building supports the 150 (organizational acquaintance network): occasional encounter in lobbies, cafeterias, corridors — the "designed collision" that Allen and Henn (2007) document as productive.

The PE framework predicts that architecture produces optimal social experience when the scale of social exposure matches the appropriate Dunbar layer for the activity. A team meeting (5-person intimate interaction) in a 200-person open floor fails because the social prediction demands (30+ irrelevant agent models running simultaneously) overwhelm the resources available for the 5-person collaborative task. The same meeting in a small room succeeds because the social brain only processes the 5 relevant agents. Architectural scaling of social exposure — matching room size and visual-acoustic boundaries to Dunbar layers — is the spatial equivalent of appropriate proxemic distance: it matches the social prediction environment to the social processing capacity.

## Cross-Examination and Debate

### Debate 1: Is the Open-Plan Office Simply Bad Design, or Does It Represent a Genuine Trade-Off?

**Challenge**: SC4 demonstrates that spatial integration increases encounter frequency and potentially innovation. Kim and de Dear demonstrate that open-plan produces dissatisfaction. Is this a design failure or an inherent trade-off?

**Evans**: The research is unambiguous on the stress side. Lack of acoustic and visual privacy produces cortisol elevation, attentional fatigue, and reduced complex task performance. These are not subjective complaints — they are physiological measurements. Any encounter benefit must be weighed against these costs.

**Frith**: And the encounter benefit may be illusory. The automatic social processing cost of co-presence means that even "casual encounters" in open-plan environments consume cognitive resources. The person who has a brief creative exchange at the water cooler has also been running 30 background agent models for the preceding two hours. The net cognitive budget may be negative.

**Sommer**: But the design failure is specific. Open-plan as typically implemented — a large undifferentiated space with uniform furniture — collapses ALL of Altman's privacy regulation mechanisms simultaneously. This is not an inherent property of open spatial configuration. An open plan with acoustic pods, visual screens, territorial markers, and withdrawal spaces could preserve much of the encounter benefit while restoring the regulatory mechanisms. The problem is not "open" — it is "undifferentiated."

**Dunbar**: The scale is also wrong. A 200-person open floor exposes each occupant to vastly more social agents than the task requires. A more appropriate design would cluster 5–15 people in acoustically and visually semi-bounded pods (matching Dunbar's sympathy group), with shared spaces between pods for inter-group encounter (matching the 50 and 150 layers). This preserves encounter at the larger scales while bounding social processing load at the working scale.

**Panel consensus**: **The open-plan office represents a genuine trade-off, but current implementations catastrophically over-weight encounter at the expense of privacy.** The solution is not to return to private offices for all, but to provide a GRADIENT of social exposure settings within the same floor plate — from private (1-person, full acoustic/visual enclosure) through pod (5–15 people, acoustic boundary, visual semi-boundary) through commons (full visual access, designed encounter). The SC4 encounter benefits operate at the commons and inter-pod scales; the privacy benefits operate at the private and pod scales. Good social design provides BOTH within the same building.

### Debate 2: Is Territorial Behavior Innate or Culturally Constructed?

**Challenge**: Newman's defensible space and Hall's proxemic zones assume universal human territorial behavior. But interpersonal distances and territorial norms vary enormously across cultures. Is the PE framework culture-dependent?

**Hall**: The proxemic zones are culturally calibrated — the DISTANCES vary across cultures (Latin cultures use closer interpersonal distances than Northern European cultures) — but the EXISTENCE of distance regulation is universal. Every culture has norms for intimate, personal, social, and public distance; the numerical values differ but the four-zone structure holds. The PE mechanism is culture-independent: the brain predicts the appropriate distance for the context, and violation produces PE. The specific distance predicted depends on cultural calibration.

**Goffman**: Similarly, the front-stage/back-stage distinction is universal but the CONTENT of performance varies. Every culture requires impression management in some settings and permits relaxation in others; what counts as "front stage" and what behaviors require monitoring are culturally determined. The architectural implication — that buildings must provide both front and back stage — holds universally, but which spaces serve which function varies culturally.

**Newman**: Territorial behavior is also universal at the functional level. Every human group establishes zones of behavioral ownership — private, shared-with-known-others, shared-with-strangers. The architectural mechanisms differ (a wall, a hedge, a painted threshold, a change in paving material), but the regulatory function is constant: territorial markers reduce social unpredictability by signaling who belongs and what behavior is expected.

**Panel consensus**: **The PE mechanisms are universal; the calibration parameters are culture-specific.** Architecture must provide proxemic regulation, privacy regulation, and territorial definition in all cultures. But the SPECIFIC distances, boundary types, and exposure norms must be calibrated to the cultural context. A PE-based social template should specify the MECHANISM (prediction of appropriate distance → architecture mediates → match or mismatch → PE) while noting that the PARAMETERS (which distances, which boundaries, which norms) are culture-dependent moderators.

### Debate 3: Can Architecture Really Cause Loneliness, or Is That an Individual Difference?

**Challenge**: Altman notes that privacy failure includes ISOLATION — actual accessibility below desired. Can architecture cause loneliness, or is loneliness a psychological trait independent of design?

**Altman**: Architecture absolutely contributes. A person who desires social contact but lives in a building with no shared spaces, no casual encounter opportunities, and no semi-public zones where spontaneous interaction can occur is architecturally isolated. The building's design has eliminated the spatial conditions that enable casual social connection. This is particularly acute for elderly residents, new community members, and people with limited mobility — precisely the populations for whom architectural encounter facilitation is most important.

**Dunbar**: The social brain needs maintenance — relationships that are not refreshed through regular interaction decay within weeks to months. Architecture that provides no encounter opportunities starves the social brain of the input it needs to maintain relationships. A high-rise apartment building with no shared space between the unit door and the street provides ZERO encounter opportunities — residents may live for years without meeting their neighbors. The architectural design has produced social isolation by eliminating the semi-public territorial zones where casual encounter occurs.

**Evans**: The empirical evidence is clear for the opposite direction: involuntary high-density exposure causes social withdrawal. But the isolation direction — that lack of encounter opportunity causes loneliness — is harder to test because loneliness has so many confounds. However, interventions that ADD shared social spaces to isolated housing environments DO produce increased social interaction and reduced reported loneliness, suggesting that the spatial variable is causal.

**Panel consensus**: **Architecture does not cause loneliness in isolation, but it can create the spatial conditions that make loneliness much more likely or much less likely.** Buildings with casual encounter zones (Newman's semi-public spaces, Sommer's sociopetal arrangements) lower the barrier to spontaneous social interaction. Buildings without such zones raise the barrier. For populations vulnerable to isolation (elderly, disabled, newcomers), architectural encounter facilitation may be the critical difference. The PE framework: the person predicts social encounter opportunity; architecture that provides none produces social-appetitive PE (unfulfilled desire for contact).

## Synthesis: Architecture as Social Prediction Environment

The three templates produced by this panel establish that architecture is not merely a container for social life — it is the primary instrument by which social prediction is structured, calibrated, and regulated. Every building creates a social prediction environment: a set of spatial conditions that determines who encounters whom, at what distance, with what level of privacy, and within what territorial framework.

**SOC1 (Proxemic PE)** establishes the micro-scale mechanism: architecture mediates interpersonal distance, and the match or mismatch between architecturally imposed distance and socially appropriate distance produces proxemic PE. This operates bidirectionally — too-close proximity (the elevator, the open-plan desk) activates behavioral inhibition, while too-far separation (the cavernous meeting room, the distant hospital visitor chair) frustrates approach. Sommer's furniture-scale analysis shows that the finest-grained social design operates at the scale of chair orientation and table distance, not at the scale of building configuration.

**SOC2 (Privacy Gradient)** establishes the regulatory mechanism: privacy is a dynamic process of matching actual social accessibility to desired accessibility, and architecture provides the regulatory instruments. The five privacy mechanisms (visual, acoustic, spatial withdrawal, territorial, convention markers) are all under design control. When they are adequate, occupants maintain social homeostasis. When they are absent — as in the canonical open-plan office — regulation fails and the chronic discrepancy produces sustained social PE that registers as cortisol elevation, attentional fatigue, and reduced satisfaction. The critical insight is that CROWDING IS NOT DENSITY — it is the PE that results from regulation failure. This reframing has direct design consequences: the solution to crowding is not reducing density but restoring regulatory mechanisms.

**SOC3 (Territorial Affordance)** establishes the macro-scale structure: architecture creates a territorial gradient from private to public, each zone carrying different social prediction demands, processing loads, and behavioral norms. Clear territorial design enables the social brain to calibrate its predictions accurately at each level. Goffman's front-stage/back-stage framework maps directly onto this gradient: front-stage zones (public, semi-public) require impression management and incur high social processing cost; back-stage zones (private, semi-private) permit performance recovery and reduce social processing to near zero. Buildings must provide both, with clear architectural transitions between them.

The relationship between this panel and SC-I is complementary rather than overlapping. SC4 describes HOW spatial integration creates encounter opportunity — the mechanics of who meets whom, how often, in which spaces. The SOC templates describe the QUALITY AND COST of social exposure — proxemic comfort, privacy regulation, territorial predictability. SC4 is the encounter generator; SOC1–SOC3 are the quality modulators. Good social design requires both: encounter opportunity (SC4) WITH proxemic comfort (SOC1), privacy regulation (SOC2), and territorial clarity (SOC3). The open-plan office failure is precisely a design that maximizes SC4 encounter while minimizing SOC1–SOC3 quality.

This establishes a design principle: **the social encounter optimum is not maximum integration, but the level of integration at which encounter benefits equal privacy costs.** For most occupancy types, this means a gradient of social exposure settings — from enclosed private spaces through semi-bounded pods to open shared commons — rather than either extreme (all private or all open).

## Testable Predictions

### Prediction 1: Proxemic PE Produces Measurable Stress at Architecturally Forced Distances

**Hypothesis**: Occupants placed at intimate-zone distance (<45cm) with strangers in architectural settings (elevator, narrow corridor, crowded waiting room) will show elevated EDA, increased cortisol, and reduced positive affect compared to the same occupants at personal-zone distance (45–120cm) in architecturally matched settings, controlling for non-social environmental variables.

**Critical experiment**: Use a controlled laboratory setting with adjustable partition distances. Pairs of strangers perform a neutral task at varying distances (30cm, 60cm, 120cm, 250cm) in otherwise identical spatial conditions. Measure EDA, salivary cortisol, task performance, and subjective comfort. Predict: U-shaped function with maximum comfort at personal-social zone boundary (~120cm) and elevated stress at both intimate-zone forced proximity and excessive public-zone distance.

**Templates tested**: SOC1.

### Prediction 2: Privacy Mechanism Availability Mediates Density → Satisfaction

**Hypothesis**: In open-plan offices matched for density, occupants with access to more privacy mechanisms (acoustic pods, visual screens, quiet rooms, territorial markers) will report higher satisfaction, lower perceived crowding, and lower cortisol than occupants with fewer mechanisms, even at identical physical densities.

**Critical experiment**: Partner with an organization undergoing office redesign. Create two matched floors: same density, same job types, but Floor A has 5 privacy mechanisms (phone booths, high partitions, assigned desks, quiet room, curtains) and Floor B has 1 (assigned desks only). Measure satisfaction (survey), perceived crowding, cortisol (salivary samples at 10am and 3pm), and task performance over 8 weeks. Predict: Floor A shows lower perceived crowding, lower cortisol, and higher satisfaction despite identical density.

**Templates tested**: SOC2.

### Prediction 3: Territorial Hierarchy Clarity Predicts Social Behavior in Shared Spaces

**Hypothesis**: Residential buildings with clear four-zone territorial hierarchies (private → semi-private → semi-public → public, with architectural markers at each transition) will show higher rates of commons use, more neighbor interaction, and higher reported sense of community than buildings with collapsed hierarchies (private → public with no intermediate zones), controlling for building size and demographics.

**Critical experiment**: Select 10 residential buildings in the same city, matched for unit count and demographics. Rate each building's territorial hierarchy clarity (number of zones, clarity of boundary markers, quality of transitions). Survey residents for commons use frequency, neighbor recognition rate, sense of community, and perceived safety. Predict: territorial clarity positively correlates with all four outcomes, with semi-public zone quality as the strongest predictor.

**Templates tested**: SOC3.

### Prediction 4: Back-Stage Access Reduces Social Fatigue in Workplace Settings

**Hypothesis**: Workers with access to back-stage recovery spaces (private phone booths, enclosed break rooms, outdoor terraces) will show lower end-of-day social fatigue, higher sustained social engagement, and lower afternoon cortisol than workers without back-stage access, in otherwise comparable workplace settings.

**Critical experiment**: Within a single large office, compare workers on floors with and without dedicated back-stage spaces (all other conditions matched). Measure social fatigue (experience sampling 3x/day), social engagement quality (observer-rated interaction), cortisol trajectory (morning, midday, afternoon), and absenteeism. Predict: back-stage access produces lower social fatigue trajectory and maintained social engagement quality through the afternoon.

**Templates tested**: SOC2, SOC3.

## Panel SOC-II Prospectus: Priorities for a Follow-Up Social Panel

### Priority 1: Cultural Calibration of Proxemic and Territorial Parameters

SOC1 and SOC3 both note that proxemic distances and territorial norms are culturally variable. A focused cross-cultural study comparing proxemic zones, territorial boundary markers, and privacy regulation strategies across at least three cultural contexts (e.g., Northern European, East Asian, Latin American) would provide the cultural calibration parameters that the current templates lack. This could leverage existing cross-cultural housing studies (Altman & Chemers, 1980) supplemented by proxemic measurement in real architectural settings.

### Priority 2: The Privacy-Encounter Trade-Off Curve

SOC-I establishes that good social design balances encounter (SC4) with privacy (SOC2). But the SHAPE of the trade-off curve is unknown. Is it linear (every increment of encounter costs an equal increment of privacy)? Concave (diminishing encounter returns at high exposure)? Is there a breakpoint beyond which privacy costs dominate? An empirical study varying the ratio of shared-to-private space across multiple office environments, measuring both encounter frequency and privacy satisfaction, would map this curve.

### Priority 3: Digital-Physical Privacy Interaction

SOC2's five privacy mechanisms are all physical. But modern workplaces add digital privacy dimensions: screen visibility, Slack status monitoring, calendar transparency, video-call backgrounds. How do digital and physical privacy mechanisms interact? Does digital surveillance exacerbate the social PE from physical openness, or does it operate through an independent channel? This is methodologically new territory but increasingly important as hybrid work becomes standard.

### Priority 4: Social Configuration in Healthcare Settings

Hospitals and care facilities present extreme cases of social configuration challenges: patients who need privacy for recovery but monitoring for safety, families who need proximity to patients but not to each other, staff who need communication efficiency but also concentrated work spaces. A healthcare-specific application of SOC1–SOC3 would need domain-specific calibration (e.g., what territorial gradient serves a 30-bed ward? what proxemic configuration optimizes the nurse-patient relationship?).

### Priority 5: Dunbar Layer Validation in Architectural Settings

SOC3 proposes that optimal social design matches architectural scale to Dunbar's social network layers. This specific prediction — that a pod of 5 supports intimate-team interaction better than a floor of 50, which supports broader-team interaction better than a building of 500 — needs direct testing. An intervention study that restructures a workplace from open-floor to Dunbar-scaled pods, measuring social satisfaction, collaboration quality, and social network structure before and after, would provide the empirical test.

## Scope Exclusions

The following topics were identified during the panel as relevant to social configuration but belonging in other panels:

**1. Urban-scale social configuration** — how neighborhood design, street layout, public space distribution, and mixed-use zoning affect community social life. These operate at the urban-morphology scale and overlap with the Urban-Scale panel flagged by SC-I. SOC-I addresses the building scale.

**2. Institutional power dynamics** — how architecture embodies and reinforces power relationships (the raised bench, the corner office, the panopticon prison). This is sociologically important but involves knowledge-meaning mechanisms (cultural interpretation of architectural symbols) more than the spatial-perceptual mechanisms that the PE framework handles. A future Meaning & Symbol panel could address this.

**3. Virtual and hybrid social space** — how remote work, video calls, virtual reality, and mixed-presence meetings create new social prediction environments. This is rapidly evolving and lacks the established scientific literature that panel-quality treatment requires. Flagged for future attention.

**4. Crowd dynamics and emergency evacuation** — how large-scale crowd behavior (panic, herding, stampede) interacts with architectural design. This is primarily a safety-engineering domain with distinct mechanisms from the social-cognitive PE framework.

**5. Social aspects of aging and child development** — how age-related changes in social cognition (reduced social network, increased dependence, developing social skills) interact with architectural social design. Belongs in the Aging & Architecture and Child Development panels flagged in the transfer context.
