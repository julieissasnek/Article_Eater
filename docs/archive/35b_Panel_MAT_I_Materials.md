## Panel Charge

This panel addresses two closely related gaps identified in Document 33 (Dual-Index Cross-Reference Layer): **A1 Materials & Surfaces** (coverage ★★ Partial) and **A7 Haptic & Thermal Properties** (coverage ★ Minimal). These domains are combined into a single panel because material properties are the primary source of haptic-thermal experience — you cannot discuss how architecture feels to the touch or to the body without discussing what it is made of.

The gap analysis found that visual and acoustic properties of materials are reasonably well-mapped to existing templates (T1 fractal fluency for wood grain, AX1/AX2 for acoustic absorption) but that haptic, thermal, and olfactory pathways are identified without being formalized. No dedicated template exists for somatosensory prediction error, thermal comfort as prediction, the affective touch pathway in architecture, or material-identity integration across modalities. Meanwhile, the key architectural question — *why does wood reduce stress?* (Doc 33, A1 open questions) — cannot be answered by the current template set because the answer is almost certainly multi-modal convergence, and the modalities beyond vision and acoustics lack mechanistic templates.

Additionally, Panel L-I (Light & Luminance, Doc 34) flagged the **light-material interaction** — how illumination reveals or transforms material appearance — as work that belongs in this panel. L1 (Luminance Contrast PE) and L4 (CCT as Ecological Signal) operate on whatever the visual system receives, and what it receives depends jointly on the light and the material. This panel must formalize that joint dependency.

The panel is charged with producing 4–6 mechanistic templates covering: (1) somatosensory prediction and haptic PE, (2) thermal comfort as allostatic prediction, (3) the C-tactile affective touch pathway in architecture, (4) multi-modal material-identity integration, (5) material-cultural conditioning and learned associations, and (6) the light-material interaction as a joint determinant of visual PE. These templates must connect to the existing infrastructure — particularly T1/T2 (visual complexity), T8 (affordance), T12 (interoceptive affect), T29 (allostatic regulation), AX5 (cross-modal congruence), L1 (luminance PE), and L4 (color rendering).

## Panel Composition

### Somatosensory Neuroscience and Affective Touch

**1. Francis McGlone** (Liverpool John Moores University, School of Natural Sciences and Psychology)
Discovered the role of C-tactile (CT) afferents in affective touch — a distinct class of unmyelinated nerve fibers that respond optimally to slow, gentle stroking at skin temperature and project to insular cortex (not somatosensory cortex), producing pleasure and social bonding signals. This pathway is the touch equivalent of the ipRGC non-visual light pathway: a parallel sensory channel with its own receptors, its own central targets, and its own functional role, separate from discriminative touch.
*Role*: Establishes the neuroscience of affective touch as distinct from discriminative touch. Provides the C-tactile pathway that architecture engages through material surface properties — texture, temperature, compliance.

**2. Håkan Olausson** (Linköping University, Center for Social and Affective Neuroscience)
Collaborator with McGlone on C-tactile afferent physiology. Conducted the landmark studies on patient G.L. — a woman lacking large-diameter myelinated afferents (no discriminative touch) who nonetheless reports pleasant sensations from slow stroking, demonstrating that the affective touch system functions independently. His fMRI work shows CT-optimal touch activates posterior insular cortex, not S1.
*Role*: Provides the clinical and neuroimaging evidence that affective and discriminative touch are genuinely independent systems. Strengthens the case that architectural surfaces engage TWO touch pathways, not one.

**3. India Morrison** (Linköping University, Department of Biomedical and Clinical Sciences)
Developed the "social touch hypothesis" — that CT afferents evolved for social bonding and that their activation by non-social stimuli (material surfaces, clothing, water) is a co-opted response. Her work on the velocity-tuning curve of CT afferents (optimal response at 1–10 cm/s, skin temperature ± 2°C) provides the psychophysical parameters that material surfaces must match to engage affective touch.
*Role*: Specifies the stimulus parameters for CT activation. Provides the bridge from neuroscience to material specification — what surface properties (texture grain, thermal conductivity, compliance) fall within the CT-optimal range?

### Thermal Comfort and Adaptive Physiology

**4. Richard de Dear** (University of Sydney, Indoor Environmental Quality Laboratory)
Developer of the adaptive thermal comfort model (ASHRAE Standard 55 revision), which overturned Fanger's steady-state PMV/PPD model by demonstrating that occupants of naturally ventilated buildings accept and prefer a wider range of temperatures than predicted by heat-balance physics — because they adapt behaviorally, physiologically, and psychologically. This adaptation is, in PE terms, a shifting prediction: the brain's thermal prediction tracks recent thermal experience, and deviations from THAT prediction (not from an absolute setpoint) determine comfort.
*Role*: Provides the predictive framework for thermal comfort. His adaptive model maps directly onto PE: thermal prediction = f(recent thermal history); thermal PE = deviation from adaptive expectation; comfort = low thermal PE. Demonstrates that buildings with occupant-accessible thermal variation (operable windows, ceiling fans, radiant panels) produce higher satisfaction than buildings that suppress all thermal variation.

**5. Lisa Heschong** (Heschong Mahone Group; author, *Thermal Delight in Architecture*)
The foundational text connecting thermal experience to architectural pleasure. Heschong's argument — that thermal variability is a source of delight, not a problem to be eliminated — anticipates the PE framework by decades. Her examples (the warmth of a fireplace, the cool of a stone floor in summer, the breeze through an open window) describe architectural thermal experiences that produce positive affect precisely because they offer moderate thermal PE against an otherwise stable background.
*Role*: Provides the phenomenological and design-practice perspective on thermal experience. Bridges between thermal engineering and architectural quality. Returns from Panel L-I, where her work on daylight convergence informed L3.

**6. Wouter van Marken Lichtenbelt** (Maastricht University, Department of Nutrition and Movement Sciences)
Research on mild cold exposure and metabolic health — demonstrating that thermal variation (as opposed to thermal constancy in the "thermoneutral zone") activates brown adipose tissue, improves insulin sensitivity, and reduces cardiovascular risk. His work challenges the building-engineering assumption that thermal constancy is optimal and provides physiological evidence that the body benefits from moderate thermal challenge.
*Role*: Provides the metabolic and physiological evidence that thermal PE is not just perceptually tolerable but physiologically beneficial. Connects the adaptive comfort model (de Dear) to health outcomes beyond subjective satisfaction.

### Cross-Modal Perception and Material Cognition

**7. Charles Spence** (University of Oxford, Crossmodal Research Laboratory)
The world's leading researcher on multisensory perception — how the brain combines information from vision, touch, hearing, smell, and taste into unified percepts. His work on material perception specifically — how visual texture predicts haptic experience, how sound affects food texture perception, how color biases temperature judgment — provides the cross-modal integration framework that is central to how materials are experienced in architecture. Over 1,000 publications, ~50,000 total citations.
*Role*: Provides the multisensory integration science. Establishes that material perception is ALWAYS cross-modal — you never perceive a material through one sense alone — and that the brain's predictions about material properties flow between modalities. Architecture manipulates these cross-modal predictions whenever it pairs a material with a particular light, sound, or spatial context.

**8. Roberta Klatzky** (Carnegie Mellon University, Department of Psychology)
Research on haptic perception, object recognition by touch, and the cognitive representation of material properties. Her work on "haptic glance" — the rapid extraction of material properties (roughness, hardness, temperature) from brief touch — parallels the visual "gist" literature and the rapid implicit evaluation mechanism (T9). She has also contributed to understanding how visual and haptic information combine in material judgment.
*Role*: Provides the perceptual psychology of touch. Specifies what information the haptic system extracts from materials, how quickly, and how this integrates with visual information. Bridges between surface physics (roughness, compliance, thermal conductivity) and perceptual representation.

**9. Massimo Zampini** (University of Trento, Center for Mind/Brain Sciences)
Known for the "parchment-skin illusion" and auditory-tactile interaction studies (with Spence). Demonstrated that the sound a material makes when touched affects perceived texture — rubbing hands together sounds different through modified audio and FEELS different. This auditory-haptic interaction is directly relevant to architectural materials: walking on a material, touching a surface, closing a door, all produce sounds that modify the haptic experience.
*Role*: Provides the auditory-haptic cross-modal evidence specific to material interaction. Extends AX5 (cross-modal congruence) into the touch-sound dimension that architecture exploits constantly — footsteps on stone vs. carpet, door handles in metal vs. wood, surfaces that creak, echo, or absorb.

### Material Phenomenology and Design Practice

**10. Juhani Pallasmaa** (Helsinki, architect and theorist; author, *The Eyes of the Skin*)
The most influential architectural theorist of haptic experience. His critique of "ocularcentrism" — the dominance of vision in architectural theory and practice — argues that architecture's deepest effects operate through the body's non-visual senses: touch, proprioception, thermal sensation, even muscular tension. The "haptic image" — the body's felt sense of a material or space, as opposed to its visual appearance — is central to his theory.
*Role*: Provides the phenomenological framework and the design-theoretic argument for why material-haptic experience matters architecturally. His taxonomies of material experience (warmth, weight, age, texture, resonance) serve the same role that Plummer's light types served in Panel L-I — the experiential catalog that the mechanistic templates must explain.

**11. Peter Zumthor** (Haldenstein, architect; Pritzker Prize 2009)
Architect whose work is most explicitly concerned with material atmosphere — the Therme Vals (stone, water, steam, light), the Bruder Klaus Field Chapel (charred timber interior, concrete exterior), the Kolumba Museum (layered brick filtering light). His theoretical writing (*Thinking Architecture*, *Atmospheres*) describes the design process of selecting and composing materials for their sensory and emotional effect. His concept of "atmosphere" — the overall emotional quality of a space, produced by the convergence of material, light, temperature, sound, and spatial proportion — is the architectural practitioner's intuitive version of multi-modal PE integration.
*Role*: Provides the practitioner's account of how material selection is used to produce specific emotional atmospheres. His Therme Vals is a canonical case study: the same stone (Vals quartzite) is experienced as cool/hard/echoing in the changing rooms, warm/smooth/enveloping in the thermal pools, rough/glittering/dramatic in the fire bath — the material is constant but the experience changes because light, temperature, and acoustic context change. This is a natural experiment in cross-modal material perception.

**12. Mark Miodownik** (University College London, Institute of Making; author, *Stuff Matters*)
Materials scientist and public communicator whose work bridges the physics and chemistry of materials with human sensory experience. His research on "sensoaesthetics" — the systematic study of how material properties map to sensory and emotional responses — provides the physical-science side of the material-perception bridge. What measurable properties of a surface (Ra roughness, thermal effusivity, spectral reflectance) predict what the hand and eye will report?
*Role*: Provides the materials-science anchor. Translates between physical parameters (thermal effusivity in W·s^½·m⁻²·K⁻¹, surface roughness in μm Ra, spectral reflectance curves) and the sensory variables that the neural mechanisms operate on. Without this translation, the templates cannot connect to measurable architectural specifications.

### Biophilic Design and Ecological Psychology

**13. Stephen Kellert** [posthumous contribution via published work] (Yale University, School of Forestry and Environmental Studies; 1943–2016)
Author of *Biophilic Design* and *Birthright*, the foundational texts connecting biophilia theory (E.O. Wilson's hypothesis that humans have an innate tendency to seek connections with nature) to architectural practice. His framework identifies natural materials as a primary biophilic design element, with specific claims about wood, stone, water, and earth as stress-reducing, attention-restoring, and emotionally positive.
*Role*: Provides the biophilic design framework that many architectural studies cite. Panel T2-C already reduced Biophilia to Tier 1 mechanisms; this panel must evaluate whether "natural materials" effects reduce to multi-modal PE convergence (as the convergence hypothesis predicts) or whether biophilic response to materials involves an additional ecological mechanism (evolved preference for natural material signatures).

**14. Yoshifumi Miyazaki** (Chiba University, Center for Environment, Health, and Field Sciences)
Leading researcher on the physiological effects of natural environments and natural materials. His "shinrin-yoku" (forest bathing) studies and wood-interior studies measure cortisol, blood pressure, heart rate variability, and natural killer cell activity in response to natural materials and environments. Provides the most extensive physiological data on wood-stress reduction and the role of phytoncides (volatile organic compounds from wood).
*Role*: Provides the physiological evidence for material-wellbeing effects, particularly wood. His cortisol and HRV data are the empirical anchors for whatever mechanistic templates this panel produces about wood-stress reduction. Also provides the olfactory pathway data — his work on phytoncides shows that volatile organic compounds from wood (particularly hinoki cypress) reduce sympathetic nervous system activity independently of visual or haptic contact.

**15. Gerd Gigerenzer** (Max Planck Institute for Human Development, Berlin)
Research on ecological rationality — how cognitive heuristics are adapted to the statistical regularities of natural environments. His "take-the-best" heuristic and "recognition heuristic" work demonstrates that fast, frugal decision-making exploits environmental cues that correlate reliably with outcomes. For materials: grain patterns, surface temperature, compliance, and resonance are ecological cues that the brain uses to make rapid, implicit judgments about safety, quality, and biological relevance.
*Role*: Provides the ecological-rationality framework for understanding WHY certain material properties are rapidly evaluated as positive or negative. Natural materials exhibit statistical regularities (1/f spectral profiles, thermal conductivity in the CT-optimal range, familiar olfactory signatures) that the brain has been calibrated to use as cues. Artificial materials that violate these regularities (uniform textures, extreme thermal conductivity, synthetic odors) challenge the brain's ecological heuristics.

## Position Statements

### SOMATOSENSORY NEUROSCIENCE: TWO TOUCH SYSTEMS

**Position Statement 1: Francis McGlone — The Dual-Touch Architecture**

Architecture touches you, and you touch architecture. Every surface your body contacts — the floor under your feet, the handrail under your palm, the chair under your weight, the wall your shoulder brushes — activates the somatosensory system. But what most architects and building scientists do not know is that there are TWO somatosensory systems, processing different information, through different neural channels, to different cortical targets, for different functional purposes.

The first is the **discriminative touch system** — large-diameter, myelinated Aβ afferents projecting through the dorsal column to the ventroposterior thalamus and primary somatosensory cortex (S1). This system provides precise spatial and temporal information about surface properties: texture, shape, edge, vibration, pressure. It tells you WHAT the surface is and WHERE you are touching it. It is fast (conduction velocity ~35–75 m/s), precise, and topographically mapped.

The second is the **affective touch system** — small-diameter, unmyelinated C-tactile (CT) afferents projecting through lamina I of the dorsal horn to the posterior insular cortex, bypassing S1 entirely (McGlone, Wessberg, & Olausson, 2014, ~500 citations; Olausson et al., 2002, ~800 citations). CT afferents respond optimally to slow, gentle stroking (1–10 cm/s) at skin temperature (approximately 32°C), and their activation produces a diffuse, pleasant sensation that we experience as comfort, warmth, or coziness. They do NOT provide precise spatial or temporal information. They provide an affective evaluation: this contact is GOOD or BAD for the organism.

The architectural relevance is direct: **material surfaces differ systematically in their ability to engage CT afferents.** A smooth, warm wooden handrail at skin temperature, encountered by a hand sliding along it at walking pace (~3–5 cm/s), falls squarely within the CT-optimal stimulus range: the right velocity, the right temperature, the right texture grain. The affective touch system responds with a comfort signal. A cold metal handrail at 18°C, with a uniform polished surface, is outside the CT-optimal range on both temperature and texture dimensions — the discriminative system registers "smooth, cold, metal" but the affective system is silent. A rough concrete handrail at ambient temperature activates the discriminative system strongly (high spatial-frequency texture) but may exceed the CT-optimal texture range, producing a neutral or mildly aversive affective signal.

In PE terms: the discriminative touch system generates predictions about surface properties based on visual appearance (AX5 cross-modal prediction) and tests them on contact — this is somatosensory PE. The affective touch system evaluates whether the contact is within the biologically optimal range for pleasant touch and generates an insular affective signal accordingly. These are parallel evaluations of the same physical contact, producing different information (spatial vs. affective), at different speeds (fast vs. slow), in different cortical targets (S1 vs. posterior insula). Architecture that engages BOTH systems — materials that are both discriminatively interesting and affectively pleasant — produces a richer, more positively-valenced haptic experience than materials that engage only one.

**Position Statement 2: Håkan Olausson — Clinical Evidence for Touch System Independence**

The case of patient G.L. provides the strongest evidence that the affective touch system functions independently of the discriminative system. G.L. has a rare hereditary sensory neuropathy that destroyed her large-diameter myelinated afferents — she has no discriminative touch, no proprioception, no vibration sense below the neck. Yet when her forearm is stroked slowly (CT-optimal velocity), she reports a faint, pleasant sensation "like a tickle" and her fMRI shows activation of posterior insular cortex — the CT cortical target — with NO activation of S1 (Olausson et al., 2002, ~800 citations; Olausson, Wessberg, Morrison, McGlone, & Vallbo, 2010, ~400 citations).

Conversely, patients with small-fiber neuropathy (loss of C-fibers, preserved Aβ fibers) retain discriminative touch — they can identify textures, shapes, and edges — but report reduced pleasure from gentle touch and show diminished insular activation. They perceive surfaces accurately but do not FEEL them as comfortable or uncomfortable.

The architectural implication: a building that is designed purely for visual and discriminative-tactile experience — precise edges, sharp geometries, smooth or abrasive surfaces — may be perceptually rich but affectively neutral. A building designed for affective-tactile experience — warm, textured, CT-optimal surfaces in areas of body contact — produces positive interoceptive affect even when the discriminative information is modest. The best buildings do both.

**Position Statement 3: India Morrison — Velocity-Tuning and the Physics of Pleasant Touch**

The CT afferent velocity-tuning curve is remarkably specific: firing rate peaks at 1–10 cm/s, drops sharply outside this range, and correlates directly with perceived pleasantness (Löken, Wessberg, Morrison, McGlone, & Olausson, 2009, ~800 citations; Morrison, Löken, & Olausson, 2010, ~300 citations). This means that the pleasantness of touching a surface depends not only on what the surface IS but on how FAST you move across it.

In architecture, the relevant velocities are determined by the occupant's behavior: walking (foot-floor contact at ~1–2 m/s — too fast for CT-optimal range, but material compliance and thermal properties still matter), hand sliding on a railing (~3–10 cm/s — within CT-optimal range), hand resting on a countertop (~0 cm/s — temperature matters but velocity is zero, so CT activation is minimal; however, CT afferents also respond to gentle pressure changes during weight shifting), and whole-body contact with seating surfaces (near-zero velocity but large contact area, with thermal conductivity dominating the affective signal).

The CT system also has a temperature preference: skin temperature ± 2°C. This means that material thermal conductivity determines whether a surface "feels" within the CT-optimal range. Wood has low thermal effusivity (~300–500 W·s^½·m⁻²·K⁻¹) — it does not rapidly conduct heat away from the skin, so the contact temperature stays close to skin temperature. Metal has high thermal effusivity (~8,000–15,000 W·s^½·m⁻²·K⁻¹) — it rapidly conducts heat away, creating a large temperature difference at the skin-surface interface. Stone falls in between (~1,500–2,500). The CT system "prefers" low-effusivity materials because they maintain skin-temperature contact — which is why wood feels "warm" and metal feels "cold" even at the same ambient temperature. This is not just a thermal judgment; it is an affective one, mediated by a specific class of nerve fiber.

### THERMAL COMFORT AS PREDICTION

**Position Statement 4: Richard de Dear — The Adaptive Model Is a Prediction Error Model**

For fifty years, thermal comfort engineering was dominated by Fanger's Predicted Mean Vote (PMV) model — a heat-balance equation that predicts comfort from air temperature, radiant temperature, humidity, air speed, clothing insulation, and metabolic rate (Fanger, 1970, ~8,000 citations). The PMV model treats the human body as a thermodynamic system and comfort as the absence of thermal load. It predicts a single optimal temperature (around 22–24°C for typical office clothing and activity) and treats deviations as discomfort.

The adaptive model emerged from a massive empirical challenge to PMV: in naturally ventilated buildings, occupants accept and prefer a MUCH wider temperature range than PMV predicts (de Dear & Brager, 1998, ~3,000 citations; de Dear & Brager, 2002, ~2,000 citations). In a mechanically air-conditioned office, occupants prefer 22–24°C as PMV predicts. In a naturally ventilated office in the same climate, occupants are comfortable at 18–30°C — a range three times wider. The adaptive model explains this through three mechanisms: behavioral adaptation (adjusting clothing, opening windows), physiological acclimatization (seasonal shifts in vasomotor and sudomotor thresholds), and psychological expectation (occupants expect and accept more variability in naturally ventilated buildings).

The psychological expectation component IS a prediction error model. The brain forms a thermal prediction based on recent experience: if the last several days have been warm, the thermal prediction shifts upward, and today's 28°C is experienced as expected rather than uncomfortable. In a mechanically conditioned building, the prediction is precise (always 22°C) and deviation of even 2°C is noticed and evaluated negatively. In a naturally ventilated building, the prediction has wider variance (could be 20–28°C) and the same 2°C deviation produces less PE. Comfort is not a temperature; it is a prediction error magnitude.

The architectural consequence is profound: **buildings that suppress all thermal variation narrow the thermal prediction and make occupants LESS tolerant of any variation.** A hermetically sealed office at constant 22°C trains the brain to predict 22°C with high precision. When the air conditioning fails and the temperature reaches 25°C, the PE is large relative to the narrow prediction, and discomfort is acute. A naturally ventilated building that exposes occupants to moderate daily variation (20–26°C) trains the brain to predict a wider range, and the same 25°C produces little PE. The irony is that the building designed to maximize comfort (constant temperature) actually reduces thermal resilience, while the building that "allows" thermal variation builds adaptive tolerance.

**Position Statement 5: Lisa Heschong — Thermal Delight Is Moderate Thermal PE**

*Thermal Delight in Architecture* (Heschong, 1979, ~500 citations) was written decades before the predictive processing framework, but its argument is precisely a PE argument: the most memorable and pleasurable thermal experiences in architecture involve moderate departures from thermal neutrality, not the absence of all thermal sensation.

The warmth of a fireplace in a cool room. The cool of a stone floor on bare feet in summer. The breeze through an open window against still, warm air. The radiant heat of a sunlight patch on a cold morning. Each of these is a thermal PE event — a localized deviation from the prevailing thermal prediction — and each produces pleasure. The mechanism is the thermal equivalent of the luminance Goldilocks zone (L1): too little thermal variation (constant HVAC) → no thermal PE → sensory blandness, no thermal delight; moderate thermal variation (radiant asymmetry, drafts, material temperature differences) → moderate thermal PE → delight, engagement, awareness; excessive thermal variation (freezing drafts, overheating) → high thermal PE → discomfort, distress.

The architectural tradition understood this intuitively. Japanese architecture uses the contrast between a heated kotatsu table and the cool surrounding room. Finnish saunas alternate extreme heat with cold water plunges. Islamic architecture uses thermally massive stone to create cool interiors against hot exteriors. These are all designed thermal PE experiences — they produce pleasure precisely because they deviate from the predicted thermal state.

The modern building-engineering tradition has largely eliminated thermal PE in pursuit of thermal "comfort" — which it defines as the absence of thermal awareness. De Dear's adaptive model demonstrates that this definition is physiologically narrow; my work demonstrates that it is experientially impoverished. A truly comfortable building provides thermal delight, which requires thermal PE, which requires thermal variation.

**Position Statement 6: Wouter van Marken Lichtenbelt — Thermal Variation as Metabolic Medicine**

The case for thermal variation extends beyond comfort and delight to metabolic health. Modern indoor environments typically maintain the thermoneutral zone (TNZ, approximately 22–25°C for lightly clothed individuals), in which the body expends no energy on thermoregulation. This is precisely the range Fanger's model targets. But sustained TNZ exposure comes at a physiological cost.

Mild cold exposure (16–19°C for 2–6 hours/day) activates brown adipose tissue (BAT), increases energy expenditure by 5–30%, improves insulin sensitivity, reduces fasting glucose, and over weeks of repeated exposure increases BAT volume and activity (van Marken Lichtenbelt et al., 2009, ~2,000 citations; Hanssen et al., 2015, ~500 citations). These effects are clinically significant — sustained mild cold exposure produces metabolic changes comparable to moderate exercise. Conversely, chronic TNZ exposure is associated with the same metabolic profile as physical inactivity: reduced BAT activity, decreased metabolic flexibility, and increased susceptibility to obesity and type 2 diabetes.

The architectural implication: buildings that maintain constant thermoneutrality are not physiologically optimal — they are metabolically sedative. Buildings that provide structured thermal variation (cooler mornings, warmer afternoons; cooler peripheral zones, warmer core zones; outdoor-connected transition spaces) offer metabolic benefits that constant-temperature buildings cannot. This aligns with de Dear's adaptive model (wider thermal acceptance in naturally ventilated buildings) and Heschong's phenomenology (thermal variation as delight), but adds a health dimension: the "comfortable" constant-temperature building may be contributing to metabolic disease through thermal deprivation.

For the PE framework: the body's thermoregulatory system, like the circadian system (L2), generates predictions and benefits from moderate prediction error. A thermal prediction that is NEVER challenged (constant TNZ) atrophies, just as a circadian prediction that is never reinforced by bright morning light (L2) drifts. The allostatic regulation template (T29) already captures chronic stress from thermal overload; van Marken Lichtenbelt's work adds the complementary finding that chronic thermal UNDER-load (no challenge, no variation) is also allostatic — the system is kept in a state that prevents adaptive recalibration.

### CROSS-MODAL PERCEPTION AND MATERIAL COGNITION

**Position Statement 7: Charles Spence — Materials Are Never Perceived Through One Sense**

The single most important fact about material perception is that it is ALWAYS multisensory (Spence, 2020, ~100 citations; Spence & Gallace, 2011, ~200 citations). When you encounter a wooden table, you see its grain, color, and reflectance; you feel its temperature, texture, and compliance; you hear the sound it makes when you set a glass on it; if it is cedar or sandalwood, you smell it. The brain does not process these as five independent streams. It binds them into a unified material percept through multisensory integration, with predictions flowing between modalities: vision predicts what touch will feel like, sound predicts what the material's density and rigidity will be, smell predicts biological origin.

The architectural consequence is that material experience depends not just on the material's properties but on the CONTEXT in which it is encountered — because context modulates cross-modal predictions. The same wooden surface under warm incandescent light looks and feels warmer than under cool fluorescent light, because the visual warmth (L4 CCT-ecological signal) primes a thermal prediction that biases the haptic experience. This is not an illusion; it is how the brain works. Visual-thermal cross-modal modulation is well-documented (Ho et al., 2014, ~150 citations). The lighting designer and the material specifier are not making independent decisions — they are jointly determining a single multisensory material experience.

For the template framework, this means that evaluating a finding about material effects requires checking WHICH modalities were controlled. A study claiming that "wood reduces stress compared to plastic" is confounded if the wood and plastic differ in visual grain (T1), thermal effusivity (CT pathway), acoustic absorption (AX1), and volatile organic compounds (olfactory pathway) simultaneously — which they invariably do. The existing cross-modal congruence template (AX5) captures the principle, but the panel needs a dedicated material-integration template that specifies how multi-modal material signals combine and how the combination produces its characteristic affective response.

**Position Statement 8: Roberta Klatzky — Haptic Glance and the Speed of Material Judgment**

The hand extracts material properties with remarkable speed and efficiency. A brief exploratory touch — what we call the "haptic glance" — lasting as little as 200ms provides enough information to categorize a material's roughness, hardness, and temperature (Klatzky & Lederman, 2010, ~400 citations; Lederman & Klatzky, 2009, ~500 citations). This parallels the 150ms visual "gist" that T9 (rapid implicit evaluation) describes for visual scenes. The somatosensory system, like the visual system, performs ultra-rapid material categorization.

The exploratory procedures for extracting material properties are stereotyped: lateral motion for texture, pressure for hardness, static contact for temperature, enclosure for shape, unsupported holding for weight (Lederman & Klatzky, 1987, ~3,000 citations). Each procedure preferentially activates different mechanoreceptor populations and extracts different material dimensions. Architecture constrains which procedures are available: floors allow only foot-contact (pressure, temperature); handrails allow lateral motion (texture, temperature); walls may be touched or not depending on social norms. The material properties that are architecturally salient — the ones that affect experience — depend on which body parts make contact and which exploratory procedures are possible.

When haptic information CONFIRMS the visual prediction (the stone looks cold and feels cold, the wood looks rough and feels rough), processing is fluent and rapid — this is the cross-modal congruence that AX5 describes. When haptic information VIOLATES the visual prediction (a "stone" countertop that feels warm because it is heated, a "wood" surface that feels plasticky because it is laminate), the result is somatosensory PE — the haptic glance returns information that contradicts the visual expectation. The magnitude of this PE depends on how strongly vision predicted the haptic properties, which in turn depends on how stereotypically the visual appearance is associated with the material category.

**Position Statement 9: Massimo Zampini — The Sound of Materials**

People underestimate how much of their material experience comes through their ears. The "parchment-skin illusion" demonstrated that modifying the high-frequency audio feedback from rubbing hands together changes the perceived roughness of the skin (Jousmäki & Hari, 1998, ~600 citations; Zampini & Spence, 2004, ~400 citations). Similarly, the sound of footsteps on a material — the bright click on marble, the muffled thud on carpet, the creak on old wood — provides information about material density, rigidity, and compliance that the somatosensory system uses to calibrate its own predictions.

In architecture, the auditory dimension of material experience is pervasive but rarely acknowledged in design specifications. Walking on stone produces a reverberation-rich, high-frequency impact sound that is processed by the auditory system as "hard, dense, massive" — confirming the visual and haptic predictions for stone and contributing to the cross-modal percept of material solidity. Walking on wood produces a mellower, lower-frequency sound with more resonance — "organic, warm, somewhat compliant." Walking on carpet produces a heavily damped, low-frequency sound — "soft, absorptive, intimate." These auditory signatures are part of the material percept, and they contribute to the spatial-acoustic character that the aural architecture templates (AX1, AX2, AX4) describe.

The cross-modal integration happens automatically. The brain does not separately evaluate "what this floor looks like," "what it feels like underfoot," and "what it sounds like when I walk on it." It forms a single, integrated material percept — "marble" or "oak" or "carpet" — that spans all three modalities. And when one modality is incongruent — a surface that looks like marble but sounds like plywood (because it IS a thin marble veneer on plywood substrate) — the resulting cross-modal PE produces the distinctive unease of material inauthenticity. The occupant may not be able to articulate what is wrong, but the multisensory prediction machinery detects the inconsistency.

### MATERIAL PHENOMENOLOGY AND DESIGN PRACTICE

**Position Statement 10: Juhani Pallasmaa — The Haptic Image and the Critique of Ocularcentrism**

Modern architecture has been dominated by the eye. Buildings are designed to be seen — in renderings, photographs, and bird's-eye plans — and the non-visual senses have been systematically marginalized (Pallasmaa, 2005, ~3,000 citations). The result is an architecture of surfaces rather than substances, of images rather than experiences. Glass, steel, and polished concrete are visually dramatic but haptically impoverished — they offer the eye a spectacle while giving the body nothing.

The "haptic image" is my term for the body's remembered and anticipated sense of a material — not how it looks but how it would feel to touch, lean against, walk on, inhabit (Pallasmaa, 2005). The haptic image of old oak is warmth, weight, faint grain under the fingertips, a slight give when pressed. The haptic image of poured concrete is coolness, mass, grit, rigidity. The haptic image of tatami is yielding warmth, the smell of dried grass, the sound of rustling. These images are not secondary to the visual; they are the body's primary relationship with the material world.

In predictive processing terms, the haptic image IS the body's material prediction — the somatosensory, thermal, olfactory, and proprioceptive expectations generated when a material is seen and before it is touched. Architecture that fulfills these predictions (honest material expression — what you see is what you will touch) produces cross-modal congruence and bodily comfort. Architecture that defeats them (fake materials, veneers, surface treatments that simulate one material with another) produces cross-modal PE and — I would argue — a subtle but pervasive erosion of bodily trust in the built environment.

This is not an argument against abstraction or invention. It is an argument that the body is a participant in architectural experience, not a spectator, and that material selection is a bodily contract with the occupant: the building promises a certain tactile, thermal, olfactory reality, and it must deliver.

**Position Statement 11: Peter Zumthor — Atmosphere as Multi-Modal Material Convergence**

When I begin a project, I do not start with form. I start with materials. I ask: what is this building made of, and how will it feel to be inside it? (Zumthor, 2006, ~1,000 citations; Zumthor, 2006b, ~800 citations). The Therme Vals begins with Vals quartzite — the local stone, dark grey-green, layered, slightly rough. Every architectural decision follows from the stone: the mass, the weight of the walls, the way the stone holds temperature (cool in the changing rooms, warm at the pool edge because of the heated water), the way it sounds (reflective, producing the echoing splash and murmur of a grotto), the way it smells when wet (mineral, ancient), the way light catches the mica flecks in the layered surfaces.

The experience I design for is not a visual experience, nor a haptic one, nor a thermal one, nor an acoustic one. It is all of these simultaneously. I call this "atmosphere" — the total sensory and emotional quality of a space, which arises from the convergence of material, light, temperature, sound, and spatial proportion (Zumthor, 2006b, ~800 citations). Atmosphere is irreducible to any single sensory channel. Remove the material and you lose the tactile and thermal dimension. Remove the light and you lose the visual and circadian dimension. Remove the sound and you lose the spatial-acoustic dimension. Atmosphere is the product of convergence.

*Panel translation to PE terms*: Zumthor's "atmosphere" is what happens when multi-modal material predictions are consistently confirmed across ALL modalities. In the Therme Vals: the stone looks massive and feels massive (visual-haptic congruence). It looks cool and feels cool, until heated by pool water (thermal prediction confirmed or interestingly violated). It looks reverberant and sounds reverberant (visual-acoustic congruence, AX2). It looks mineral and smells mineral (visual-olfactory congruence). This multi-modal prediction confirmation is the material analog of what L3 describes for daylight: not a single-channel effect but a convergent multi-channel confirmation that produces a sense of material REALNESS — of being in a genuine place made of actual substances. The opposite — a space of veneers, laminates, concealed speakers, and scented HVAC — produces the uneasy sense of material fakeness that Pallasmaa calls the "alienation of the senses."

**Position Statement 12: Mark Miodownik — The Materials Science of Sensory Experience**

The bridge between what architects call "material quality" and what neuroscientists call "sensory processing" runs through measurable physical properties. Let me specify the translation (Miodownik, 2013, ~200 citations):

**Thermal effusivity** (e = √(kρc), units W·s^½·m⁻²·K⁻¹): determines the rate of heat transfer at the skin-surface interface. This is what the hand measures when it "feels" whether a material is "warm" or "cold." Wood: 300–500. Brick: 1,000–1,500. Stone: 1,500–2,500. Steel: 12,000–15,000. The CT-optimal range (Morrison's data) corresponds to materials that maintain contact temperature within ±2°C of skin temperature — roughly, materials with e < 800. Wood is squarely in this range; metal is far outside it.

**Surface roughness** (Ra, arithmetic mean roughness in μm): determines the tactile texture signal. Polished marble: Ra < 0.5 μm. Sanded wood: Ra ~ 10–50 μm. Rough-sawn timber: Ra ~ 100–500 μm. Exposed aggregate concrete: Ra ~ 500–2,000 μm. The CT system responds best to surfaces with moderate roughness — the texture must provide enough variation to generate a neural signal at CT-optimal velocities but not so much as to be unpleasant. Empirically, pleasantness peaks for surfaces in the Ra 10–100 μm range (Skedung et al., 2011, ~300 citations), which maps to sanded to lightly textured natural materials.

**Spectral reflectance**: determines visual appearance under illumination — this is where the light-material interaction (L-I's Holthuijsen recommendation) becomes quantitative. A material's visual character depends jointly on its reflectance spectrum AND the illuminant's emission spectrum. Natural materials have broadband, slowly varying reflectance spectra. Synthetic materials and coatings can have narrow spectral features. Under natural daylight (broadband), both look similar. Under narrow-band LED illumination, the synthetic material may look markedly different — because the illuminant's spectrum interacts with the reflectance features to produce unexpected chromaticity (L4 color constancy mechanism).

**VOC emission profile**: determines olfactory experience. Fresh wood releases terpenes (α-pinene, limonene) that activate olfactory receptors and downstream limbic structures. Hinoki cypress (Chamaecyparis obtusa) releases chamaecyparis-specific monoterpenes that reduce sympathetic nervous system activity (Miyazaki's work). Fresh concrete releases calcium hydroxide dust. New carpet releases 4-phenylcyclohexene and other synthetic VOCs that many people find aversive. These are measurable, specifiable parameters that predict the olfactory component of material experience.

The practical value of this translation: when Article Eater encounters a finding about material effects, it needs to know not just "the material was wood" but WHICH physical properties were present and which were controlled. A study comparing "wood" to "laminate that looks like wood" isolates the non-visual channels (thermal, haptic, acoustic, olfactory). A study comparing "wood" to "steel" confounds all channels simultaneously. The template system should flag these methodological distinctions.

### BIOPHILIC DESIGN AND ECOLOGICAL PSYCHOLOGY

**Position Statement 13: Stephen Kellert [posthumous, from published work] — Natural Materials as Biophilic Design Elements**

The biophilic design framework identifies natural materials — wood, stone, earth, water, natural fibers — as a primary design element supporting the human-nature connection (Kellert, Heerwagen, & Mador, 2008, ~2,000 citations; Kellert & Calabrese, 2015, ~500 citations). The theoretical claim is that humans have an evolved predisposition to respond positively to natural material stimuli because these stimuli were reliably associated with survival-relevant information throughout evolutionary history: wood signaled vegetation and water (habitable landscape), stone signaled shelter and permanence, earth signaled fertility, water signaled the most critical survival resource.

The empirical evidence for preferential response to natural materials is substantial but methodologically heterogeneous. Studies consistently find that wood interiors reduce physiological stress markers (cortisol, blood pressure, sympathetic nervous activity) relative to metal or plastic interiors (Nyrud & Bringslimark, 2010, ~200 citations; Sakuragawa et al., 2005, ~150 citations; Burnard & Kutnar, 2015, ~100 citations). But as noted in Document 33's open question: these studies typically confound visual grain (1/f spectral profile), thermal properties (low effusivity), acoustic properties (moderate absorption), and olfactory properties (terpene release) simultaneously. The "biophilic" explanation — that we respond to wood BECAUSE it is natural, through an evolved mechanism — is underdetermined by data that can equally be explained by multi-modal sensory convergence operating through domain-general PE mechanisms.

*Panel note*: Panel T2-C already reduced the Biophilia construct to Tier 1 mechanisms (T1 fractal fluency for visual naturalness, T5 for prospect-refuge, T2 for complexity). The question for this panel is whether material-specific biophilic response reduces similarly to the multi-modal sensory mechanisms we are formalizing, or whether an additional evolved-preference mechanism is needed.

**Position Statement 14: Yoshifumi Miyazaki — Wood, Phytoncides, and the Autonomic Nervous System**

Our laboratory has conducted extensive studies on the physiological effects of wood in interior environments and the specific role of wood-derived volatile organic compounds (phytoncides). The findings:

Viewing wood surfaces reduces prefrontal cortex activity (assessed by near-infrared spectroscopy) and reduces sympathetic nervous system markers relative to viewing steel or white-painted surfaces (Tsunetsugu, Miyazaki, & Sato, 2007, ~300 citations). This effect occurs within 90 seconds of visual exposure and is modulated by the proportion of wood in the visual field — rooms with 45% wood-surface ratio produced optimal physiological relaxation; rooms with 90% wood produced less relaxation, suggesting an inverted-U function for wood proportion (Tsunetsugu et al., 2007). This is consistent with the Goldilocks zone: too little natural material → insufficient biophilic signal; optimal proportion → maximal relaxation; too much → the space may feel overwhelming or rustic, losing the contrast between natural and architectural that provides the PE.

The phytoncide pathway is separable from the visual pathway. Inhalation of hinoki cypress oil (the primary volatile from Japanese cypress wood) reduces salivary cortisol, reduces sympathetic nerve activity (measured by urinary adrenaline), and increases natural killer cell activity (Li et al., 2006, ~800 citations; Li et al., 2009, ~1,200 citations). These effects occur in the absence of any visual or haptic wood contact — subjects inhale the oil in a controlled room. The mechanism involves olfactory receptor activation → limbic system (amygdala, hippocampus) → hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis modulation. This is a direct olfactory-autonomic pathway that operates independently of the visual PE mechanisms.

The olfactory pathway raises an important question: is the phytoncide effect "biophilic" (evolved response to plant volatiles that signaled safe, resource-rich environments) or is it pharmacological (specific molecular interactions between terpenes and neural receptors, like any drug)? The answer matters because a pharmacological effect would not require evolutionary history — any molecule that binds the right receptors would produce the effect regardless of its biological origin. A biophilic effect would be specific to naturally-occurring volatile profiles.

**Position Statement 15: Gerd Gigerenzer — Ecological Cues and Material Heuristics**

The brain does not analyze material properties from first principles. It uses heuristics — fast, frugal rules that exploit the statistical regularities of natural environments (Gigerenzer, Todd, & the ABC Research Group, 1999, ~5,000 citations). For material assessment, the relevant heuristics include:

The **recognition heuristic**: a material pattern that is recognized (familiar wood grain, known stone texture) is judged more positively than one that is not. Familiar materials have been safe in the past; unfamiliar materials are uncertain. This produces a conservatism bias in material preference — occupants tend to prefer materials they have encountered before, or that resemble materials they have encountered.

The **fluency heuristic**: materials whose sensory properties are processed fluently — without effort, without prediction error — are judged more positively (Reber, Schwarz, & Winkielman, 2004, ~3,000 citations). Natural materials tend to have 1/f spectral statistics in their visual texture (T1), moderate thermal effusivity (CT-optimal range), and familiar olfactory signatures — all of which produce fluent processing. Synthetic materials that depart from these statistics (uniform textures, extreme thermal conductivity, novel chemical odors) require effortful processing and are judged less positively.

The **take-the-best heuristic**: when evaluating a material rapidly (the "haptic glance" that Klatzky describes), the brain does not weigh all sensory dimensions equally. It takes the single most diagnostic cue and bases its judgment on that. For material safety assessment, temperature may be the most diagnostic cue (extreme cold or heat signals danger). For material quality assessment, visual grain may be most diagnostic (regular, complex grain signals natural origin). The hierarchy of diagnostic cues differs by context, but the principle is the same: the brain uses the fastest, most reliable cue rather than performing an exhaustive multi-dimensional analysis.

For the template framework: the ecological-rationality perspective suggests that material preferences are not arbitrary or purely cultural — they reflect the statistical regularities that natural materials have exhibited throughout evolutionary history. Wood, stone, and earth have stable statistical profiles (1/f visual spectra, moderate thermal effusivity, characteristic VOC signatures) that the brain's ecological heuristics are calibrated to. Synthetic materials that mimic these profiles on the most diagnostic dimension (visual appearance) while departing on secondary dimensions (thermal, haptic, olfactory) produce a subtle mismatch that the ecological heuristic system detects — the material "looks right" but "feels wrong." This is a form of cross-modal PE, but it is driven by ecological calibration, not just sensory prediction.

## Cross-Examination and Debate

### Debate 1: Is the Wood-Stress Effect Multi-Modal Convergence or Biophilic?

**Challenge** (from Doc 33 open question): Why does wood reduce stress? Is it (a) multi-modal convergence of fractal grain (T1), warm thermal (CT pathway), absorptive acoustics (AX1), and pleasant VOCs (olfactory), all operating through domain-general PE mechanisms? Or (b) an evolved biophilic preference for natural materials that operates through a mechanism beyond PE?

**Miyazaki**: The phytoncide data suggest at least one pathway that is independent of the sensory PE mechanisms. Inhaling hinoki oil reduces cortisol without any visual, haptic, or acoustic wood contact. This is not PE — no prediction is being generated or violated. It is a direct pharmacological or evolved-olfactory response to specific molecules.

**Gigerenzer**: But that does not require a "biophilic" mechanism. The fluency heuristic explains it: the brain is calibrated to natural volatile profiles. Terpenes from wood are ecologically familiar molecules that the olfactory system processes fluently. Synthetic VOCs (formaldehyde from particleboard, 4-PC from carpet) are ecologically novel molecules that the system processes with more effort or flags as potentially dangerous. The stress reduction from wood VOCs could be the ABSENCE of stress from synthetic VOCs rather than a positive biophilic signal.

**Spence**: The critical experiment would separate the channels. Compare: (1) real wood (all channels), (2) printed wood-grain laminate + wood scent diffuser + wooden acoustic panel behind the laminate (matched on visual, olfactory, and acoustic but with laminate thermal/haptic properties), (3) real wood with VOC sealant (visual, haptic, thermal, acoustic but no olfactory). If the stress reduction is multi-modal convergence, condition 1 should show the largest effect, with conditions 2 and 3 showing partial effects. If it is primarily olfactory-pharmacological, conditions 1 and 2 should be similar. If it is biophilic (beyond sensory convergence), condition 1 should show an additional effect beyond what the sensory channels predict.

**Morrison**: The CT pathway adds another testable component. If you compare wood at room temperature (22°C, so contact temperature ~25°C, within CT-optimal range) with wood cooled to 10°C (far outside CT range), the visual, acoustic, and olfactory channels are identical but the affective touch channel is eliminated. A significant decrement in stress reduction would implicate the CT pathway as a contributor.

**Klatzky**: And the haptic glance — the discriminative touch component — can be separated from the affective component by using gloves (which eliminate thermal and fine-texture signals while preserving gross shape and hardness) versus bare-hand contact.

**Panel consensus**: **The wood-stress effect is most parsimoniously explained as multi-modal convergence through domain-general mechanisms (visual PE, CT affective touch, acoustic absorption, olfactory familiarity), operating without requiring a specific "biophilic" mechanism.** The phytoncide pathway is real but is better modeled as olfactory-autonomic rather than biophilic — it operates through specific receptor-molecular interactions, not through an evolved preference for "naturalness" per se. However, the ecological-rationality account (Gigerenzer) adds value: the REASON natural materials engage these mechanisms favorably is that they match the statistical regularities the brain's heuristics are calibrated to. This ecological calibration is the explanatory bridge between "domain-general mechanisms" and "why natural materials specifically."

### Debate 2: Is Thermal Comfort Really About PE, or Is It Just Physiology?

**Challenge**: De Dear and Heschong describe thermal comfort in PE terms, but the body's thermoregulatory system is a physiological control system, not a perceptual prediction system. Is it appropriate to frame thermal comfort as prediction error, or is this forcing a PE interpretation onto a homeostatic mechanism?

**de Dear**: The adaptive model IS an empirical prediction error model, whether or not we use that terminology. The core finding — that comfort depends on the deviation of temperature from the ADAPTIVE expectation, not from an absolute setpoint — is exactly the structure of PE. The adaptive expectation IS the prediction. The temperature deviation IS the error. The comfort response IS the evaluation of error magnitude. The physiology (vasodilation, shivering, sweating) is the error-correction mechanism. Whether we call this "predictive processing" or "adaptive homeostasis" is terminological, not substantive.

**van Marken Lichtenbelt**: The metabolic evidence strengthens the PE framing. If thermal comfort were purely homeostatic, then maintaining the thermoneutral zone should be optimal — the body maintains temperature with minimal effort. But sustained TNZ exposure produces WORSE metabolic outcomes than moderate thermal challenge. This makes sense under PE: a prediction system that is never challenged becomes decalibrated. The circadian system (L2) shows the same pattern — constant light conditions degrade circadian function. The thermal system shows the same: constant thermal conditions degrade metabolic flexibility.

**Miodownik**: From a physics perspective, the body is not a passive thermal object — it actively regulates contact temperature at the skin surface. When the hand touches a cold surface, the body increases local blood flow to maintain skin temperature. When it touches a warm surface, it reduces blood flow. This active regulation IS a form of prediction and correction — the body predicts what skin temperature should be and adjusts to maintain it. The "comfort" signal arises when the correction effort is small (low PE); "discomfort" arises when the correction effort is large (high PE).

**Panel consensus**: **Thermal comfort is legitimately modeled as prediction error**, with the adaptive expectation as the prediction and temperature deviation as the error. The PE framework adds value over simple homeostasis by explaining: (a) why comfort depends on recent history (the prediction adapts), (b) why naturally ventilated buildings produce wider comfort ranges (the prediction has wider variance), and (c) why thermal variation is both delightful (moderate PE) and metabolically beneficial (the system stays calibrated). The template should note that this is a slower, physiological PE (hours-to-days timescale) distinct from perceptual PE (milliseconds-to-seconds), paralleling the circadian PE distinction made in Panel L-I.

### Debate 3: How Does the Light-Material Interaction Work Mechanistically?

**Challenge** (from Panel L-I, Holthuijsen's recommendation): The appearance of a material depends jointly on its reflectance properties and the illumination. How should this interaction be formalized — as a modifier on L1 (luminance PE), as a modifier on the material templates, or as a separate template?

**Spence**: The light-material interaction is a cross-modal prediction: vision generates a material-identity prediction (this surface is oak, this surface is marble) that includes expected color, luminance, and texture properties. These expected properties are computed GIVEN the current illumination. When the illumination changes (natural daylight to fluorescent to LED), the expected visual properties change. Color constancy (Hurlbert, from L-I panel) compensates partially, but when the illumination is extreme (narrow-band LED, colored theatrical light), constancy fails and the material appears different from the prediction. This is a visual PE event, but it is specifically about material identity — the surface appears to be a different material than expected.

**Miodownik**: Quantitatively, the interaction depends on the material's spectral reflectance and the illuminant's spectral power distribution. Broadband illuminants (daylight, incandescent) produce stable material appearances because the reflectance integral is well-sampled. Narrow-band illuminants (LEDs with spectral peaks) can produce metamerism — two surfaces that look identical under one illuminant but different under another. In architecture, this means a material palette selected under showroom lighting may look different when installed under the building's actual lighting. This is a form of light-material PE that is entirely predictable from physics but requires knowing both the reflectance and the illumination.

**Pallasmaa**: And there is a deeper interaction: light REVEALS material. Raking light across a stone surface reveals texture that flat light conceals. A shaft of light on a wooden floor reveals grain depth and variation that uniform lighting flattens. The material is the same, but the PERCEIVED material richness depends on the light. This means that L1 (luminance contrast) and the material template are not independent — the luminance PE from light is partially ABOUT material, because contrast between surfaces is contrast between material reflectances.

**Panel consensus**: **The light-material interaction should be formalized as a scope condition on L1 and on the material-identity template (to be written), not as a separate template.** L1 should note that the magnitude and spatial structure of luminance PE depend on material reflectance properties (high-reflectance-contrast material pairs produce stronger luminance PE than low-contrast pairs). The material-identity template should note that material identity prediction accuracy depends on illumination quality (broadband → accurate; narrow-band → potentially inaccurate). This connects the two template sets without requiring a third.

## Panel Synthesis: What the Five Templates Collectively Reveal

### Opening Remarks from the Chair

**Spence**: We have produced five templates: MAT1 (affective touch via CT pathway), MAT2 (thermal comfort as adaptive PE), MAT3 (multi-modal material identity integration), MAT4 (natural material convergence — the wood template), and MAT5 (material-cultural conditioning). Before we leave, the panel should be explicit about the structural architecture these templates reveal and what it means for Article Eater.

### The Dual-Touch Architecture Parallels the Dual-Light Architecture

**McGlone**: The most striking structural finding from this panel is the parallel with Panel L-I. Light has two pathways: image-forming (rods/cones → LGN → V1) and non-visual (ipRGCs → SCN → pineal). Touch has two pathways: discriminative (Aβ → S1) and affective (CT → posterior insula). In both cases, the same physical stimulus activates two neurally distinct systems with different cortical targets and different functional roles. In both cases, architecture can optimize for one pathway at the expense of the other. A polished steel surface is discriminatively rich (precise edge, uniform reflectance, high-frequency tactile signal) but affectively null (too cold for CT engagement). A soft, warm wooden surface is CT-optimal but discriminatively modest.

**Olausson**: And in both cases, the most satisfying architectural experience engages BOTH pathways simultaneously. The best lighting is both visually interesting (L1) and circadianly supportive (L2). The best material experience is both haptically informative (discriminative) and affectively pleasant (CT pathway). The dual-pathway architecture is emerging as a general principle of sensory-architectural interaction.

### The Convergence Principle Extends Across Domains

**Heschong**: I served on both panels — L-I and this one — and the convergence principle has now appeared twice. L3 (daylight) is a multi-channel convergence template: daylight simultaneously engages circadian, visual, attentional, temporal, and view channels. MAT4 (natural materials) is a multi-channel convergence template: wood simultaneously engages visual-fractal, haptic-thermal, acoustic, olfactory, and ecological channels. The structural parallel is striking and I think deliberate on the framework's part.

**Spence**: The convergence principle is exactly what multisensory integration research predicts. The brain does not evaluate a material through five independent channels and then add the results. It integrates them into a unified percept, with cross-modal predictions flowing between modalities. When all modalities confirm each other (the material looks, feels, sounds, and smells like what it IS), the integration is maximally fluent. When modalities conflict (the material looks like wood but feels like plastic), integration requires error resolution and the experience is degraded. This is not a domain-specific principle — it is how multisensory perception works.

**Gigerenzer**: And the convergence maps onto the ecological-rationality framework: natural environments present coherent multi-modal stimuli because physical reality is consistent. Wood looks warm because it IS warm (low effusivity). Stone looks cold because it IS cold (high effusivity). Water looks wet because it IS wet. The brain's ecological heuristics exploit this cross-modal consistency. Artificial materials that decouple the modalities — laminate that looks like wood but feels like plastic — violate the ecological regularity that the heuristics depend on.

### The Material-Light Interface

**Miodownik**: The panel has confirmed Holthuijsen's recommendation from L-I: the light-material interaction is real and mechanistically important, but it does NOT require a separate template. L1 (luminance contrast PE) operates on whatever luminance distribution the material reflectances and illumination jointly produce. MAT3 (material identity integration) includes a scope condition noting that material identity prediction accuracy depends on illumination quality. The connection is bidirectional: light reveals material properties, and material properties determine what light does in a space. But the mechanisms are already captured in the existing templates.

**Pallasmaa**: I want to make sure this does not become a purely quantitative statement. The experience of light on stone — the way raking light across a rough-hewn surface reveals the micro-geography of the stone, the way a sunlit patch on a wooden floor makes the grain glow — these are qualitative experiences that define architectural atmosphere. The templates capture the mechanism, but the designer needs to understand the experiential reality: material selection and lighting design are a single decision, not two separate ones.

### Where the Evidence Is Strong, Where It Is Contested

**McGlone**: MAT1 (affective touch) rests on solid neuroscience. The CT afferent system is well-characterized: we know the receptor type, the conduction velocity, the velocity-tuning curve, the temperature preference, and the cortical target. What we do NOT know is the full mapping from architectural material properties to CT activation magnitude. Morrison's parameters (velocity 1–10 cm/s, temperature ±2°C, moderate roughness) are established for bare-skin laboratory stimulation. Whether they transfer to architectural encounters — touching a handrail through a shirt sleeve, walking on a floor through shoe soles — is uncharacterized. The mechanism is established; the architectural parameter mapping is preliminary.

**de Dear**: MAT2 (thermal comfort as adaptive PE) has both the strongest empirical base and the most direct architectural translation. The adaptive model is ASHRAE Standard 55 — it IS the engineering tool. The PE reframing adds theoretical depth and connects thermal comfort to the other PE-based templates, but the practical predictions (wider comfort range in naturally ventilated buildings, occupant adaptation to seasonal variation) are already codified in engineering standards. This is our most calibrated template.

**Spence**: MAT3 (material identity integration) is conceptually well-grounded in the multisensory integration literature but lacks architectural-specific calibration. We know cross-modal material prediction operates — the visual-haptic interaction, the auditory-tactile interaction — but the specific parameters for architectural materials (at what level of visual-haptic discrepancy does the occupant detect "fakeness"?) have not been measured. Wastiels's work begins this mapping but is limited to a narrow range of materials.

**Miyazaki**: MAT4 (natural material convergence) has the strongest architectural-application evidence — the wood studies are numerous and consistent — but the weakest mechanistic decomposition. We know wood reduces stress; we do not know how much each channel contributes. The convergence model is the most plausible account, but it is still a hypothesis. The channel-separation experiment that Spence described in Debate 1 has not been conducted.

**Gigerenzer**: MAT5 (material-cultural conditioning) is well-supported by the general evaluative conditioning literature (Reber et al., 2004; De Houwer, Thomas, & Baeyens, 2001) but has almost no architectural-specific evidence. We know that learned associations affect material preferences — marble "means" luxury, raw concrete "means" brutalism — but the specific learned associations, their cross-cultural variability, and their interaction with the sensory mechanisms have not been systematically studied. This is our most preliminary template.

## Testable Predictions and Critical Experiments

### Panel Discussion: What Should We Predict, and What Would Falsify Us?

**Prediction 1: The CT-Material Hierarchy**

**Morrison**: MAT1 makes a specific prediction about the ordering of material pleasantness based on CT-optimal parameters. For hand-contact surfaces (handrails, countertops, furniture), materials should rank in pleasantness approximately as follows:

1. Sanded wood (low effusivity, moderate roughness, skin-temperature maintenance) → highest CT activation → most pleasant
2. Natural stone at room temperature (moderate effusivity, moderate roughness) → moderate CT activation → moderately pleasant
3. Smooth plastic at room temperature (low effusivity, low roughness) → CT activation from temperature but minimal texture → moderately pleasant but "boring"
4. Polished metal at room temperature (very high effusivity, no texture) → CT-aversive temperature drop, no texture signal → least pleasant

**Klatzky**: The critical control: repeat the test with all materials equilibrated to skin temperature (32°C) using heating elements. If the ranking collapses when temperature is controlled, the CT thermal preference IS the dominant mechanism. If the ranking persists, texture and other factors contribute independently.

**McGlone**: And repeat with gloved hands to eliminate all CT input. The gloved-hand ranking should be entirely discriminative (based on visual appearance and gross texture) and should NOT match the bare-hand ranking — specifically, polished metal may rank higher with gloves (visually striking, smooth to gross touch) than bare-handed (CT-aversive cold).

**Prediction 2: The Adaptive Thermal Tolerance Test**

**de Dear**: MAT2 predicts that occupants of thermally variable buildings develop wider thermal comfort ranges than occupants of mechanically conditioned buildings. The standard test uses adaptive comfort surveys, but a more incisive prediction:

Move two groups of office workers: Group A from constant-temperature offices (22±1°C year-round) to a naturally ventilated building (18–28°C seasonal range). Group B from naturally ventilated to constant-temperature. MAT2 predicts: Group A initially shows high thermal PE (the prediction is narrow, the environment is variable) → reported discomfort. Over 2–4 weeks, the thermal prediction widens and comfort restores. Group B initially shows low thermal PE (narrow environment matches narrow prediction) → high comfort. Over months, Group B's thermal prediction narrows further, and they become LESS tolerant of any variation — if the AC fails and temperature reaches 26°C, Group B reports MORE discomfort than Group A would at the same temperature after adaptation.

**van Marken Lichtenbelt**: And the metabolic prediction: Group A shows improved metabolic flexibility (better insulin sensitivity, increased BAT activity) over months of exposure to thermal variation. Group B shows metabolic decline. This is testable with standard metabolic panels.

**Prediction 3: The Wood Channel-Separation Experiment**

**Spence**: MAT4 (natural material convergence) predicts that the wood stress-reduction effect is the sum of multiple independent channels. The definitive experiment:

| Condition | Visual | Haptic-thermal | Acoustic | Olfactory | Predicted stress reduction |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Real wood room | Wood grain | Low effusivity | Absorptive | Terpenes | 100% (baseline) |
| Laminate + wood scent | Wood grain | High effusivity | Variable | Terpenes | ~60% (visual + olfactory, no haptic-thermal) |
| Real wood, sealed | Wood grain | Low effusivity | Absorptive | Blocked | ~70% (all except olfactory) |
| Real wood, cooled to 10°C | Wood grain | CT-aversive | Absorptive | Terpenes | ~65% (all except affective touch) |
| Wood-grain wallpaper on steel | Wood grain | CT-aversive | Reflective | None | ~30% (visual only) |
| Unpainted MDF (no grain) | No grain | Low effusivity | Absorptive | Mild VOC | ~40% (haptic-thermal + acoustic, no visual or olfactory) |

MAT4 predicts the real-wood condition shows maximum stress reduction, with each degraded condition showing a partial but significant decrement. If ANY single condition matches the real-wood baseline, convergence is wrong and that single channel is sufficient. If the wood-grain-wallpaper condition shows ZERO effect, the visual-only channel is insufficient and the non-visual channels are necessary.

**Miyazaki**: The critical falsification: if the hinoki oil inhalation condition (olfactory only, no other wood contact) produces stress reduction equal to the real-wood room, then the phytoncide pathway dominates and convergence is unnecessary.

**Prediction 4: The Material Authenticity Detection Threshold**

**Pallasmaa**: MAT3 predicts that cross-modal material PE (the "fakeness" signal) depends on the NUMBER and MAGNITUDE of modality discrepancies. A simple hierarchy:

- High-quality veneer on solid wood substrate (visual match, thermal match, acoustic match, only micro-tactile edge-grain mismatch) → low material PE, rarely detected
- Laminate on MDF (visual approximate match, thermal mismatch, acoustic mismatch, no olfactory) → moderate material PE, detectable by attentive occupants
- Printed film on steel (visual mismatch at close range, thermal mismatch, acoustic mismatch, olfactory mismatch) → high material PE, universally detected

**Klatzky**: The test: present occupants with material samples spanning this authenticity gradient and measure (a) explicit detection ("is this real wood?"), (b) implicit processing fluency (response time in a material-categorization task), and (c) physiological stress markers. MAT3 predicts that even when explicit detection fails (the veneer "fools" the eye), implicit processing fluency is lower for fake materials — the multisensory system detects the inconsistency even when conscious judgment does not.

**Prediction 5: The Thermal Delight Inverted-U**

**Heschong**: MAT2 combined with the Goldilocks principle predicts an inverted-U function for thermal variation and pleasure:

- No thermal variation (constant HVAC, 22±0.5°C) → no thermal PE → no thermal delight, metabolic stagnation
- Moderate thermal variation (natural ventilation, radiant asymmetry, material temperature contrasts: 18–26°C range with slow transitions) → moderate thermal PE → thermal delight, metabolic benefit
- Excessive thermal variation (drafty, poorly insulated, wild temperature swings: 12–35°C) → high thermal PE → thermal distress, allostatic overload

The peak of the inverted-U should correspond approximately to de Dear's adaptive comfort range for naturally ventilated buildings. Below the peak is the "thermally dead" zone of modern sealed buildings. Above the peak is the "thermally hostile" zone of poorly designed buildings.

## Open Questions, Unfinished Business, and Panel MAT-II Prospectus

### What a Second Materials Panel Would Do

**Spence**: Like L-II, a second materials panel would focus on empirical calibration rather than theoretical extension. The framework is built. It needs tuning.

**MAT-II Priority 1: Calibrate MAT4 (Natural Material Convergence) — The Channel-Separation Experiment**

This is the most architecturally consequential calibration target. The wood-stress question is one of the most-cited findings in the architecture-wellbeing literature, and our answer ("multi-modal convergence") is a hypothesis. MAT-II must either confirm it with channel-weight estimates or identify a dominant single channel.

**What MAT-II would produce**: Channel weights for the five convergence channels (visual-fractal, haptic-thermal, acoustic, olfactory, ecological), based on the channel-separation experiment described in Prediction 3. If convergent, estimated relative contributions (e.g., visual ~20%, haptic-thermal ~30%, acoustic ~15%, olfactory ~25%, ecological ~10%). If not convergent, identification of the dominant channel.

**Panelists who would return**: Spence (cross-modal design), Morrison (CT parameters), Miyazaki (physiological measurement), Miodownik (material parameter specification).

**Additional panelist needed**: Someone running or designing channel-separation studies — possibly Burnard (InnoRenew CoE, Slovenia, wood and wellbeing, ~100 citations) or Nyrud (Norwegian Institute of Wood Technology, psychophysiological responses to wood, ~200 citations).

**MAT-II Priority 2: Calibrate MAT1 (CT Pathway) for Architectural Contact Scenarios**

The CT velocity/temperature parameters are from bare-skin laboratory stimulation. Architecture involves clothed contact (shoe soles on floors, sleeve on countertop), brief contact (hand touching a doorknob), and indirect thermal contact (radiant warmth from a material). MAT-II must extend the CT model to these real-world contact modes.

**What MAT-II would produce**: Modified CT activation estimates for key architectural contact scenarios: barefoot on floor materials (wood, stone, tile, carpet), hand on railing/countertop materials, seated contact with chair surfaces. For each scenario: estimated CT activation as a function of material thermal effusivity and surface roughness, accounting for clothing insulation where relevant.

**Panelists who would return**: McGlone (CT physiology), Morrison (velocity/temperature parameters), Klatzky (haptic perception in real-world conditions).

**Additional panelist needed**: A textile/ergonomics scientist who studies how clothing mediates surface contact — possibly Gerrett (Loughborough, skin thermal physiology) or Filingeri (Loughborough/Southampton, thermal sensation through clothing).

**MAT-II Priority 3: Map MAT3 (Material Identity Integration) Detection Thresholds**

When does the occupant detect that a material is "fake"? At what level of cross-modal discrepancy does material PE become consciously detectable? These thresholds determine how much latitude designers have with veneers, laminates, and surface treatments before the multisensory system flags the inconsistency.

**What MAT-II would produce**: Discrepancy detection thresholds for key material-veneer pairings: wood veneer on MDF (what veneer thickness preserves acoustic and thermal properties?), stone veneer on concrete (at what thickness does the thermal and acoustic signature diverge detectably from solid stone?), printed-film simulacra (at what viewing distance and contact duration is the simulation detected?).

**Panelists who would return**: Klatzky (haptic detection), Spence (cross-modal discrepancy), Miodownik (material parameter specification), Zampini (auditory-tactile interaction).

**MAT-II Scope Exclusions — Work That Belongs Elsewhere**

**Pallasmaa**: The phenomenology of material aging and patina — how materials change over time, how weathering produces or destroys beauty — is a temporal-material interaction that connects MAT templates to A10 (Temporal). A dedicated Temporal panel could address this alongside circadian architecture (L2) and seasonal adaptation (MAT2).

**de Dear**: Thermal zoning and microclimate design — how different zones of a building can provide different thermal environments — is an architectural-configuration topic (A3) that uses MAT2 predictions but is not a material question per se.

**Gigerenzer**: Individual and cultural variation in material preferences is a framework-level issue, paralleling the individual-differences gap flagged in L-I. Cross-cultural material-association studies would inform MAT5 but require ethnographic methods that are not standard panel fare.

- A1 coverage: ★★★ → ★★★★ (convergence model confirmed and parameterized)
- A7 coverage: ★★★ → ★★★★ (CT model extended to architectural contact, thermal model already calibrated)
