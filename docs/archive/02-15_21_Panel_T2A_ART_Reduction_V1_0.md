## Part 1: ART Overview and Construct Definitions

### 1.1 Rachel Kaplan — What ART Actually Claims

I want to begin by correcting some common misunderstandings about ART before the panel attempts reductions. Stephen and I developed ART over several decades (S. Kaplan & Talbot, 1983; R. Kaplan & S. Kaplan, 1989; S. Kaplan, 1995), and the theory has been simplified in ways that lose important nuance.

**The core claim of ART**: Directed attention (voluntary, effortful, top-down attention) is a finite resource that fatigues with sustained use. Certain environments restore this resource by engaging a different attentional mode — involuntary attention ("fascination") — that allows directed attention mechanisms to rest and recover. The restoration is not merely the absence of demand; it is an active process that occurs in environments with specific properties.

**The four properties** are not independent modules — they are interacting conditions that jointly determine the restorative potential of an environment:

1. **Fascination**: The environment holds attention without effort. But there is a critical distinction we always made between "soft" and "hard" fascination. Soft fascination (clouds, sunsets, flowing water) engages attention gently and allows room for reflection. Hard fascination (watching a car crash, a football game) demands attention fully and does not permit reflection. Only soft fascination is restorative.

2. **Being Away**: A sense of physical or psychological distance from one's usual environment and concerns. This is not merely "being in a different place" — you can be physically away but mentally still at work. Being Away requires a *cognitive shift* — setting aside the mental content that was demanding directed attention.

3. **Extent**: The environment has enough content and structure to occupy the mind, creating a sense of being in a "whole other world." Extent has two sub-components: *scope* (there is enough to see and experience) and *coherence* (the content hangs together, it is not random or chaotic).

4. **Compatibility**: There is a match between the person's purposes and the environmental supports and demands. A nature walk is compatible because the environment supports what you want to do (walk, observe, reflect) without requiring actions you don't want to take. An unfamiliar and confusing building is incompatible because you want to get somewhere but the environment frustrates that goal.

**Critical point the panel must understand**: These four properties are *jointly necessary*. An environment with soft fascination but no extent (a single pretty flower in a hospital bed) provides minimal restoration. An environment with extent but no compatibility (the Grand Canyon when you're terrified of heights) provides minimal restoration. The reductive exercise must capture these interactions, not just decompose each construct independently.

## Part 2: Reduction 1 — Soft Fascination

### 2.1 Panel Discussion

**Opus**: Panel D-1b produced a corrected 10-edge DAG for Soft Fascination. Rather than re-derive it, I ask this panel to review, validate, amend, and formalize it. The D-1b DAG has: auditory scene analysis → salience network ← visual PE, with salience network controlling DMN↔CEN switching, PPA/RSC bridging to medial temporal DMN subsystem, and a constitutive link from medial temporal DMN engagement to the fascination experience.

**R. Kaplan**: The D-1b DAG captures the neural mechanism well, but it misses the *reflective* component. Soft fascination is not just "DMN is engaged." It is DMN engaged *in a specific mode* — reflective thought, rumination-free mind-wandering. The distinction between restorative mind-wandering and ruminative mind-wandering is critical. Rumination engages the same DMN core subsystem but in a self-focused, repetitive, negatively-valenced loop. Restoration requires a shift *away* from rumination toward constructive internal processing.

**Andrews-Hanna**: Rachel is exactly right, and the neural distinction maps onto my subsystem model. Rumination is characterized by high connectivity between the core subsystem (amPFC, PCC) and the dorsal medial subsystem (dmPFC), with reduced medial temporal subsystem engagement. Restorative mind-wandering shows the opposite: high core-to-medial temporal connectivity (scene construction, episodic memory, prospective thought) with reduced dorsal medial engagement. The D-1b DAG specifies medial temporal DMN engagement, which is correct, but should additionally specify that dorsal medial DMN engagement should NOT be elevated — otherwise you get rumination, not restoration.

**Berman**: This connects to the Bratman et al. (2015) finding. They measured subgenual PFC (sgPFC) activity and self-reported rumination before and after nature vs. urban walks. The nature walk reduced sgPFC activity and rumination. The sgPFC is part of the core subsystem and is implicated in self-referential negative affect — exactly the node that drives rumination. So the mechanism may be: nature reduces sgPFC ruminative activity, which permits the core subsystem to connect to the medial temporal subsystem (constructive internal processing) rather than to the dorsal medial subsystem (ruminative loop).

**Uddin**: I want to add that the salience network's role in this is more specific than D-1b captured. The anterior insula doesn't just control DMN↔CEN switching — it also modulates *which DMN subsystem* is active. Recent work (Uddin, Yeo, & Spreng, 2019) suggests the salience network gates information flow between DMN subsystems. In a threatening or stressful environment, SN may preferentially engage the dorsal medial subsystem (social threat monitoring). In a safe, non-demanding environment, SN allows the medial temporal subsystem to dominate. This gives us a mechanism for why natural environments specifically promote restorative (not ruminative) mind-wandering: the non-threatening, non-socially-demanding character of nature biases the SN toward medial temporal DMN engagement.

**Hunter**: I want to add the dose-response data. In our "nature pill" study (Hunter, Gillespie, & Chen, 2019), we found that 20–30 minutes of nature exposure produced the greatest rate of cortisol reduction, with diminishing returns after that. This suggests the restorative mechanism has a temporal dynamic: it takes ~10 minutes for the salience network to habituate to the low-threat, low-demand environment and allow sustained DMN engagement, and the maximum restorative benefit accumulates over the next 20 minutes. Beyond 30 minutes, the person may become bored or restless (insufficient fascination to sustain engagement).

**Joye**: The visual component needs more specificity. In D-1b, Bar described the magnocellular gist → prediction error mechanism. I want to add: natural scenes have specific mid-range fractal dimensions (D ≈ 1.3–1.5) that produce maximal processing fluency — the visual system processes them with minimal effort because they match the spatial frequency tuning of early visual cortex (Spehar et al., 2003; Taylor, Spehar, Van Donkelaar, & Hagerhall, 2011). This fluency is the perceptual basis of soft fascination: the visual input is *interesting enough to look at* (nonzero PE) but *easy to process* (high fluency). The fluency signal itself may contribute to positive affect via the hedonic marking hypothesis (Reber, Schwarz, & Winkielman, 2004).

**White**: I want to flag that water features add an additional component. In the BlueHealth studies (White et al., 2020), aquatic environments showed stronger restorative effects than green environments. The mechanism may be partly auditory (water sounds are broadband noise that masks speech — connecting to Janata's point about speech salience) and partly visual (water movement provides continuous moderate visual PE with very high predictability at the statistical level — you can't predict the exact motion of each ripple, but you can predict the distribution of motions). Water may be the optimal soft fascination stimulus because it simultaneously satisfies the auditory (low speech salience) and visual (moderate PE + high fluency) conditions.

**Kahn**: Let me push on the sufficiency question. In our "technological nature" studies (Kahn et al., 2008), we compared real nature windows with plasma screen nature displays. The real window provided significantly more restoration than the screen. Both had identical visual content. The difference must be something the screen lacks — my hypothesis is that it's the *multisensory coherence*. A real window provides correlated visual, auditory, olfactory, and thermal information. A screen provides only visual. The brain's prediction machinery detects this incoherence (visual nature + office auditory/olfactory/thermal), and the prediction error from the cross-modal mismatch partially disrupts the restorative state. This means the soft fascination reduction needs a multisensory coherence term.

**CONSENSUS on Soft Fascination amendments to D-1b DAG**:
1. Add **anti-rumination specification**: the constitutive link (E8) should specify medial temporal DMN engagement AND suppressed dorsal medial (ruminative) engagement
2. Add **SN subsystem gating**: the salience network (E5) doesn't just control DMN↔CEN switching — it biases which DMN subsystem dominates
3. Add **dose-response temporal dynamics**: onset ~10min (SN habituation), peak benefit 20–30min, diminishing returns after 30min (Hunter)
4. Add **processing fluency edge**: fractal fluency → positive affect → sustained engagement (Joye)
5. Add **multisensory coherence moderator**: cross-modal congruence enhances restoration; incongruence disrupts it (Kahn)
6. Add **water-specific pathway**: aquatic features provide simultaneous auditory masking + visual moderate PE (White)

## Part 3: Reduction 2 — Being Away

### 3.1 Panel Discussion

**R. Kaplan**: Being Away is the most psychologically subtle of the four ART constructs. It is NOT merely physical relocation. You can be physically in nature and mentally still at work — checking your phone, worrying about deadlines. Being Away requires a *cognitive context shift* — the mental content associated with directed attention fatigue must be set aside, replaced by a different frame of reference.

**Andrews-Hanna**: This maps directly onto DMN context processing. The DMN medial temporal subsystem performs *mental context construction* — building a mental model of "where I am and what I'm doing" that frames all other processing. A cognitive context shift means the medial temporal subsystem constructs a new context model that is incompatible with the previous one (the work context). Once the new context is active, the work-related thoughts cannot be maintained because they don't fit the active context model.

**Berman**: The hippocampus is central to this. Context-dependent memory means that retrieval of work-related concerns is easier in work-like contexts (office, desk, computer) and harder in novel contexts (forest, beach, unfamiliar city). Physical displacement to a novel environment literally makes it harder for the brain to retrieve the mental content that was causing directed attention fatigue. This is not metaphorical — it is a well-established property of hippocampal context-dependent memory encoding (Godden & Baddeley, 1975; Smith & Vela, 2001).

**Ellard**: In our urban walking studies, we see this context shift in physiological data. When participants walk from a busy commercial street into a park, there is a measurable transition period (~3–5 minutes) during which EDA (electrodermal activity) drops, HRV increases (vagal tone rising), and self-reported rumination decreases. The transition is not instantaneous — it takes time for the new context to fully replace the old one. This transition time is the period during which the hippocampal context model is being reconstructed.

**Kahn**: The "being away" feeling can be produced by design without physical displacement. A well-designed contemplation garden within a hospital can produce Being Away if it creates sufficient perceptual distinction from the clinical environment — different materials, different sounds, different spatial scale. The critical variable is *perceptual discontinuity* from the prior context, not physical distance.

**Uddin**: From the salience network perspective, Being Away involves a related but distinct mechanism from Soft Fascination. In Soft Fascination, the SN stays below threshold (no switching). In Being Away, the SN may actually *trigger a switch* — but a beneficial one. Entering a novel environment that is non-threatening activates the SN (novelty detection) which initiates a context shift: the current cognitive set (work mode) is abandoned, and a new cognitive set (exploration/restoration mode) is constructed. The key is that this is a *single switch* followed by sustained engagement in the new mode, rather than the constant switching that fatigues attention.

**Bar**: The prediction machinery supports this. When you enter a novel natural environment, the magnocellular gist generates predictions based on the new context ("this is a forest"), which are very different from the predictions that were active in the old context ("this is an office"). The new prediction set is incompatible with the old one — you literally cannot maintain office-related expectations while your visual cortex is generating forest-related predictions. The prediction error from context change is initially large (the first few minutes) and then settles as the new predictions become calibrated. This settling period corresponds to the ~3–5 minute transition Ellard observes.

**Hunter**: The dose-response data show that cortisol reduction begins almost immediately upon entering a natural environment (within 5 minutes) but the rate of reduction accelerates between 10–20 minutes. I interpret the first 5 minutes as the "Being Away" phase — context shift, initial HPA downregulation — and the 10–20 minute acceleration as the "Soft Fascination" phase — sustained DMN engagement producing deeper restoration.

## Part 4: Reduction 3 — Extent

### 4.1 Panel Discussion

**R. Kaplan**: Extent is the least studied ART construct and perhaps the most interesting architecturally. Extent means the environment has *enough richness and structure to occupy the mind* — it feels like a "whole other world." There are two components: **scope** (there is enough content — not just one tree but a forest, not just a garden but a landscape) and **coherence** (the content hangs together — it forms a legible, interpretable whole, not a random collection).

**Bar**: Extent's scope component connects directly to the predictive processing framework. An environment with high scope provides a deep, hierarchical prediction structure: scene-level predictions ("this is a forest"), mid-level predictions ("that region contains a path"), object-level predictions ("that is a birch tree"), and detail-level predictions ("the bark has this texture"). The brain can descend this hierarchy as deeply as it wants, always finding more to predict and more to engage with. An environment with low scope (a blank wall, a featureless room) has a shallow prediction hierarchy — the brain runs out of things to predict quickly, and boredom ensues.

**Berman**: This connects to something we've been studying. The preference for high-scope natural scenes correlates with their information-theoretic richness. Natural scenes have high *entropy* at multiple spatial scales — there is always more detail to discover. Urban scenes can have high entropy too, but it's often *adversarial* entropy (text, signs, advertisements) that demands directed attention rather than permitting involuntary engagement.

**Joye**: The coherence component is about fractal structure. Fractals are, by definition, self-similar across scales — the structure you see at the macro level repeats at the micro level. This means the brain's predictions are *transferable* across scales: once you've encoded the statistical structure at one scale, the same prediction works at all other scales. This is why fractals feel coherent — the prediction model is simple despite the visual complexity. An environment with high scope but low coherence (a junkyard: lots of stuff but no pattern) is not restorative because the prediction models at different scales are unrelated.

**Andrews-Hanna**: The neural substrate for Extent is primarily the medial temporal DMN subsystem — specifically, the hippocampal *scene construction* function (Hassabis & Maguire, 2007). When the hippocampus constructs a rich, coherent scene model (high scope + high coherence), the result is a sense of immersion — "being in a whole other world." This scene construction process engages the hippocampus, parahippocampal cortex, and retrosplenial cortex, all of which are medial temporal DMN nodes.

The key insight: Extent provides the *content* that Soft Fascination engages with. Without Extent, the medial temporal DMN subsystem has nothing to construct scenes about. This is why Rachel says the four constructs are jointly necessary — Soft Fascination is the mode of engagement, and Extent is the material it engages with.

**Kahn**: I want to challenge the assumption that Extent requires physical scale. In our studies, even small nature elements (a tabletop garden, a window with a view) can produce some sense of extent if they imply a larger world beyond what is visible. A vista through a window suggests scope beyond the window frame. The brain *infers* extent from partial cues — you don't have to show the entire forest, just enough that the brain can construct the rest.

**Ellard**: This is measurable. In our studies, we find that participants' physiological responses (reduced EDA, increased HRV) increase with *perceived* environmental depth, not just physical depth. A space that creates an illusion of depth (receding sightlines, depth cues, layered planes) produces similar responses to a genuinely deep space. The brain's scene construction apparatus responds to depth *cues*, not to physical depth per se.

## Part 5: Reduction 4 — Compatibility

### 5.1 Panel Discussion

**R. Kaplan**: Compatibility is the most neglected ART construct in the empirical literature, yet it may be the most architecturally important. Compatibility means: the environment supports what you want to do without demanding things you don't want to do. When you walk in nature, you want to walk and observe — and the environment supports exactly that. When you're lost in an unfamiliar hospital, you want to find a room — and the environment frustrates that goal. Incompatibility produces directed attention demand even in otherwise restorative settings.

**Berman**: Rachel is right that it's neglected. The empirical literature on Compatibility is essentially the person-environment fit literature from environmental psychology (Kaplan, 1983), not the cognitive neuroscience literature. We have almost no neural data on how the brain computes compatibility or incompatibility.

**Bar**: I can offer a predictive processing account. Compatibility is the match between the brain's *action predictions* (what I expect to be able to do in this space) and the environment's *affordances* (what the space actually allows). When I enter a park, my brain predicts "I can walk freely, stop anywhere, sit on that bench." If the park confirms these predictions (smooth path, available bench, no barriers), PE is low and compatibility is high. If the park violates these predictions (muddy impassable path, bench removed, "keep off grass" signs), PE is high in the action domain and compatibility is low.

This is distinct from perceptual PE (which drives Soft Fascination). Compatibility involves *action PE* — prediction errors about what you can and cannot do. The neural substrate for action PE is different from perceptual PE: it involves the premotor cortex, supplementary motor area, and dorsolateral PFC for action planning, rather than the visual cortex for perceptual prediction.

**Uddin**: Incompatibility produces a specific kind of salience signal. When the environment frustrates goal-directed action, the error is detected by the dACC (dorsal anterior cingulate cortex) — the error monitoring component of the salience network. The dACC signals that current actions are not producing expected outcomes, which triggers cognitive control engagement: "I need to figure out what to do differently." This is precisely the kind of directed attention demand that ART says fatigues.

So: incompatibility → dACC error signal → cognitive control engagement → directed attention demand → fatigue. This is the mechanism by which an incompatible environment undermines restoration *even if* the other three conditions (Fascination, Being Away, Extent) are met.

**Ellard**: I can add a proxemics dimension. Compatibility includes the match between the space's social expectations and the person's social desires. A space designed for group interaction (communal table, no private corners) is incompatible for someone seeking solitude. A space designed for solitary work (cubicles, no gathering areas) is incompatible for someone seeking social connection. This connects to T48 (Social Affordance Reading) — the social compatibility of a space is read through the mentalizing system.

**Aspinall**: Ecologically, compatibility is the most measurable ART construct. You can directly observe person-environment fit: do people use the space as the designer intended? Do they adapt the space (moving furniture, finding workarounds)? Do they leave? Behavioral mismatch — when people's actions don't match the space's intended use — is a visible indicator of low compatibility. It can also be measured via self-report (PRS compatibility subscale) and physiologically (frustration produces elevated frontal theta and increased EDA).

## Part 6: The Four Constructs as a System

### 6.1 Inter-Construct Interactions

**R. Kaplan**: Now that we have individual reductions, I want to insist that the panel formalize the interactions I described at the start. These four constructs are not independent — they are a system.

**Opus**: Agreed. Based on the discussion, here is the interaction structure:

```
TEMPORAL SEQUENCE:
  1. Being Away (context shift) — opens the door to restoration (~0-5 min)
  2. Soft Fascination (sustained DMN engagement) — the restoration mechanism (~5-30 min)
  3. Extent provides the content that sustains Fascination (concurrent with 2)
  4. Compatibility is the permission condition (must hold throughout)

DEPENDENCY STRUCTURE:
  Being Away → enables → Soft Fascination
    (context shift permits DMN engagement; without it, habitual concerns persist)
    
  Extent → sustains → Soft Fascination  
    (environmental richness provides ongoing engagement material;
     without it, fascination wanes after novelty habituates)
    
  Compatibility → permits → {Being Away, Soft Fascination, Extent}
    (incompatibility produces directed attention demand that undermines all three;
     without it, the environment works against the person)
    
  Soft Fascination → constitutes → Restoration
    (the actual restorative mechanism — sustained DMN medial temporal engagement
     with suppressed rumination)

FAILURE MODES:
  Missing Being Away: Person is physically in nature but mentally at work → no restoration
  Missing Fascination: Person has shifted context but environment is boring → boredom, not restoration
  Missing Extent: Person is fascinated initially but environment has no depth → fascination fades
  Missing Compatibility: Person is in a fascinating, extensive, away environment
    but can't find the exit / is too cold / is socially overwhelmed → stress overrides restoration
```

### 7.3 Panel Assessment

**Berman**: The 24% unaccounted rate is significant. It tells us that ART's empirical footprint extends beyond the four-construct model. The most important unaccounted clusters are creativity (which may relate to medial temporal DMN's role in divergent thinking) and olfactory contributions (which are a genuine gap — no template covers olfactory processing). These should be noted as potential future template targets.

**R. Kaplan**: The creativity cluster doesn't surprise me. Stephen and I always considered creativity enhancement a *downstream consequence* of restoration — once directed attention capacity is restored, it can be deployed for creative problem-solving more effectively. But there may also be a direct mechanism: sustained medial temporal DMN engagement (the scene construction, prospective thinking mode) is precisely the neural substrate associated with divergent thinking and creative insight (Beaty et al., 2016). So soft fascination may directly enhance creativity, not just via restored attention.
