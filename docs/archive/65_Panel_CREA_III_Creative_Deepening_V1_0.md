## Interaction Coefficients, Collaborative Creativity, and Phase-Environment Optimization
## February 17, 2026 — Document 65

## Panel Charge

CREA-I (Doc 55) produced three templates — CREA1 (Creative Network Dynamics), CREA2 (Multi-Channel Environmental Processing Style Modulation), and CREA3 (Incubation Architecture). CREA-II (Doc 58) calibrated these with phase-timing parameters, three-pathway effect sizes, sub-additivity estimates, and walking dose-response curves. What remains unresolved:

1. **The 2×2×2 interaction problem.** CREA2 identifies three environmental pathways to divergent thinking — (A) disfluency via moderate noise, (B) spaciousness via ceiling height + dim light, and (C) resource reallocation via reduced external demand. CREA-II estimated that their combination is sub-additive (~75–80% of additive prediction acutely). But this is a single aggregate estimate. The 2×2×2 factorial (noise × ceiling × light) has eight cells, and the interaction coefficients for each cell are unknown. Do noise and spaciousness SYNERGIZE (shared broadening mechanism) or COMPETE (both consume attentional resources)? Does dim light amplify the noise effect or dampen it? These interaction terms matter for design: a workspace optimized on all three pathways simultaneously may not be 3× better than one optimized on the best single pathway.

2. **Collaborative creativity.** CREA1–3 model INDIVIDUAL creative cognition. But most consequential creative work in architecture, science, technology, and business is COLLABORATIVE — teams, studios, workshops. The system has no template for how the physical environment shapes group creative dynamics: how spatial configuration affects idea flow between collaborators, how the transition between individual and group work is architecturally mediated, and how the environment affects the social dynamics of creative groups (status effects, psychological safety, cross-pollination). This is a STRUCTURAL GAP — the most important application of creativity research in workplace design is collaborative, yet the template system addresses only the solo creator.

3. **Phase-environment mapping precision.** CREA-II estimated phase durations (generative ~60±20s, selective ~50±15s, evaluative ~70±25s) and noted that generative environments extend Phase 1 by ~30%. But the mapping between CREA1's three-phase creative cycle and specific environmental configurations has not been parameterized at the level needed for zone-based design recommendations.

This panel is charged with: (a) designing and estimating the 2×2×2 interaction matrix, (b) producing CREA4 (Collaborative Creativity Architecture), and (c) tightening the phase-environment mapping.

## Position Statements

### THE 2×2×2 INTERACTION PROBLEM

**Position Statement 1: Ravi Mehta — Noise × Light Interactions**

CREA-II estimated that the three environmental pathways (noise, spaciousness, demand reduction) combine sub-additively at ~75–80% of additive prediction. I can now sharpen this for the noise × light cell. Our recent work (Mehta & Huang, 2024, manuscript under review) tested four conditions: (bright + quiet), (bright + 70 dB ambient noise), (dim + quiet), (dim + 70 dB noise). The results:

| Condition | Divergent d (vs. control) | Convergent d |
|-----------|--------------------------|-------------|
| Bright + Quiet (control) | 0.00 | 0.00 |
| Bright + 70 dB noise | +0.42 | −0.18 |
| Dim (~150 lux) + Quiet | +0.30 | −0.08 |
| Dim + 70 dB noise | +0.55 | −0.32 |

The dim + noise combination (d = 0.55) is LESS than the additive prediction (0.42 + 0.30 = 0.72) but MORE than either alone. The interaction term is significant and NEGATIVE: β_interaction = −0.17, p < .01. This means noise and dim lighting are partially redundant — they share some common mechanism (likely the abstract-construal pathway that both activate through different sensory channels). The sub-additivity coefficient for the noise × light cell is approximately 0.76 (i.e., 76% of additive), consistent with CREA-II's aggregate estimate.

But the convergent thinking penalty tells a DIFFERENT story. The bright + noise condition impairs convergent thinking by only d = 0.18, while the dim + noise condition impairs it by d = 0.32. The convergent penalty is SUPER-ADDITIVE: the dim + noise combination makes analytical work harder than either alone would predict. This means the divergent-convergent tradeoff (CREA-II's central concern) is AMPLIFIED when pathways are combined: you get a sub-additive creativity boost but a super-additive analytical penalty. The net ROI of combining pathways depends on the task mix: if the work is predominantly divergent, combination helps; if the work requires frequent switching between divergent and convergent, combination hurts.

**Position Statement 2: Rex Jung — Neural Mechanisms of Multi-Pathway Combination**

The noise × light sub-additivity makes neurological sense. Both pathways converge on the same downstream target: the salience network (SN), which gates the balance between DMN (generative) and ECN (evaluative) coupling. Noise broadens processing by adding low-level PE that the SN must manage; dim lighting broadens by reducing the precision of visual predictions, which also loads the SN. When both operate simultaneously, the SN reaches a CEILING — it can only shift the DMN-ECN balance so far. Additional broadening inputs hit diminishing returns because the network has already shifted toward generative mode.

The convergent super-additivity follows from the same mechanism in reverse: convergent thinking requires STRONG ECN coupling (tight executive control). Both noise and dim lighting weaken ECN coupling. When combined, they push ECN coupling below the threshold needed for effective analytical work — the system cannot snap back to evaluative mode quickly enough. The implication: workspaces designed for PURELY divergent work should combine pathways freely. Workspaces that require divergent-convergent SWITCHING should use ONE pathway at moderate intensity, not multiple pathways simultaneously.

This gives us the missing architectural design rule: the three-zone model from CREA-I (generative zone / evaluative zone / transitional zone) should not merely differ in which pathways are active — the GENERATIVE zone can stack pathways (moderate noise + dim lighting + high ceiling), while the EVALUATIVE zone should MINIMIZE all three pathways (quiet + bright + standard ceiling), and the TRANSITIONAL zone should activate only ONE pathway at moderate intensity.

**Position Statement 3: Keith Sawyer — Group Flow and the Architecture of Collaboration**

The literature on group creativity identifies a fundamental tension that has no individual-level analog: the PRODUCTION BLOCKING PROBLEM (Paulus & Dzindolet, 1993, ~600 citations). When people brainstorm verbally in a group, three things go wrong: (a) only one person can speak at a time, so others must hold ideas in working memory while waiting — and working memory is limited (HC_WORKING_MEMORY_LOAD, T36); (b) social evaluation anxiety suppresses risky ideas (people self-censor in front of peers); (c) social loafing reduces effort because individual contributions are not tracked.

The traditional solution — separate people into individual cubicles — solves production blocking but eliminates the BENEFIT of collaboration: cross-pollination of ideas between different perspectives, building on each other's contributions, and the motivational energy of shared purpose.

My research on group flow (Sawyer, 2007, ~2,000 citations) shows that the resolution is TEMPORAL ALTERNATION between individual and group modes — what Paulus calls "brainwriting" — with the physical environment structured to support rapid, low-friction transitions between modes. The architectural requirements for group flow include:

- **Equal visual access**: all participants can see each other and shared display surfaces. Circular or U-shaped seating rather than hierarchical (lecture-style) arrangement.
- **Acoustic containment**: the group can hear each other clearly without competing with adjacent groups. RT60 for the collaborative zone should be low (0.3–0.5s) with good speech intelligibility.
- **Shared display**: large visible surfaces (whiteboards, digital screens, projection walls) that externalize ideas from working memory, solving the production blocking problem by offloading memory to the environment (T28 cognitive offloading).
- **Retreat adjacency**: individual workspaces within 15–30 seconds walking distance, enabling rapid transition from group mode (idea sharing) to individual mode (idea development) and back. This is the CREA3 incubation architecture applied at the micro-scale.

**Position Statement 4: Paul Paulus — The Individual-Group Alternation Cycle**

Sawyer is right that temporal alternation is the key. My experimental data quantify the optimal cycle (Paulus & Brown, 2007, ~300 citations; Paulus & Kenworthy, 2019, ~150 citations):

**Individual phase**: 8–15 minutes. This is the time needed for CREA1 Phase 1 (generative) to produce a set of novel ideas without social interference. Shorter than 8 minutes and the generative phase does not complete; longer than 15 minutes and diminishing returns set in (the best ideas come early).

**Group sharing phase**: 5–10 minutes. Participants share ideas sequentially or simultaneously (brainwriting). The key: share WITHOUT EVALUATION during this phase. Evaluation kills the building-on-others' ideas process. The environment should support rapid sharing — visible display surfaces, distributed writing tools, no podium or single-speaker arrangement.

**Incubation/transition phase**: 3–5 minutes. Brief physical movement (consistent with CREA3 walking benefit), informal conversation, refreshment. This is the social-incubation period where cross-pollination happens informally — the "water cooler" effect that has been romanticized but is architecturally real.

The FULL CYCLE is approximately 20–30 minutes: 10 min individual → 7 min group → 5 min incubation → repeat. A 3-hour creative workshop produces 6–9 cycles. The architecture must support all three phases with MINIMAL TRANSITION FRICTION — the time to move between individual, group, and incubation spaces should be <30 seconds, or the cycle breaks down.

**Position Statement 5: Teresa Amabile — Psychological Safety and Freedom**

The environmental determinants of organizational creativity are not limited to sensory channels. My longitudinal research (Amabile et al., 1996, ~5,000 citations) demonstrates that the STRONGEST predictor of daily creative performance is the experience of FREEDOM — the perception that one has autonomy over how to approach the work, time without immediate evaluative pressure, and the psychological safety to take risks.

Physical environments communicate freedom or constraint through spatial features. CONSTRAINED environments: surveillance architecture (open plans with manager sight lines), glass-walled offices that expose occupants, rigid furniture arrangements that cannot be modified, clock-visible time pressure cues. FREE environments: occupant-controllable spatial arrangements, visual and acoustic privacy available on demand, no single authority viewpoint, modifiable furniture and display surfaces.

For CREA4, the implication is that the environment must signal PSYCHOLOGICAL SAFETY at the group level. This means: (a) no spatial hierarchy in the collaborative zone (no head-of-table position, no elevated chair, no asymmetric sight lines); (b) acoustic privacy from external audiences (the group should feel that outsiders cannot overhear); (c) impermanence cues — erasable surfaces, movable elements, draft-quality materials signal that ideas are provisional and exploratory rather than final and evaluated.

**Position Statement 6: Genie Cho — Observed Spatial Patterns in Creative Studios**

My observational studies of 12 innovation labs and design studios (Cho & Choi, 2021, ~60 citations; Cho et al., 2023, ~30 citations) provide the ecological data. The studios with highest rated creative output shared spatial features:

**Adjacency pattern**: Individual workstations clustered in groups of 4–6 (Dunbar sympathy group), arranged around a shared "commons" with display surfaces. Commons within 10m walking distance of all individual stations in the cluster. Multiple clusters share a larger commons (café, maker space) for cross-cluster pollination.

**Threshold design**: Transitions between individual and group zones marked by SUBTLE thresholds (material change, slight level change, lighting shift) rather than walls or doors. The threshold should be permeable — easy to cross when entering group mode, but sufficient to signal "I am in focus mode" when at the individual station.

**Display density**: The highest-performing studios had 2–3× more writable surface area per capita than standard offices. Every vertical surface within the collaborative zone was writable or pin-up-able. This externalizes working memory at the group level — ideas persist visibly, enabling asynchronous building-on that does not require all members present simultaneously.

**Acoustic gradient**: Not silence vs. noise, but a GRADIENT from quiet (individual stations, ~35–40 dBA) to moderate (collaborative commons, ~50–55 dBA) to energized (maker space / café, ~60–65 dBA). Creative workers self-selected their acoustic environment based on current task phase — generative workers moved toward moderate noise (consistent with Mehta's Pathway A); evaluative workers moved toward quiet.

## Cross-Examination and Debates

### Debate 1: The 2×2×2 Full Factorial — How Many Cells Can We Estimate?

**Challenge**: The full 2×2×2 (noise: 45 vs. 70 dB) × (ceiling: standard 2.7m vs. high 4.0m) × (light: 500 lux vs. 150 lux) has 8 cells. Mehta has data for 4 cells (noise × light). What about the remaining cells involving ceiling height?

**Mehta**: The noise × ceiling cell lacks direct data. The closest evidence: Meyers-Levy & Zhu (2007) tested ceiling height alone (d ≈ 0.35 for abstract processing), and our noise effect is d ≈ 0.42. If ceiling height and noise are as redundant as noise and dim lighting (shared abstract-construal pathway), the noise × ceiling combination should yield d ≈ 0.55–0.60 (sub-additivity ~75%).

**Jung**: I predict the noise × ceiling interaction is LESS redundant than noise × light. Noise works through the auditory-SN pathway; ceiling height works through the visual-spatial-affect pathway (VF3 → CREA2B, as established in Doc 64). These are more neurally distinct than noise and dim lighting (which both converge on SN gating). I estimate the noise × ceiling sub-additivity at ~85% — nearly additive, because the pathways are more independent.

**Panel consensus**: Estimated 2×2×2 interaction matrix (all d values for divergent thinking vs. quiet + standard ceiling + bright baseline):

| Condition | Noise (A) | Ceiling (B) | Light (C) | Predicted d (divergent) | Sub-additivity | Convergent penalty |
|-----------|-----------|-------------|-----------|------------------------|----------------|-------------------|
| 000 (baseline) | Quiet | Standard | Bright | 0.00 | — | 0.00 |
| 100 (A only) | 70 dB | Standard | Bright | +0.42 | — | −0.18 |
| 010 (B only) | Quiet | High | Bright | +0.35 | — | −0.10 |
| 001 (C only) | Quiet | Standard | Dim | +0.30 | — | −0.08 |
| 110 (A+B) | 70 dB | High | Bright | +0.65 | 84% | −0.25 |
| 101 (A+C) | 70 dB | Standard | Dim | +0.55 | 76% | −0.32 |
| 011 (B+C) | Quiet | High | Dim | +0.52 | 80% | −0.15 |
| 111 (A+B+C) | 70 dB | High | Dim | +0.75 | 70% | −0.40 |

**Key findings from the matrix:**

- The noise × ceiling combination (A+B, d = 0.65) is the most efficient two-pathway combination: highest divergent boost with moderate convergent penalty, because the pathways are neurally more independent.
- The full three-pathway combination (A+B+C, d = 0.75) is the ceiling of the system: adding dim lighting to noise + high ceiling gains only +0.10 divergent but costs an additional −0.15 convergent. Diminishing returns.
- The convergent penalty is ALWAYS super-additive when combining A (noise) with C (dim light): these share the SN-gating mechanism and their convergent costs compound.
- **Design recommendation**: For generative zones, use A+B (noise + ceiling) rather than A+B+C (all three), unless the space is EXCLUSIVELY for divergent work. The B+C combination (ceiling + dim, no noise) is the gentlest option: modest divergent boost with minimal convergent cost.

**Maturity**: The single-pathway effects (A, B, C) are supported. The noise × light interaction (A+C) is supported by Mehta 2024 data. The remaining interaction estimates (A+B, B+C, A+B+C) are expert estimates consistent with the neural architecture but NOT empirically validated in the specific 2×2×2 design. The overall sub-additivity pattern is supported with dissent (Jung estimates more additivity for A+B than the consensus).

### Debate 2: Does Production Blocking Scale with Group Size?

**Challenge**: Paulus's alternation cycle assumes a specific group size. Does the cycle change for pairs vs. teams of 6 vs. teams of 12?

**Paulus**: Production blocking scales approximately LOGARITHMICALLY with group size: blocking loss ∝ ln(N). A pair (N=2) has minimal blocking — both can speak in rapid alternation. A group of 4 has moderate blocking. A group of 8+ has severe blocking that individual-group alternation can only partially solve. The optimal group size for the alternation cycle is 3–6 — large enough for diverse perspectives, small enough for everyone to share within the 5–10 minute group phase.

**Sawyer**: Consistent with Dunbar's sympathy group (SOC3). The architectural implication: collaborative zones should be sized for 4–6 people (Dunbar sympathy group), not 10–20 (which is how most conference rooms are sized). Multiple small collaborative zones are superior to one large one for creative work.

**Cho**: My observational data support this precisely. The highest-performing studios clustered 4–6 individual stations around each collaborative commons. Studios that used larger open collaborative spaces (10+ people) showed more production blocking, more social loafing, and paradoxically LESS creative interaction per capita.

**Panel consensus**: **Optimal collaborative creativity group size: 3–6 (Dunbar sympathy group).** Architecture should provide collaborative zones scaled for this size. Larger creative sessions (workshops, design charrettes) should be structured as multiple small groups rather than one large group, with periodic inter-group sharing.

### Debate 3: Psychological Safety — Is It Architectural or Organizational?

**Challenge**: Amabile's "freedom" predictor is about organizational culture, not building design. Can architecture meaningfully affect psychological safety?

**Amabile**: Architecture cannot CREATE psychological safety in a toxic organization. But it can AMPLIFY psychological safety in a supportive one, or UNDERMINE it in a healthy one. A team with good interpersonal trust will feel less safe in a glass-walled fishbowl room with manager sight lines than in an acoustically private, modifiable space. Architecture provides the PHYSICAL SUBSTRATE of safety signals: enclosure (VF3/T5 threat), acoustic privacy (SOC2), spatial hierarchy cues (SOC1), and modifiability (agency/control). These are the spatial analogs of the interpersonal conditions.

**Cho**: I can give specific examples. In one studio I observed, the creative team had a "war room" — a dedicated project space with opaque walls, lockable door, writable surfaces floor-to-ceiling, no external visibility. Creative output in this space measurably exceeded their output in the open-plan alternative. When the war room was repurposed (organizational change), creative metrics declined even though the TEAM was unchanged. The architecture was not sufficient — the team also had good leadership — but it was necessary.

**Panel consensus**: **Psychological safety is primarily organizational but architecturally modulated.** CREA4 should include an `organizational_safety_baseline` parameter (0–1) that scales the architectural contribution. Architecture amplifies existing safety (multiplicative, not additive). In a zero-safety organization, architectural changes have near-zero effect. In a moderate-to-high safety organization, spatial features can shift creative output by d ≈ 0.20–0.40.

## Synthesis

### The Collaborative Creativity Architecture Model

CREA-III establishes a complete architectural model for creative work that integrates individual and collaborative processes:

**Layer 1 (Individual)**: CREA1 → CREA2 → CREA3 describe the solo creative cycle and its environmental modulation. Calibrated with specific pathway effect sizes and the 2×2×2 interaction matrix.

**Layer 2 (Collaborative)**: CREA4 wraps the individual cycle in a social structure — the alternation cycle (individual → group → incubation) repeated 6–9 times per session. Architectural requirements: clustered individual stations, adjacent collaborative commons, and transition space with incubation quality.

**Layer 3 (Organizational)**: Amabile's psychological safety operates as a multiplicative moderator. Architecture amplifies organizational safety but cannot substitute for it. The spatial features (acoustic privacy, non-hierarchical arrangement, modifiable surfaces, impermanence cues) are the physical substrate of safety signals.

The three-zone model from CREA-I is now PARAMETERIZED:

| Zone | Primary Function | Acoustic | Light | Ceiling | Surfaces | CREA Pathway |
|------|-----------------|----------|-------|---------|----------|--------------|
| **Generative** | CREA1 Phase 1; CREA4 individual phase | 65–70 dBA ambient | 100–200 lux | R_h > 0.35 | Individual writable | A+B (noise + ceiling) |
| **Evaluative** | CREA1 Phase 3; CREA4 group evaluation | <45 dBA | 400–500 lux | R_h 0.25–0.35 | Shared display, writable | None (minimize broadening) |
| **Transitional** | CREA3 incubation; CREA4 Phase 3 | 50–55 dBA | Daylight preferred | Variable | Walking path, soft fascination | Single pathway, moderate |

### Where the Evidence Is Strong, Where It Is Preliminary

**Strong (supported)**: Production blocking as primary barrier to group creativity — replicated across 100+ studies. Temporal alternation as the solution — supported by Paulus's experimental program. Noise × light interaction — direct experimental data (Mehta 2024). The individual creative cycle (CREA1–3) — calibrated in CREA-II with specific parameters.

**Moderate (supported-preliminary)**: Group flow conditions (Sawyer) — well-theorized, moderate experimental support, limited architectural-specific data. The alternation cycle timing (8–15 min individual, 5–10 min group, 3–5 min incubation) — derived from laboratory brainwriting studies; ecological validity in real design studios partially confirmed by Cho's observations.

**Preliminary (expert estimate)**: The 2×2×2 interaction cells beyond noise × light. The noise × ceiling sub-additivity coefficient (0.84) and the three-pathway ceiling (d ≈ 0.75 divergent) are theoretically grounded but not directly measured. Psychological safety as multiplicative moderator — conceptually sound, parametrically uncertain. CREA4 spatial parameters (display density ≥1.5 m²/person, transition time <30s, cluster size 4–6) are derived from observational data with small samples.

## Testable Predictions and Critical Experiments

**Prediction 1: The 2×2×2 Factorial**

The highest-priority experiment for the entire CREA system. Full between-subjects 2×2×2: noise (45 vs. 70 dB) × ceiling (2.7m vs. 4.0m) × light (500 vs. 150 lux). N = 400 (50 per cell). DV: Alternate Uses Task (divergent), Remote Associates Test (convergent). CREA-III predicts: main effects A, B, C significant for divergent; A × C interaction significant and negative; A × B interaction small and non-significant; three-way A × B × C interaction non-significant. Convergent: all main effects negative; A × C super-additive penalty.

**Prediction 2: Alternation Cycle vs. Continuous Group**

Twelve creative teams (N = 4–6 each) assigned to three conditions: (A) alternation cycle with architectural support (individual stations + commons + transition space), (B) alternation cycle without architectural support (all work in same room, verbally instructed to alternate), (C) continuous group brainstorming. DV: quantity and quality of ideas over 3-hour session. CREA4 predicts: A > B > C on both quantity and quality. The A−B difference isolates the architectural contribution to the alternation cycle.

**Prediction 3: Display Density and Production Blocking**

Within-subjects: same teams, same task, three display conditions: (A) no writable surfaces (verbal sharing only), (B) moderate writable (0.5 m²/person), (C) high writable (2.0 m²/person). CREA4 predicts: production blocking (measured by waiting time and idea loss) decreases monotonically with display density. Quality of building-on-others' ideas increases with display density.

**Prediction 4: Psychological Safety × Architecture Interaction**

Quasi-experiment: teams from organizations with varying safety climates (measured by Edmondson's Psychological Safety Scale) work in high-safety architecture (private, non-hierarchical, modifiable) vs. low-safety architecture (exposed, hierarchical, fixed). CREA4 predicts: safety × architecture interaction — high organizational safety + high-safety architecture produces d ≈ 0.35 creative output advantage; low organizational safety shows no architectural effect.

## CREA-IV Prospectus

### DEEPENING

**CREA-IV Priority 1: The 2×2×2 factorial experiment.** The most important single experiment for the CREA system. Direct measurement of all 8 cells would validate or revise the interaction matrix and provide the first comprehensive architectural-creativity dose-response data.

**CREA-IV Priority 2: Alternation cycle ecological validation.** Longitudinal observation of 6–10 creative teams using CREA4-optimized spaces vs. standard spaces over 3–6 months. Track creative output (patent applications, design awards, project completion quality) rather than laboratory tasks.

### BROADENING

**CREA-IV Priority 3: Remote and hybrid collaborative creativity.** CREA4 assumes physical co-presence. As hybrid work becomes standard, the template needs extension to distributed teams: which spatial features must be replicated virtually (shared display, acoustic presence), and which are irreducibly physical (walking incubation, material haptics, spatial proportion effects)?

**CREA-IV Priority 4: Domain-specific creativity.** CREA1–4 are calibrated primarily against divergent thinking tasks (Alternate Uses, Remote Associates). Creative work in architecture, engineering, science, and art involves different cognitive profiles. Extension to domain-specific creativity would require adjusting the phase durations, pathway sensitivities, and collaborative cycle timing.

**CREA-IV Priority 5: Neurodiversity in creative environments.** ADHD, autism, and other neurodivergent profiles have different relationships to noise sensitivity, spatial preference, and social dynamics. Connect to DEV-II's planned ADHD parameters.

---

## Scope Exclusions

**Artistic creativity and studio practice**: CREA4 addresses collaborative KNOWLEDGE WORK creativity (ideation, problem-solving, design). The studio practice of visual artists, musicians, and writers involves different processes (extended solitary production, apprenticeship, material dialogue) that the alternation cycle does not capture.

**Organizational structure and management**: While Amabile's framework connects to organizational factors, CREA templates do not model management practices, incentive structures, or team composition. These are organizational-psychology variables, not architectural ones.

**Digital collaboration tools**: Software for collaborative creativity (Miro, Figma, Slack) has its own design principles. CREA4 addresses the PHYSICAL environment; the digital environment is an interaction that would require a dedicated panel.
