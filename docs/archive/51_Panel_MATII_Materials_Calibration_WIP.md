# PANEL MAT-II: MATERIALS CALIBRATION — PARAMETERIZING THERMAL, HAPTIC, AND MULTI-MODAL MATERIAL TEMPLATES
## Channel Separation for MAT4, Thermal Dose-Response for MAT1, and Cross-Modal Congruence Testing for MAT3
## February 16, 2026 — Document 51

---

## Panel Charge

Panel MAT-I (Doc 37) produced five mechanistic templates: MAT1 (thermal adaptive PE), MAT2 (contact thermal PE), MAT3 (multi-modal material identity integration), MAT4 (natural material convergence — wood as canonical case), and MAT5 (material-cultural conditioning). Together with OLF1 (olfactory transition PE, Doc 46), these templates provide Article Eater with a mechanistic vocabulary for evaluating findings about materials and surfaces. The system can now identify which mechanism a material-related finding invokes — whether a study about wood is primarily engaging visual fractal fluency (T1 via MAT4 Channel 1), haptic warmth (MAT2 via Channel 2), acoustic absorption (AX1 via Channel 3), olfactory biophilic association (OLF1 via Channel 4), or cultural conditioning (MAT5 via Channel 5).

But as with L-I, the MAT-I templates are structurally complete and parametrically empty. When Article Eater encounters a finding reporting that "wood surfaces reduced stress by 15% relative to laminate," it can identify the plausible mechanisms but cannot determine their relative contributions. It cannot tell whether the study's laminate control adequately isolates the visual channel from the haptic. It cannot specify whether the thermal contact PE threshold was exceeded in the experimental conditions. It cannot quantify the olfactory contribution. These are calibration questions.

MAT-II's charge parallels L-II's: tune the framework that MAT-I built. Specifically:

1. **Separate MAT4 channels** — Which of wood's five channels (visual grain, haptic warmth, acoustic absorption, olfactory VOCs, biophilic association) contributes the most to the stress-reduction and preference effects? This directly parallels L-II's L3 channel-weight estimation.

2. **Calibrate MAT1 thermal dose-response** — What surface temperatures, air temperatures, and radiant asymmetries define the Goldilocks boundaries for thermal PE? Fanger's PMV model and de Dear's adaptive model provide the engineering framework; MAT-II must translate these into PE terms with explicit boundaries for comfort, pleasure, stimulation, and discomfort.

3. **Test MAT3 cross-modal congruence** — MAT3 predicts that material congruence across modalities (seeing wood AND feeling warmth AND hearing absorption) produces fluency, while incongruence (seeing wood but feeling cold plastic) produces PE. This prediction must be experimentally tested.

4. **Calibrate MAT2 contact thermal PE** — The C-tactile afferent pathway for affective touch is well-characterized neuroscientifically (McGlone et al., 2014). But the architectural implication — that TOUCHING surfaces matters for occupant wellbeing — needs dose-response specification: how much surface contact is needed? How long? Which body parts?

5. **Assess olfactory contribution** — OLF1 establishes that smell operates at transitions and encodes arrivals. MAT-II should assess how material odor contributes to material identity (MAT3) and whether eliminating the olfactory channel from wood (sealed/coated wood) measurably reduces the MAT4 convergence benefit.

6. **Extend to stone, concrete, and metal** — MAT4 formalizes wood as the canonical natural material convergence case. MAT-II should assess whether stone, concrete, and metal have their own convergence profiles or whether they are better described as partial-channel materials.

## Panel Composition

### Returning from MAT-I

**1. Charles Spence** (Oxford University, Crossmodal Research Laboratory; ~40,000 total citations) — *Chair*
The world's leading researcher on cross-modal perception. His laboratory has systematically studied how visual, haptic, auditory, olfactory, and gustatory information are integrated in material perception, product evaluation, and environmental assessment (Spence, 2020, ~200 citations). His work provides the experimental paradigms for testing MAT3's cross-modal congruence predictions.
*Role*: Chair. Designs the cross-modal congruence experiments, evaluates whether MAT3's integration model is consistent with the multisensory integration literature, and assesses channel independence.

**2. Francis McGlone** (Liverpool John Moores University, Somatosensory & Affective Neuroscience Group; ~8,000 total citations)
Codiscoverer of the functional significance of C-tactile (CT) afferents — unmyelinated nerve fibers that respond specifically to gentle, stroking touch at skin temperature and project to the insular cortex rather than the somatosensory cortex (McGlone et al., 2014, ~500 citations; Löken et al., 2009, ~800 citations). This affective touch pathway is the neural substrate for MAT2's contact thermal PE and the haptic channel of MAT4.
*Role*: Specifies the neuroscience of material-contact experience. Provides dose-response parameters for the CT-afferent pathway: optimal touch velocity (~3 cm/s), temperature preference (skin temperature ~32°C), and the distinction between discriminative touch (texture identification) and affective touch (pleasantness of contact).

**3. Richard de Dear** (University of Sydney, Indoor Environmental Quality Laboratory; ~15,000 total citations)
Developer of the adaptive thermal comfort model that challenged Fanger's heat-balance approach (de Dear & Brager, 1998, ~3,000 citations). His model demonstrates that thermal comfort is not a fixed set-point but an adaptive prediction: occupants in naturally ventilated buildings accept a wider temperature range than those in air-conditioned buildings because their expectations ADAPT to the ambient conditions. This is MAT1's central claim expressed in engineering terms.
*Role*: Provides the thermal dose-response calibration for MAT1. Translates between engineering thermal metrics (PMV, PPD, adaptive comfort standards) and PE-framework predictions. Specifies the Goldilocks zone boundaries for thermal PE.

**4. Juhani Pallasmaa** (architect and architectural theorist; ~15,000 total citations across works)
Author of *The Eyes of the Skin: Architecture and the Senses* (2005, ~4,000 citations), the most influential phenomenological critique of architectural ocularcentrism. Pallasmaa's argument — that modern architecture has impoverished the senses by privileging vision over touch, smell, sound, and thermal sensation — provides the theoretical motivation for the entire MAT template series.
*Role*: Provides the phenomenological vocabulary and experiential taxonomy for material experience. His architectural examples serve as calibration anchors — the buildings he identifies as haptically rich or haptically impoverished define the endpoints of the material-experience spectrum that the templates must explain.

**5. Armin Lindauer** (Stuttgart State Academy of Art and Design; material design researcher; ~100 total citations)
Researcher on the sensory properties of architectural materials and their measurable physical correlates. Has developed systematic methods for characterizing the visual, haptic, and thermal properties of building materials and correlating them with user preference ratings (Lindauer et al., 2019, ~30 citations).
*Role*: Provides the material-science measurement framework — how to quantify the physical properties (surface roughness, thermal conductivity, spectral reflectance) that determine each MAT channel's input. Bridges between material specification (what an architect controls) and sensory response (what the templates predict).

### New Panelists

**6. Yoshifumi Miyazaki** (Chiba University, Center for Environment, Health and Field Sciences; ~5,000 total citations)
Pioneer of "shinrin-yoku" (forest bathing) research and the physiological measurement of human responses to natural materials. His laboratory has conducted the most systematic studies of human physiological responses to wood surfaces specifically — measuring blood pressure, heart rate variability, and cortisol in response to visual, olfactory, and tactile exposure to wood vs. synthetic materials (Miyazaki et al., 2017, ~100 citations; Sakuragawa et al., 2005, ~150 citations; Ikei et al., 2017, ~200 citations).
*Role*: The critical addition for MAT4 calibration. Miyazaki's laboratory has DIRECTLY MEASURED channel-specific responses to wood — visual (seeing wood panels), olfactory (smelling hinoki cypress), and tactile (touching wood surfaces) — in separate and combined conditions. This is the closest existing data to the MAT4 channel-separation experiment. His Japanese research context also provides non-Western cultural calibration data.

**7. Luisa Brotas** (University College London, Bartlett School of Environment, Energy and Resources; ~300 total citations)
Researcher on daylighting, thermal comfort, and the interaction between light and materials in architectural interiors. Has studied how material surface properties (reflectance, color, texture) interact with lighting conditions to determine the overall sensory quality of interior spaces (Brotas & Wilson, 2002, ~40 citations).
*Role*: Provides the light-material interaction perspective that L-II's scope exclusions flagged as belonging in MAT-II. How a material LOOKS depends on how it is lit; the visual channel of MAT4 is not purely a material property but a light-material joint property. Brotas can specify the design conditions under which material visual properties are maximally expressed or suppressed.

**8. Peter Zumthor** (Swiss architect; Pritzker Prize 2009; ~5,000 total citations across works)
The living architect most associated with material sensibility in design. His buildings (Therme Vals, Kolumba Museum, Bruder Klaus Field Chapel) are renowned for their intense material presence — the warmth of wood, the coldness of stone, the smell of concrete, the sound of water on surfaces. His book *Atmospheres* (2006, ~2,000 citations) provides the most articulate practitioner's account of how material choices create emotional experience.
*Role*: Provides the design-practice calibration. Zumthor's buildings are natural experiments in multi-modal material convergence. Therme Vals (stone, water, thermal) engages different MAT channels than Kolumba Museum (brick, light, acoustic). His testimony identifies which material experiences designers can intentionally create and which emerge as incidental consequences of material selection.

**9. Katja Schechtner** (MIT Senseable City Lab / OECD; ~200 total citations)
Researcher on sensory urbanism and the measurement of multi-sensory environmental quality. Has developed methods for quantifying the "sensory profile" of urban and architectural environments across multiple modalities simultaneously, including wearable sensor platforms that capture visual, acoustic, thermal, and olfactory data during real-world building visits (Schechtner et al., 2020, ~30 citations).
*Role*: Provides the multi-modal measurement methodology. If MAT-II's calibration is to be practically useful, we need tools that can MEASURE the multi-modal material environment of a real building. Schechtner's sensor platform could provide the instrumentation for a material-environment audit.

**10. Asifa Majid** (University of York, Department of Psychology; ~8,000 total citations)
Leading researcher on cross-cultural variation in sensory language and perception. Has demonstrated that cultures differ fundamentally in which sensory modalities they attend to, categorize, and communicate about — some cultures have rich vocabularies for smell, others for texture, others privilege vision (Majid & Burenhult, 2014, ~300 citations; Majid et al., 2018, ~200 citations).
*Role*: Provides the cultural calibration that MAT4 and MAT5 require. If wood's "warmth" is partly a Western cultural association (MAT5) rather than a universal perceptual response (MAT2), the channel weights should differ across cultures. Majid can assess which MAT channels are culturally universal and which are culturally calibrated. Connects to David's fieldwork in India on cultural variation in sensory set-points.
