# PANEL COL-I: ARCHITECTURAL COLOR AND CHROMATIC PREDICTION
## Mechanistic Templates for How Color in Built Environments Affects Cognition, Emotion, and Behavior
## February 16, 2026 — Document 45

---

## Panel Charge

This panel addresses the absence of any dedicated color template in the inventory. Color is one of the most salient and immediately perceived properties of architectural surfaces, yet it has been systematically deferred. VF-I addressed contour geometry, visual rhythm, and spatial proportions but explicitly excluded color. MAT-I addressed material identity and cultural associations but treated color only as one component of visual material appearance. T1 addresses spectral/fractal properties but only in terms of spatial frequency, not hue or saturation. The result: no template formalizes how architectural color operates as a PE mechanism.

The scientific literature on color and affect is substantial but contested. There is genuine disagreement about whether color effects on emotion and cognition are (a) hardwired via evolutionary associations, (b) learned through cultural conditioning, (c) mediated by arousal/activation rather than specific hue-emotion links, or (d) largely contextual and interaction-dependent. The panel must navigate these disagreements honestly rather than presenting a false consensus.

The panel is charged with producing 2–3 templates covering: (a) chromatic PE — how the brain generates color predictions for architectural contexts and how violations produce PE, (b) color-arousal modulation — how warm/cool and saturated/desaturated color palettes modulate physiological arousal in architectural settings, and (c) color-meaning and cultural conditioning — how learned color associations shape emotional responses to architectural surfaces. These must connect to T1 (spectral processing), T2 (complexity Goldilocks), T9 (rapid implicit evaluation), MAT3 (multi-modal material identity), MAT5 (material-cultural conditioning), and L1 (luminance contrast).

## Panel Composition

### Color Science and Perception

**1. Stephen Palmer** (University of California, Berkeley, Department of Psychology)
Developer of the Ecological Valence Theory (EVT) of color preference — the most empirically grounded theory of why humans prefer certain colors over others (Palmer & Schloss, 2010, ~700 citations). EVT proposes that color preferences are shaped by the valence (positive or negative affect) of objects strongly associated with each color: blue is preferred because sky and clean water are positive; brown is disliked because feces and rotting food are negative. The theory predicts color preferences from weighted affective valence of color-associated objects with remarkable accuracy (r ≈ 0.9 across cultures). Palmer was on the VF-I panel but his color work was deferred.
*Role*: Establishes the color preference mechanism. EVT is the strongest empirical account of color affect. For architecture, it predicts that color preferences are NOT arbitrary and NOT purely cultural — they reflect statistical regularities in the affective associations of colored objects in the environment. A blue room is preferred partly because the brain's color-object associations (sky, water) carry positive valence. A brown room is disliked partly because brown-object associations carry negative valence. These associations are modifiable by experience (a person who loves chocolate may like brown more) but the population-level pattern is robust.

**2. Andrew Elliot** (University of Rochester, Department of Psychology)
Developer of Color-in-Context theory — the framework that color effects on cognition and behavior are context-dependent rather than universal (Elliot & Maier, 2012, ~1,500 citations; Elliot et al., 2007, ~1,800 citations). His most cited finding: red impairs performance on achievement tasks (tests, exams) because red is associated with failure and danger in achievement contexts, but red enhances attractiveness judgments because it is associated with sexual receptivity in mating contexts. Same color, different effects — the context determines the meaning, and the meaning determines the response.
*Role*: Provides the critical theoretical corrective. Many architectural color claims assume universal effects ("blue is calming," "red is energizing"). Elliot's work demonstrates that color effects are always mediated by context and meaning. A red wall in a restaurant may enhance appetite and social energy; a red wall in a testing room may impair cognitive performance. Architecture cannot apply color rules context-free. The PE framework must incorporate context as a moderating variable: the brain's color prediction includes the CONTEXTUAL MEANING of the color, not just its wavelength.

**3. Patricia Valdez and Albert Mehrabian** (UCLA, Department of Psychology)
Authors of the foundational study on color and emotional response using Mehrabian's PAD (Pleasure-Arousal-Dominance) framework (Valdez & Mehrabian, 1994, ~1,500 citations). Their systematic mapping of hue, saturation, and brightness onto pleasure and arousal established the dimensional structure of color-emotion: brightness and saturation predict arousal (brighter, more saturated → higher arousal); hue contributes modestly to pleasure (blue-green most pleasant, yellow-green least); the interaction of dimensions matters more than any single dimension.
*Role*: Provides the dimensional mapping. Rather than assigning specific emotions to specific colors (an approach that fails empirically), Valdez and Mehrabian show that color affects AROUSAL and PLEASURE along continuous dimensions. This maps naturally onto the PE framework: high-saturation, high-brightness colors produce higher physiological activation (increased arousal) which may be positive (energizing) or negative (overstimulating) depending on context and duration. Low-saturation, moderate-brightness colors produce moderate activation compatible with sustained occupancy.

**4. Rikard Küller** (Lund University, School of Architecture; posthumous)
Pioneer of color research in real architectural settings — not laboratory swatches but actual rooms with colored walls, ceilings, and floors (Küller et al., 2006, ~300 citations; Küller & Mikellides, 1993, ~200 citations). His work demonstrated that the effects of color in real rooms are more modest and more complex than laboratory studies suggest. Color interacts with room size, lighting conditions, surface texture, and occupancy duration. His key finding: color effects on mood are significant but small compared to lighting effects, and they habituate substantially over time — the initial impact of a colored room diminishes within days to weeks of sustained occupancy.
*Role*: Provides the architectural reality check. Laboratory studies of color typically present isolated color swatches against neutral backgrounds for seconds to minutes. Architectural color is experienced as immersive, multi-surface, multi-textured, dynamically lit, and sustained over hours, days, and years. Küller's work demonstrates that translation from lab to building is not straightforward: (a) immersive color is more arousing than isolated color, (b) lighting modulates color effects substantially (the same wall color appears different under warm vs. cool light, daylight vs. artificial), (c) habituation reduces the affective impact of any constant color over time. The PE framework must account for habituation: a novel color produces PE; a habituated color produces none.

### Color in Architectural and Design Practice

**5. Faber Birren** (posthumous; formerly color consultant and author)
The most prolific writer on color in built environments (Birren, 1978, ~500 citations; Birren, 1988, ~300 citations). His practical framework for architectural color — warm colors advance and stimulate, cool colors recede and calm, high saturation demands attention, low saturation recedes — has influenced architectural practice for decades. While his theoretical claims are sometimes over-simplified, his observational database of color effects in hospitals, offices, schools, and factories provides an empirical foundation that more rigorous researchers have drawn upon.
*Role*: Provides the practitioner's accumulated wisdom. Birren's warm/cool and advance/recede principles are rough but real: warm colors (red, orange, yellow) do produce modestly higher arousal than cool colors (blue, green) in most studies, and high-saturation colors do attract more attention than low-saturation colors. These are not wrong — they are INCOMPLETE. The PE framework refines them: the warm/cool dimension modulates arousal (a prediction about activation level), and the saturation dimension modulates attention capture (a prediction about PE magnitude). But context, duration, lighting, and cultural associations modulate both.

**6. Jean-Philippe Lenclos** (Atelier 3D Couleur, Paris)
Architect and color researcher who developed the "Geography of Colour" — the systematic study of how regional color palettes emerge from local materials, climate, vegetation, and cultural traditions (Lenclos & Lenclos, 2004, ~100 citations). His documentation of Mediterranean ochres, Scandinavian greys and whites, Japanese earth tones, and Mexican vivid polychromy demonstrates that color expectations are geographically and culturally calibrated. A red building in Morocco fits; the same red in Stockholm is startling.
*Role*: Provides the cultural-ecological calibration. The brain generates color predictions partly from ecological regularities (Palmer's EVT) and partly from PLACE-SPECIFIC expectations shaped by regional color palettes. A building painted in colors consistent with its regional palette produces chromatic prediction confirmation — the colors fit. A building painted in colors from a different regional palette produces chromatic PE — the colors are surprising, which may be exciting (a deliberately exotic restaurant) or disturbing (an incongruent intrusion into a historic streetscape). The PE framework must include regional color expectation as a prediction source.

### Neuroscience of Color Processing

**7. Bevil Conway** (National Eye Institute, NIH; formerly MIT)
Neuroscientist who has mapped the cortical organization of color processing, demonstrating that color is processed in dedicated cortical modules (V4 and adjacent areas) that are organized by hue category with warm-cool as a primary organizational axis (Conway et al., 2010, ~300 citations; Conway & Tsao, 2009, ~400 citations). His work shows that the brain's color representation is NOT a uniform color space — it has privileged axes (warm-cool, light-dark) and categorical structure (the brain groups continuous wavelength variation into discrete color categories). This categorical structure means that within-category color changes produce less PE than between-category changes.
*Role*: Provides the neural architecture. Conway's finding that color is categorically organized has direct architectural implications: a wall painted in a slightly different shade of blue (within-category) produces minimal chromatic PE. The same wall painted green (between-category) produces substantial chromatic PE. This explains why monochromatic and analogous color schemes feel harmonious (within-category, low PE) while complementary and contrasting schemes feel dynamic (between-category, high PE). The brain's categorical color structure IS the prediction structure for architectural color.

**8. Anna Franklin** (University of Sussex, Sussex Colour Group)
Developmental psychologist and color scientist whose work on color categorization and preference across development and across cultures has demonstrated that some aspects of color perception are universal (categorical perception at blue-green boundary, infant preference for saturated colors) while others are culturally calibrated (category boundaries, naming effects, preference patterns) (Franklin et al., 2005, ~400 citations; Franklin & Davies, 2004, ~300 citations). Her cross-cultural work provides the evidence base for distinguishing universal from culturally-specific color mechanisms.
*Role*: Provides the universal-vs-cultural adjudication. For the PE framework, Franklin's work establishes that: (a) categorical color perception is partly universal (all languages have basic color terms at similar boundaries) and partly language-dependent (naming affects discrimination speed), (b) color preferences show both universal patterns (blue preference is near-universal) and cultural variation (white is mourning in some cultures, celebration in others), (c) infants show color preferences before cultural conditioning, suggesting a biological substrate. The template must accommodate both universal and culturally-calibrated prediction sources.

## Position Statements

### COLOR PREFERENCE AND ECOLOGICAL VALENCE

**Position Statement 1: Stephen Palmer — Why We Like the Colors We Like**

Color preferences are not random, not purely cultural, and not simply about wavelength. They are about the OBJECTS associated with colors (Palmer & Schloss, 2010, ~700 citations). Blue is the most universally preferred color because the objects most strongly associated with blue — clear sky, clean water — are overwhelmingly positive in valence. Dark yellow-green is among the least preferred because its associated objects — bile, mucus, rotting vegetation — are overwhelmingly negative. Red is intermediate and variable because its associated objects span strongly positive (ripe fruit, love) and strongly negative (blood, danger, fire).

The Ecological Valence Theory predicts color preferences with remarkable accuracy (r ≈ 0.88 across studies) by computing the weighted affective valence of all objects associated with each color. The weights reflect two factors: how strongly the object is associated with the color (blue → sky is very strong; blue → bruise is weak) and how extreme the object's affective valence is (sky = strongly positive; jeans = mildly positive). The prediction: colors associated with many strongly positive objects and few strongly negative objects will be preferred; colors with the opposite profile will be disliked.

For architecture: EVT predicts that color preferences in buildings are not arbitrary design choices — they are constrained by the brain's color-object association network. A blue ceiling evokes sky. A green wall evokes vegetation. A warm wood tone evokes natural materials. A grey concrete wall evokes overcast sky or industrial surfaces — which may be negative for most occupants but positive for those who associate exposed concrete with architectural sophistication (this is the MAT5 cultural-conditioning modifier). The architect works with and against a prediction landscape shaped by ecological color associations.

**Position Statement 2: Andrew Elliot — Color Means Nothing Without Context**

The single most important fact about color and psychology is that CONTEXT DETERMINES EFFECT (Elliot & Maier, 2012, ~1,500 citations). Red enhances attractiveness but impairs cognitive performance. Blue enhances creative thinking but reduces alertness. Green enhances reading speed in some contexts but has no effect in others. Every color-behavior finding is moderated by the evaluative context — the domain of activity, the social setting, the cultural frame, and the personal history of the individual.

This means that architectural color rules of the form "use blue for calm, red for energy" are misleading. A red accent wall in a restaurant may enhance social energy and appetite (positive context: celebration, food, warmth). The same red in an exam room may elevate anxiety and impair performance (negative context: failure, evaluation, threat). The brain does not respond to wavelength — it responds to MEANING, and meaning is constructed from color + context jointly.

The PE framework handles this naturally: the brain's color prediction includes contextual meaning. In a restaurant context, the prediction for warm colors is positive (food, social warmth, celebration), so warm colors confirm a positive prediction → pleasant experience. In an academic testing context, the prediction for red is negative (errors, failure, prohibition), so red confirms a negative prediction → elevated anxiety. The same physical stimulus produces different PE because the PREDICTION differs by context.

**Position Statement 3: Valdez and Mehrabian — Color as Arousal Modulation**

The most reliable effect of color on emotion is not specific hue → specific emotion but color dimensions → arousal level (Valdez & Mehrabian, 1994, ~1,500 citations). Brightness and saturation are the primary drivers: bright, highly saturated colors produce higher physiological arousal (elevated heart rate, increased skin conductance) regardless of hue. Dim, desaturated colors produce lower arousal. Hue contributes a secondary, modest effect: warm hues (red, orange, yellow) produce slightly higher arousal than cool hues (blue, green) at equivalent brightness and saturation.

For architecture: the practical implication is that the DIMENSIONS of color matter more than the SPECIFIC HUES. An architect choosing wall colors should think first about the appropriate arousal level for the activity — high arousal for a gym or social space (bright, saturated); moderate arousal for an office or classroom (moderate saturation, moderate brightness); low arousal for a bedroom or meditation space (desaturated, dim). Within the appropriate arousal band, hue choice can follow aesthetic preference, cultural convention, or ecological association (Palmer's EVT) without dramatically altering the arousal effect.

This maps onto the PE framework as arousal prediction. The brain generates a predicted arousal level for the context (I am entering a library; I predict low arousal → quiet, focused, calm). Color that confirms this prediction (desaturated, cool, moderate brightness) produces zero arousal PE → comfortable. Color that violates it (a bright red library wall) produces arousal PE → uncomfortable surprise. The PE is not about the color per se but about the mismatch between color-induced arousal and context-predicted arousal.

### COLOR IN ARCHITECTURAL CONTEXT

**Position Statement 4: Rikard Küller — Color Effects in Real Rooms Are Smaller Than You Think**

Decades of research on color in actual architectural settings demonstrates three things that laboratory studies often miss (Küller et al., 2006, ~300 citations; Küller & Mikellides, 1993, ~200 citations). First, LIGHTING DOMINATES COLOR. The apparent color of a wall is a product of surface reflectance and illuminant — the same paint looks different under warm incandescent, cool fluorescent, and daylight. Architectural color cannot be specified without specifying lighting. Second, IMMERSIVE COLOR IS DIFFERENT FROM SWATCH COLOR. A small color swatch viewed against a neutral background is evaluated differently from the same color surrounding the observer on walls, ceiling, and floor. Immersive color produces stronger arousal effects and stronger spatial effects (warm colors make rooms feel smaller; cool colors make rooms feel larger). Third, HABITUATION IS RAPID. The affective impact of a novel wall color diminishes substantially within days of sustained occupancy. A dramatic red accent wall produces excitement on day one and background normalcy by week two.

The implication for the PE framework is central: architectural color habituation IS the reduction of chromatic PE through prediction updating. On first encounter, the colored room violates the occupant's default expectation (most rooms are neutral), producing chromatic PE. Over repeated exposure, the prediction updates to include the color, PE drops to zero, and the color ceases to have affective impact. This is the same habituation mechanism that operates for spatial familiarity (SC1), acoustic habituation (T31), and thermal adaptation (MAT2). It means that DYNAMIC color — color that changes over time with light, season, or deliberate intervention — maintains PE where static color habituates.

**Position Statement 5: Faber Birren — Practical Color Principles and Their Limits**

Half a century of applied color research in workplaces, hospitals, schools, and factories has established a set of practical principles that work in most settings (Birren, 1978, ~500 citations). Warm colors (yellow, orange, warm red) increase perceived room temperature by ~2°C and increase social animation. Cool colors (blue, green, blue-grey) decrease perceived room temperature and reduce social animation. High-saturation colors demand attention and produce visual fatigue over sustained exposure. Low-saturation (grayed, muted) colors permit sustained visual engagement without fatigue. Light colors make rooms feel larger; dark colors make rooms feel smaller. These principles are reliable at the population level.

But they are not rules — they are tendencies. And they interact with every other variable in the room: lighting, material texture (a matte blue wall is very different from a glossy blue wall — the same hue produces different specular and diffuse reflections), spatial configuration (a small room with warm walls feels intimate or claustrophobic depending on ceiling height and window presence), and cultural context (white walls are clinical in North America and elegant in Scandinavia). The PE framework accommodates this interaction: the color prediction is generated from the FULL context (color + texture + lighting + space + culture), not from color alone.

**Position Statement 6: Jean-Philippe Lenclos — The Geography of Architectural Color**

Every region of the world has developed a characteristic color palette through centuries of building with local materials under local light conditions (Lenclos & Lenclos, 2004, ~100 citations). The ochres and terracottas of Mediterranean architecture emerge from local clay, limestone, and iron oxide pigments under intense sunlight. The whites and greys of Scandinavian architecture emerge from birch, granite, and the need to maximize light reflection under low sun angles. The earth tones of Japanese architecture emerge from unpainted wood, paper, and clay under filtered forest light. The vivid polychromy of Mexican architecture emerges from volcanic pigments and an aesthetic tradition that celebrates color intensity.

These regional palettes are PREDICTION SOURCES. A person who grows up in a Mediterranean town develops a chromatic prediction for architectural surfaces: ochre, terracotta, white, with blue accents. A building that matches this prediction (a new building using the regional palette) feels chromatic-ally "right" — the prediction is confirmed. A building that violates it (a bright purple house in a Tuscan village) produces chromatic PE that is experienced as jarring incongruity. This regional chromatic prediction operates alongside Palmer's ecological associations and Elliot's contextual meanings — three prediction sources that converge to determine the PE response to any architectural color.

### NEURAL COLOR PROCESSING

**Position Statement 7: Bevil Conway — Color Categories and the Brain's Chromatic Structure**

The brain does not process color as a continuous spectrum. It organizes color into CATEGORIES with privileged axes — warm-cool is the primary organizational dimension in V4, with light-dark as the secondary dimension (Conway et al., 2010, ~300 citations). These categorical boundaries mean that color changes that cross a category boundary (blue → green) produce more perceptual PE than changes of equal physical magnitude within a category (light blue → medium blue).

For architecture: this categorical structure explains the perceptual logic of color harmony. Monochromatic schemes (variations within a single category) produce minimal chromatic PE — the brain processes all surfaces as "same category, different value." Analogous schemes (adjacent categories: blue + blue-green) produce low PE — close categories, easy prediction. Complementary schemes (opposite categories: blue + orange) produce high PE — maximum categorical distance, maximum prediction violation. Triadic and tetradic schemes produce intermediate PE depending on the specific category combinations.

The Goldilocks principle (T2) applies: moderate chromatic PE (analogous or limited complementary schemes) produces aesthetic pleasure — enough chromatic variation to sustain interest without overwhelming the color processing system. Excessive chromatic PE (many saturated colors from distant categories) produces visual overload. Zero chromatic PE (a monochromatic or all-neutral environment) produces chromatic understimulation. Architectural color composition, like spatial composition (SC3), has an optimal PE range.

**Position Statement 8: Anna Franklin — What Is Universal and What Is Cultural in Color**

The evidence supports a two-source model of color response (Franklin et al., 2005, ~400 citations). Some color responses are universal: categorical perception at the blue-green boundary appears in pre-linguistic infants, suggesting a biological substrate. Blue preference is near-universal across cultures. High-saturation preference appears in infancy. Warm-cool arousal effects are consistent across cultures. These universal components likely reflect ecological regularities (Palmer's EVT provides the mechanism) and neural architecture (Conway's categorical organization provides the substrate).

Other color responses are culturally specific: white means mourning in some East Asian cultures and purity in Western cultures. Red means luck and celebration in China and danger in many Western contexts. Green means nature and sustainability in contemporary Western culture and Islam in Middle Eastern contexts. Black signals sophistication in fashion contexts and death in funeral contexts. These culturally specific responses are LEARNED PREDICTIONS — exactly the mechanism that MAT5 (material-cultural conditioning) formalizes for materials. The brain acquires color-meaning associations through cultural exposure, and these associations become the predictions against which architectural color is evaluated.

The PE framework must accommodate BOTH sources: (a) universal ecological associations (Palmer's EVT) produce a baseline color-affect prediction that is modifiable but not arbitrary, and (b) culturally-conditioned associations produce an overlay of learned color meanings that vary by culture, subculture, and individual experience. Both contribute to the prediction; both produce PE when violated.

## Cross-Examination and Debate

### Debate 1: Are Color Effects on Mood Real or Artifact?

**Challenge**: Many color-mood claims fail to replicate. The effect sizes are small. Lighting, context, and demand characteristics confound most studies. Is there a real architectural color effect?

**Palmer**: The PREFERENCE effects are robust. EVT predictions replicate across cultures with high accuracy. People reliably prefer some colors over others, and the preference pattern is predictable from ecological valence. This is not artifact.

**Elliot**: The BEHAVIORAL effects are real but context-dependent. Red impairs achievement performance — this replicates across multiple labs and paradigms. But the effect is specific to achievement contexts, not universal. The mistake is overgeneralizing: "red impairs performance" is false; "red impairs performance in evaluative achievement contexts" is supported.

**Küller**: In real architectural settings, the effects are SMALLER than laboratory studies suggest and they HABITUATE. A color intervention in a real workplace produces a measurable but modest mood effect in the first week, which diminishes over subsequent weeks. This doesn't mean the effect is zero — it means it's transient. Dynamic color (color that changes with lighting or season) can maintain the effect by preventing habituation.

**Valdez/Mehrabian**: The arousal effects are the most robust. Bright, saturated colors reliably increase physiological arousal. This is not cognitive — it's subcortical, mediated by retinal stimulation intensity. The arousal effect is real, replicable, and architecturally relevant because it's sustained (it habituates less than the hedonic effect because it's driven by luminance/saturation, not meaning).

**Panel consensus**: **Color effects on mood are real but smaller, more context-dependent, and more habituating than popular claims suggest.** The most robust effects are: (a) arousal modulation from brightness/saturation (Valdez & Mehrabian — physiological, relatively context-independent, partially resistant to habituation), (b) preference from ecological valence (Palmer — stable, cross-cultural, but not specific to architecture), (c) context-dependent behavioral effects (Elliot — real but requiring contextual specification). The PE framework should model color as a modest but real contributor to architectural experience, always in interaction with lighting, material, space, and context.

### Debate 2: Should Architecture Use Bold Color or Restrained Palettes?

**Challenge**: Modernist architecture tends toward neutral palettes (white, grey, black, natural materials). Post-modernist and vernacular traditions use bold color. Is there a PE-based argument for one over the other?

**Conway**: The brain's categorical color system suggests that SOME chromatic variation is optimal. An all-neutral environment provides zero chromatic PE — the color processing system is understimulated. But the optimal amount of chromatic variation is not "as much as possible" — it's a Goldilocks zone, just as T2 predicts for visual complexity generally. The brain has more computational resources dedicated to warm-cool discrimination than to fine within-category distinctions, which suggests that warm-cool contrast (a warm wood accent against a cool grey wall) may be more perceptually efficient than full-spectrum polychromy.

**Lenclos**: Regional palettes have evolved over centuries toward color balances that work under local light conditions. Mediterranean polychromy works under intense sun; the same colors look garish under Scandinavian grey sky. The "right" palette is always relative to the light environment. The PE framework: the prediction includes the expected light condition. Colors that match regional-light expectations confirm the prediction; colors imported from a different light environment violate it.

**Birren**: The practical answer is that most sustained-occupancy spaces benefit from restrained palettes (low saturation, moderate brightness) with selective high-chromatic accents for wayfinding, identity, and visual interest. This is the arousal Goldilocks: the base palette maintains moderate arousal appropriate for sustained work; the accents provide periodic chromatic PE that prevents monotony. It's the same logic as SC3's promenade: a base PE level with deliberate peaks.

**Panel consensus**: **The PE framework does not prescribe bold or restrained color — it prescribes APPROPRIATE PE for the context and activity.** Sustained occupancy spaces benefit from low base chromatic PE (restrained palette) with controlled chromatic peaks (accent colors). Transient spaces (lobbies, corridors, retail) can tolerate higher chromatic PE because habituation has less time to operate. The designer composes chromatic PE over space and time, just as SC3 composes spatial PE over the promenade.

## Synthesis: Color as Modest but Real Contributor to Architectural Experience

The two templates produced by this panel establish color as a genuine but frequently overstated dimension of architectural experience. The panel's most important contribution may be its CORRECTIVE function — replacing simplistic color-emotion rules with a more accurate, more nuanced, and more useful framework.

**COL1 (Chromatic PE)** establishes that color effects are ALWAYS context-dependent, operating through three converging prediction sources: ecological associations (Palmer's EVT), cultural-contextual meanings (Elliot's Color-in-Context), and regional architectural expectations (Lenclos). No color has a universal, context-free effect. Blue is not inherently calming — it is calming when it confirms a context prediction for low arousal (a bedroom, a meditation space) and neutral or even negative when it violates a context prediction for high energy (a gym, a celebration space). The brain's categorical color organization (Conway) structures the PE landscape: within-category color variation produces harmony (low PE); between-category variation produces contrast (high PE); the Goldilocks principle applies.

**COL2 (Color-Arousal)** establishes the most robust color mechanism: arousal modulation through saturation and brightness. This is the one color effect that operates with relative context-independence because it is partially subcortical — driven by retinal stimulation intensity rather than cognitive meaning. Highly saturated, bright colors increase arousal; desaturated, dim colors decrease it. Warm hue adds a modest secondary effect. The architect's primary chromatic design tool is therefore the arousal budget: specifying the activation level appropriate for the intended activity through saturation and brightness, then using hue for aesthetic and cultural objectives.

The critical insight across both templates is HABITUATION. Color effects diminish with sustained exposure. This means that color matters most in transient encounters (entering a building, passing through a corridor, arriving at a threshold) and matters least in sustained occupancy (an office occupied 8 hours daily). The connection to TP2 (episodic boundaries) and SC3 (promenade thresholds) is direct: color is most effective as a PE instrument at spatial transitions — the moments when the occupant is in a fresh perceptual state and habituation has not yet occurred. Color at thresholds enhances the episodic boundary; color in steady-state spaces habituates to background.

This panel also establishes an important methodological principle: architectural color CANNOT be specified independently of lighting. The same surface color appears different under warm, cool, and natural light. The perceived color — the product of surface reflectance and illuminant — determines the PE, not the paint-chip specification. This connects COL1/COL2 to L1 (luminance contrast), L2 (circadian light), and L5 (dynamic light): as daylight changes through the day, the same wall produces a changing chromatic experience. Dynamic color is inherent in any building with windows — the architect who understands this designs surface colors that produce appropriate PE across the daily light cycle.

## Testable Predictions

### Prediction 1: Ecological Valence Predicts Architectural Color Preference

**Hypothesis**: Occupant preference ratings for room colors in a real architectural setting will correlate with Palmer's EVT predictions (weighted affective valence of color-associated objects) more strongly than with simple warm/cool or saturation dimensions alone.

**Critical experiment**: Paint 12 otherwise identical rooms in 12 different colors spanning the hue circle at controlled saturation and brightness. Have occupants rate each room for pleasantness, comfort, and willingness to spend time. Correlate ratings with EVT predictions, arousal predictions (Valdez & Mehrabian), and simple warm/cool categorization. Predict: EVT accounts for unique variance beyond arousal dimensions, confirming that meaning-based prediction (ecological association) contributes above and beyond arousal modulation.

**Templates tested**: COL1 (EVT prediction source).

### Prediction 2: Color-Arousal Mismatch Produces Measurable PE

**Hypothesis**: Occupants performing a task in a room whose color-induced arousal level mismatches the task-appropriate arousal level will show both physiological stress markers and performance decrements compared to occupants in color-arousal-matched rooms.

**Critical experiment**: Two tasks (focused reading = low arousal optimal; physical exercise = high arousal optimal) × two room colors (desaturated blue = low arousal; saturated red-orange = high arousal). Measure heart rate, EDA, cortisol, task performance, and subjective comfort. Predict: significant interaction — reading performance better in blue room; exercise performance better in red room. The mismatch conditions produce elevated cortisol and reduced performance.

**Templates tested**: COL2 (arousal-context mismatch).

### Prediction 3: Color at Thresholds Resists Habituation More Than Immersive Color

**Hypothesis**: Color applied at spatial transitions (doorways, corridor entries, stair landings) will maintain its affective impact over longer periods than the same color applied to sustained-occupancy spaces (office walls, bedroom walls), because threshold color is encountered intermittently rather than continuously.

**Critical experiment**: In a workplace, apply color accents at thresholds (entry doors, transition corridors) and in sustained spaces (open-plan walls). Measure affective impact (brief mood survey) at weeks 1, 2, 4, and 8. Predict: threshold color maintains impact across all time points; sustained-space color shows significant habituation by week 2–4.

**Templates tested**: COL1 (habituation), TP2 (episodic boundaries), SC3 (threshold PE).

## Panel COL-II Prospectus: Priorities for Follow-Up

### Priority 1: Architectural Color Under Different Lighting Conditions

The color-lighting interaction is the most practically important unresolved issue. A systematic study of how 10 representative wall colors appear and affect occupants under warm (2700K), neutral (4000K), cool (5000K), and natural (variable) illumination would provide the cross-modal color-light calibration that both COL1/COL2 and L1/L2 need.

### Priority 2: Color Habituation Time Course in Real Buildings

Küller documents habituation but does not provide a precise time course. A longitudinal study measuring color affect at 1, 3, 7, 14, 28, and 56 days of occupancy would parameterize the habituation curve and determine whether dynamic color strategies (seasonal repainting, adaptive LED surfaces) can maintain chromatic PE.

### Priority 3: Cross-Cultural Color Preference in Architecture

Palmer's EVT has been tested cross-culturally for isolated swatches but not for immersive architectural settings. The same color may produce different EVT predictions in different cultural contexts (white is positive in Western architecture, mourning-associated in some East Asian contexts). Cross-cultural architectural color studies would calibrate the cultural-contextual prediction source.

### Priority 4: Color and Wayfinding

Color is a powerful wayfinding tool (color-coding floors, departments, zones) but no template formalizes how chromatic distinctiveness aids navigational prediction (connecting to SC1). A color-wayfinding template could formalize how color reduces navigational PE in complex buildings (hospitals, airports, universities).

## Scope Exclusions

**1. Color therapy and chromotherapy** — claims that specific colors heal specific ailments. Not supported by evidence and outside the scope of the PE framework.

**2. Color in digital displays and screens** — emitted light operates through different mechanisms than reflected light from architectural surfaces. A separate treatment would be needed.

**3. Architectural lighting color temperature** — addressed by L1 (luminance), L2 (circadian), and L5 (dynamic light). COL-I addresses surface color; lighting color belongs in the L-series.

**4. Color blindness and atypical color vision** — affects ~8% of males. Important for inclusive design but requires specialized treatment beyond the typical-vision assumptions of this panel.

**5. Color symbolism and semiotics** — how color carries symbolic meaning (religious, political, institutional). This operates through the knowledge-meaning vertex of Chatterjee's aesthetic triad and is noted as a gap in the system but belongs in a Meaning & Symbol panel rather than a perceptual color panel.
