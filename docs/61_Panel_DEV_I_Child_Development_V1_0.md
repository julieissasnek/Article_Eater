## Developmental Moderation Parameters for Mechanistic Templates
## February 17, 2026 — Document 61

## Panel Charge

Panel AGE-I (Doc 60) formalized how aging transforms the architecture-wellbeing relationship from middle adulthood onward. DEV-I addresses the other end of the lifespan: how developing sensory, cognitive, motor, and social systems in children and adolescents interact differently with built environments than mature adult systems. The charge:

1. **Formalize developmental moderation for calibrated template series** — how children at different stages process architectural PE differently from the adult baseline.
2. **Identify developmentally sensitive periods** — ages where environmental quality has disproportionate impact on outcomes.
3. **Specify school and learning environment parameters** — the most consequential architectural setting for child development.
4. **Connect to AGE-I to create a full lifespan model** — U-shaped sensitivity curves showing how PE vulnerability changes from birth through old age.

## Position Statements

### EXECUTIVE FUNCTION DEVELOPMENT: THE 25-YEAR MATURATION

**PS-Diamond: Prefrontal Cortex Is Under Construction Until ~25**

The most architecturally consequential fact about child development is that the prefrontal cortex — the brain region responsible for executive function, attentional control, working memory, and cognitive flexibility — does not reach full maturation until approximately age 25 (Diamond, 2013, ~5,000 citations). This has pervasive implications for every template in the library that involves cognitive control.

Developmental executive function milestones:

| Age | Executive function state | Architectural implication |
|-----|------------------------|--------------------------|
| 0–3 | Minimal inhibitory control; stimulus-driven attention | Environment must be SAFE above all; stimulus novelty → immediate approach |
| 3–6 | Emerging inhibitory control; short sustained attention (~5 min) | Structured environments support fragile control; distractors overwhelm easily |
| 6–9 | Growing working memory; beginning cognitive flexibility | Can navigate moderate complexity; still need clear spatial structure |
| 9–12 | Approaching adult working memory; improving inhibition | Can handle more environmental complexity; benefit from wayfinding challenge |
| 12–16 | Near-adult executive capacity but with risk-seeking bias | Thrill-seeking interacts with architectural affordance; challenging environments attractive |
| 16–25 | Adult-level cognition but still-developing prefrontal integration | Gradual convergence toward adult parameters |

For the template library, this means that the adult-calibrated parameters in CREA2 (processing style modulation), TP2 (threshold processing), and T38 (cognitive load) must be age-adjusted. A 7-year-old has approximately 60% of adult working memory capacity and 50% of adult sustained attention capacity. A classroom designed to adult parameters would either overwhelm or understimulate depending on which parameters are applied.

### NATURE AND ATTENTION IN CHILDREN

**PS-Kuo: Nature Exposure Is More Important for Children Than Adults**

Our research shows that nature exposure produces LARGER attention-restoration effects in children than in adults, and the effect is especially pronounced in children with ADHD (Kuo & Taylor, 2004, ~2,000 citations; Taylor et al., 2001, ~1,500 citations). A 20-minute walk in a park improved attention in children with ADHD by d ≈ 0.5–0.7 — an effect size comparable to methylphenidate.

The mechanism through the PE framework: children's directed attention systems are MORE depletable (less prefrontal capacity) AND slower to recover (less efficient restoration). Nature provides soft fascination (VIEW1 Channel 3) that permits the fragile directed-attention system to rest. Because children's attentional systems are more easily depleted AND more responsive to restoration, the benefit of nature is AMPLIFIED.

Estimated VIEW1 Channel 3 (restoration) age modifiers:

| Age | Restoration multiplier | Rationale |
|-----|----------------------|-----------|
| 3–6 | 1.5× | Highly depletable, highly responsive |
| 6–9 | 1.4× | Still easily depleted |
| 9–12 | 1.3× | Improving capacity but still benefits more than adults |
| 12–16 | 1.15× | Approaching adult levels |
| 16–25 | 1.05× | Near-adult |
| 25+ (adult) | 1.0× | Reference |

Note: This mirrors AGE-I's finding that older adults ALSO show amplified restoration (1.2–1.3×). The lifespan curve is U-shaped: high sensitivity in childhood, lowest in prime adulthood, rising again in old age.

**PS-Wells: Housing Quality Has Cumulative Developmental Effects**

The relationship between environmental quality and child development is not just acute but CUMULATIVE (Wells, 2000, ~800 citations; Evans, 2006, ~3,000 citations). Children living in crowded, noisy, low-quality housing show progressive deficits in reading ability, self-regulatory behavior, and physiological stress markers. The effects compound over years: 1 year of poor housing produces small effects; 5 years produces effects that are extremely difficult to reverse.

For the template library: the adult templates assume acute environmental effects (enter building → experience PE → outcome). For children, there must be an additional CHRONIC EXPOSURE term: sustained environmental quality shapes the developing nervous system, not just the momentary experience. A child in a well-designed school for 6 years is building different neural architecture than a child in a poorly designed one.

### MOTOR DEVELOPMENT AND ARCHITECTURAL EXPLORATION

**PS-Adolph: Children Are Natural Explorers — Architecture Should Support This**

Motor development research shows that infants and young children engage in extraordinary amounts of environmental exploration — a typical toddler takes approximately 2,400 steps per hour and falls approximately 17 times per hour (Adolph & Hoch, 2019, ~400 citations). This exploratory behavior is not waste; it is how children build their spatial cognitive maps (T3), calibrate their motor prediction systems (TP1), and develop environmental competence.

For TP1 (motor PE): Children's MFI values are LOW not because of decline (as in aging) but because their motor systems are still calibrating. A 3-year-old on stairs has an MFI comparable to a frail 80-year-old — but the developmental trajectory is UPWARD, and the motor challenge is BENEFICIAL rather than dangerous (with appropriate safety provisions).

| Age | Baseline MFI (flat) | Stairs MFI | Falls/hour | Architectural need |
|-----|---------------------|-----------|------------|-------------------|
| 1–2 | 0.50–0.60 | N/A (crawling) | 15–20 | Soft surfaces, no unguarded drops |
| 2–4 | 0.60–0.70 | 0.35–0.50 | 10–17 | Graded challenge, padded edges |
| 4–6 | 0.70–0.80 | 0.50–0.65 | 5–8 | Varied surfaces encouraged |
| 6–9 | 0.80–0.88 | 0.65–0.78 | 2–3 | Near-adult on simple surfaces |
| 9–12 | 0.85–0.92 | 0.75–0.85 | < 1 | Approaching adult competence |
| 12+ | 0.90–0.95 | 0.80–0.90 | Rare | Near-adult |

The architectural principle is the INVERSE of universal design for aging: where AGE-I prescribes reducing challenge to protect declining capacity, DEV-I prescribes PROVIDING challenge to build developing capacity. Children NEED surfaces to climb, slopes to navigate, textures to explore. Over-sanitized, featureless environments (flat carpet, no steps, no variety) deprive developing motor systems of the calibration signals they need.

### CLASSROOM DESIGN AND LEARNING

**PS-Barrett: The HEAD Study Quantifies Classroom Effects**

The HEAD study (Barrett et al., 2013, ~500 citations) measured the effect of classroom design on learning progress in 3,766 pupils across 153 classrooms in 27 schools. The results identified three design principles that collectively explained 16% of variance in academic progress — a substantial effect for an environmental variable:

1. **Naturalness** (light, temperature, air quality, nature views): ~50% of the explained variance. Classrooms with better daylight, appropriate temperature, and nature visibility produced faster learning. This maps directly to L2/L3 (light), VIEW1 (nature), and T29 (allostatic regulation).

2. **Individualization** (ownership, flexibility, connection): ~25% of the explained variance. Classrooms where children had personal territory (SOC3 territorial affordance) and flexible furniture arrangements learned faster.

3. **Stimulation level** (complexity, color): ~25% of the explained variance. Moderate visual complexity (T2 Goldilocks) and age-appropriate color (COL1/COL2) produced better outcomes. Both understimulation AND overstimulation impaired learning.

For CREA templates: the Barrett data provide an important constraint. The CREA2 "creative environment" (spacious, dim, moderate noise) may be COUNTERPRODUCTIVE for young children whose fragile executive systems need MORE structure, not less. The freedom-constraint tradeoff resolves differently at different ages: young children benefit from structured environments that support their developing executive function; older adolescents can benefit from the looser environments that promote adult-style divergent thinking.

### SENSITIVE PERIODS AND ENVIRONMENTAL IMPRINTING

**PS-Maurer: There Are Windows Where Environment Matters Most**

Visual development research reveals sensitive periods — time windows during which the developing brain is especially responsive to environmental input (Maurer & Lewis, 2001, ~500 citations). The most well-established: if a child does not receive patterned visual input in the first 2–3 years (e.g., due to congenital cataracts), the visual cortex does not develop normally, and the deficit persists even after the obstruction is removed.

For the template library, the sensitive-period concept extends beyond vision:

| Domain | Sensitive period | Architectural implication |
|--------|-----------------|--------------------------|
| Visual acuity & pattern | 0–6 years | Rich visual environments critical; understimulation harmful |
| Spatial cognition (T3 cognitive maps) | 3–8 years | Independent wayfinding practice builds spatial competence |
| Auditory processing (speech) | 0–7 years | Acoustic quality in childcare/schools affects language |
| Motor calibration (TP1) | 1–6 years | Varied motor challenge builds adaptive gait |
| Attention regulation (T25/ART) | 3–12 years | Nature exposure during this window has lasting effects |
| Social cognition (SOC templates) | 3–10 years | Social space design affects social skill development |

The architectural implication: design quality in environments for young children (childcare centers, kindergartens, primary schools) has DISPROPORTIONATE impact compared to design quality for adults. The same difference between a well-designed and poorly-designed building that produces a 5% effect for an adult may produce a 15–20% effect for a 5-year-old, and the developmental effect may persist long after the exposure ends.

### The Lifespan U-Curve

Combining DEV-I with AGE-I, the lifespan trajectory of environmental PE sensitivity follows a U-curve:

| Age range | PE sensitivity | Mechanism |
|-----------|---------------|-----------|
| 0–6 | HIGH | Developing systems; sensitive periods; limited executive buffer |
| 6–12 | MODERATE-HIGH | Growing capacity but still developing; high learning stakes |
| 12–25 | MODERATE | Approaching adult capacity; prefrontal still maturing |
| 25–50 | LOW (reference) | Peak processing capacity; maximum environmental resilience |
| 50–65 | MODERATE | Beginning decline; cascade initiating |
| 65–80 | MODERATE-HIGH | Significant decline; cascade multiplicative |
| 80+ | HIGH | Severe decline; minimal margin of safety |

This U-curve is the single most important cross-cutting finding across AGE-I and DEV-I: architecture matters most at the EXTREMES of the lifespan, and the same environmental features that are barely noticeable for a healthy 35-year-old may be developmentally critical for a 5-year-old or potentially dangerous for an 85-year-old.

## Testable Predictions

### Prediction 1: Nature Restoration Amplified in Children

**Protocol**: Within-subjects: nature-view vs. wall-view exposure (15 min), UFOV recovery measured. Children ages 6–9 (N=40) vs. adults 25–40 (N=40).
**Prediction**: Children show 30–50% larger attention restoration effect (d ≈ 0.6–0.8 vs. adult d ≈ 0.4–0.5).

### Prediction 2: Classroom Design Predicts Learning (HEAD Replication)

**Protocol**: Multi-site study in 50+ classrooms, measuring naturalness, individualization, stimulation. Outcome: standardized test progress over 1 academic year.
**Prediction**: Design variables explain 12–18% of variance in progress (replicating Barrett ~16%).

### Prediction 3: Motor Challenge Builds Capacity

**Protocol**: Longitudinal (2 years). Children aged 4–6 in motor-enriched playground design vs. standard flat playground. Measure: MFI progression, proprioceptive accuracy.
**Prediction**: Motor-enriched group shows faster MFI improvement (+0.10 vs. +0.05 over 2 years).

### Prediction 4: Lifespan U-Curve Validation

**Protocol**: Cross-sectional: 7 age groups (5–9, 10–14, 15–24, 25–40, 50–64, 65–79, 80+). Same standardized building assessment. Measure: wellbeing sensitivity to building quality score.
**Prediction**: U-shaped sensitivity curve peaking at extremes, with 25–40 as minimum.

## Scope Exclusions

1. **Infant/neonatal environments**: NICU and infant-room design requires specialized medical expertise beyond this panel.
2. **Special education**: Autism spectrum, intellectual disability, and specific learning disabilities each require dedicated environmental specifications.
3. **Playground safety standards**: Engineering safety standards (fall heights, impact attenuation) are regulatory rather than PE-mechanistic.
4. **Digital screen environments**: Children's interaction with digital/virtual environments deferred.
