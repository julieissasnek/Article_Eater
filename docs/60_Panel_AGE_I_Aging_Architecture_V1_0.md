## Cross-Cutting Age Moderation Parameters for Mechanistic Templates

## Panel Charge

Age moderation effects appear scattered across the template library — L2 provides an age-corrected melanopic irradiance formula, TP1 includes attentional cost multipliers for older adults, SOC1 references age-related proxemic shifts, and CREA-II documents individual-difference moderation. But no document systematically addresses how aging transforms the entire architecture-wellbeing relationship. Panel AGE-I is charged with:

1. **Systematizing age moderation across all calibrated template series** (L, MAT, TP, SOC, CREA, VIEW) — identifying which parameters shift, by how much, and through which mechanisms.

2. **Identifying age-specific architectural vulnerabilities** — conditions where aging transforms a mild PE into a dangerous or debilitating one.

3. **Specifying universal design parameters** — quantitative thresholds that ensure architectural environments remain supportive across the lifespan.

4. **Formalizing the sensory-decline cascade** — how presbyopia, presbycusis, olfactory decline, and proprioceptive degradation interact to compound architectural vulnerability.

## Position Statements

### THE FUNDAMENTAL AGING MECHANISM: REDUCED PROCESSING RESOURCES

**PS-Salthouse: Processing Speed Mediates Almost Everything**

The single most robust finding in cognitive aging research is the decline in processing speed — approximately 0.5 SD per decade from age 30 onward (Salthouse, 2010, ~3,000 citations). This speed decline mediates 75-80% of age-related variance in working memory, reasoning, and spatial ability. For the architectural PE framework, this means: every cognitive operation that mediates between environment and wellbeing SLOWS with age.

The architectural implications are pervasive. Wayfinding decisions take longer. Spatial orientation after transitions requires more time. Threshold crossings (TP2) demand more processing to update the event model. Motor adjustments to surface changes (TP1) are slower and more attentional. The prediction machinery itself is slower — predictions are generated less efficiently, and the error signal takes longer to resolve.

Estimated processing speed multiplier by decade:

| Age band | Speed multiplier | Working memory | Spatial reasoning |
|----------|-----------------|----------------|-------------------|
| 20-30 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 |
| 30-40 | 0.95 | 0.97 | 0.95 |
| 40-50 | 0.88 | 0.92 | 0.88 |
| 50-60 | 0.80 | 0.85 | 0.80 |
| 60-70 | 0.72 | 0.78 | 0.72 |
| 70-80 | 0.63 | 0.70 | 0.63 |
| 80-90 | 0.55 | 0.60 | 0.55 |

### THE SENSORY DECLINE CASCADE

**PS-Owsley: Vision Losses Are Not Just About Acuity**

The architectural community typically addresses visual aging as an acuity problem — make text larger, increase lighting levels. But the more consequential visual aging changes for architecture are: (a) contrast sensitivity decline — 2-3× worse by age 70, making low-contrast edges, step risers, and surface transitions harder to detect; (b) glare susceptibility — 3-5× worse, making direct sunlight, specular reflections, and bright-to-dark transitions disabling rather than merely uncomfortable; (c) dark adaptation slowdown — from ~7 minutes at age 20 to ~20+ minutes at age 70, making transitions between differently lit spaces hazardous; (d) useful field of view constriction — peripheral detection drops ~30% by age 70 (Ball et al., 1988, ~1,000 citations).

For the L-series templates:
- L1 (spectral PE): Lens yellowing filters short wavelengths; blue/violet contrast reduced ~50% by age 65
- L2 (circadian): Crystalline lens transmission at 480nm drops ~50% by age 65; elderly need ~2× melanopic irradiance (already in L-II calibration)
- L3 (daylight convergence): Glare susceptibility means optimal daylight must be INDIRECT for older adults; direct sun becomes a hazard rather than a benefit
- L4 (CCT ecological signal): Reduced blue sensitivity means warm CCT (< 3000K) appears even warmer; compensatory blue enrichment needed

**PS-Gordon-Salant: Hearing Loss Is Architectural**

Presbycusis affects ~33% of adults over 65 and ~50% over 75 (Lin et al., 2011, ~2,000 citations). The hearing loss is primarily high-frequency (> 2 kHz), which degrades speech consonant perception. In architectural acoustics, this means: (a) reverberation that is tolerable for young listeners becomes unintelligible for older listeners — RT60 tolerance drops from ~0.6s to ~0.4s; (b) background noise that is ignorable for young ears masks speech for older ears — the signal-to-noise ratio requirement increases from +5 dB to +15 dB; (c) spatial hearing (localizing sound sources) degrades, making large open spaces acoustically disorienting.

For the template library:
- AX1 (acoustic comfort): Speech intelligibility thresholds shift by approximately +10 dB SNR for adults 70+
- SOC templates: Conversational distance increases because speech must be louder and closer
- TP2 (threshold): Acoustic channel weight for threshold detection is reduced — older adults may miss acoustic transitions that signal spatial boundaries

**PS-Doty: Olfactory Decline Is Dramatic and Underappreciated**

Olfactory identification ability peaks at 20-40 and declines sharply after 60 — by age 80, ~75% of adults have significant olfactory impairment (Doty et al., 1984, ~3,000 citations). This is the most severe sensory decline by percentage, yet it receives the least architectural attention.

For OLF1 (olfactory transition PE): The olfactory channel contribution to threshold detection (TP2 weight 0.08) effectively drops to near zero for adults 70+. Olfactory wayfinding cues (bakery smell, coffee, garden scent) become unreliable. The safety implications are also severe — reduced ability to detect smoke, gas leaks, and spoiled food.

### MOTOR AGING AND ARCHITECTURAL VULNERABILITY

**PS-Hausdorff: The Gait Signature of Aging Predicts Falls**

Returning from TP-II with the MFI framework. The key aging data: DFA scaling exponent (alpha) declines from ~0.80 at age 30 to ~0.65 at age 70 and ~0.50 at age 80+ (Hausdorff et al., 1997, ~1,200 citations). This means the gait pattern becomes less adaptively complex — less able to make the moment-to-moment adjustments that smooth surfaces, manage transitions, and recover from perturbations.

The TP1 calibration already includes age bands. What AGE-I adds is the INTERACTION with other template parameters:

- **Surface × age**: A surface that produces MFI 0.85 in young adults may produce MFI 0.55 in frail older adults — below the universal design threshold of 0.70.
- **Threshold × age**: A 3-channel threshold that produces d ≈ 0.68 boundary strength in young adults demands more processing to cross for older adults. The recovery window extends from 3-5 strides to 8-12 strides.
- **Light × surface × age**: A dimly lit surface transition that is manageable for a young person with good contrast sensitivity becomes a fall hazard for an older person with reduced contrast sensitivity AND reduced gait adaptability.

This is the CASCADE principle: sensory decline + motor decline + cognitive slowing interact multiplicatively, not additively. A 3-factor combination that each reduces performance by 30% does not reduce total performance by 90% (additive) — it may reduce it by 66% (multiplicative: 0.7³ ≈ 0.34 remaining capacity).

**PS-Woollacott: Attentional Cost of Balance Doubles Then Triples**

Returning from TP-II. The key finding: maintaining balance in challenging conditions demands ~5% of attentional resources at age 30 but ~20-35% at age 80 (Woollacott & Shumway-Cook, 2002, ~1,500 citations). This has massive implications for multi-tasking in architectural environments — an older adult navigating an irregular surface while reading signage while managing a threshold transition may exceed 100% attentional capacity.

The implication for CREA templates: the walking-incubation benefit (CREA3) may be REDUCED or ELIMINATED for older adults on challenging surfaces, because the motor system consumes the attentional resources that would otherwise support creative mind-wandering. On smooth, well-designed surfaces, the incubation benefit should persist because the motor demand remains low.

### SOCIAL AND EMOTIONAL AGING

**PS-Carstensen: Aging Changes WHAT Matters, Not Just HOW MUCH**

Socioemotional selectivity theory (Carstensen et al., 1999, ~5,000 citations) shows that as people age and time horizons shorten, social motivations shift: older adults prioritize emotional meaningfulness over information-seeking, prefer smaller social networks of close ties over broad networks, and seek positively valenced experiences over novel ones.

For the SOC templates:
- SOC1 (proxemic PE): Older adults may tolerate CLOSER interpersonal distance with familiar others (smaller intimate zone for close relationships) but require GREATER distance from strangers (larger stranger zone)
- SOC3 (Dunbar territorial): The 5-layer intimate group becomes proportionally MORE important; the 150-layer clan becomes less salient. Architectural design for older adults should prioritize intimate-scale gathering spaces (4-8 people) over large communal areas
- SOC2 (privacy-encounter): The optimal privacy-encounter ratio may shift from 0.50 to ~0.55-0.60 for older adults — slightly more privacy preferred, but with easy access to chosen social contact

**PS-Chaudhury: Dementia Transforms the Entire PE Landscape**

For individuals with dementia (~10% of adults 65+, ~30% of those 85+), the PE framework predicts a catastrophic shift: the prediction machinery itself degrades. Spatial predictions become unreliable (disorientation), temporal predictions fail (time confusion), social predictions break down (failure to recognize), and material predictions weaken (inability to interpret environmental affordances).

Architectural implications for dementia-friendly design:
- **Reduce PE to near zero**: Minimize prediction errors by making environments simple, consistent, legible, and familiar
- **Maximize sensory redundancy**: If one channel fails, others must compensate — visual wayfinding cues backed by tactile cues backed by olfactory cues
- **Eliminate thresholds where possible**: TP2 threshold crossings that produce constructive episodic segmentation in healthy adults may produce disorienting confusion in dementia
- **Preserve overlearned spatial schemas**: Long-term spatial memory (scene grammar, T3 cognitive maps) is more preserved than episodic memory — buildings that match familiar spatial schemas produce less PE than novel configurations

## Cross-Examination and Calibration Debates

### Debate 1: Is Aging a Single Mechanism or Multiple Independent Declines?

**Salthouse**: Processing speed decline explains most age-related cognitive change. The various declines (memory, reasoning, spatial ability) are largely shared-variance phenomena explained by a common factor.

**Owsley**: The sensory declines are partially independent. You can have severe presbycusis with preserved vision, or vice versa. The CASCADE interaction is important precisely because the components can vary independently — predicting an individual's architectural vulnerability requires knowing their specific sensory profile, not just their age.

**Panel consensus**: **Age is a useful proxy but an imprecise one.** For population-level design guidelines, age bands work well (Salthouse's processing speed multiplier is robust). For individual assessment, sensory-specific profiling is needed. The template library should provide BOTH: age-band defaults and sensory-profile overrides.

### Debate 2: Should Architecture Compensate for Aging or Promote Active Aging?

**Hausdorff**: There is a tension between making environments EASIER for older adults (removing challenges, reducing PE) and maintaining the physical and cognitive DEMANDS that preserve function. The "use it or lose it" principle applies to gait dynamics: older adults who walk only on flat, smooth surfaces lose adaptive gait capacity faster than those who navigate varied terrain.

**Woollacott**: The answer is a GRADED CHALLENGE model. Environments should provide a baseline of safety (universal design thresholds) with OPTIONAL challenge layers — garden paths with varied surfaces alongside smooth corridors, stairs alongside elevators, complex wayfinding alongside clear direct routes. The older adult can CHOOSE the level of challenge appropriate to their current capacity.

**Panel consensus**: **Universal design thresholds are floors, not ceilings.** Every building should meet minimum accessibility standards (MFI > 0.70 for frail older adults). But the building should also OFFER graded challenge for those who can benefit from it. This is the "challenge gradient" principle: easy baseline + optional difficulty.

### The Sensory Cascade Interaction Model

When multiple sensory declines co-occur, the combined effect is MULTIPLICATIVE, not additive:

**Architectural vulnerability index** = Processing_speed × Vision × Hearing × Motor × Olfaction

Where each factor ranges from 1.0 (intact) to 0.3 (severely impaired).

Example for a healthy 75-year-old:
- Processing speed: 0.63
- Vision: 0.70 (moderate decline)
- Hearing: 0.75 (mild presbycusis)
- Motor: 0.70 (moderate gait decline)
- Olfaction: 0.50 (significant decline)

Vulnerability index: 0.63 × 0.70 × 0.75 × 0.70 × 0.50 = **0.116**

This means the person has approximately 12% of the environmental processing capacity of a healthy young adult — an 88% reduction. This is why environments that are merely mildly challenging for young adults can become hazardous for older adults: the CASCADE compresses the margin of safety dramatically.

### Universal Design Thresholds

Based on the cascade model, minimum architectural parameters for the 10th percentile of community-dwelling adults aged 75+:

| Domain | Parameter | Threshold | Rationale |
|--------|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| Light | Minimum ambient illuminance | 300 lux | Contrast sensitivity compensation |
| Light | Maximum luminance ratio (bright:dim) | 3:1 within room | Adaptation time compensation |
| Light | Transition zone illuminance gradient | 5 min adaptation space | Dark adaptation at 20+ min |
| Light | M-EDI minimum for circadian | 250 M-lux (2× standard) | Lens transmission loss |
| Acoustic | Maximum RT60 | 0.4s (speech spaces) | Presbycusis speech intelligibility |
| Acoustic | Minimum SNR for speech | +15 dB | Gordon-Salant threshold |
| Acoustic | Background noise maximum | 35 dBA (speech spaces) | Reduced noise filtering |
| Surface | Minimum COF (slip resistance) | 0.55 | Falls prevention |
| Surface | Maximum grade without rail | 1:20 (5%) | Motor stability threshold |
| Surface | Step riser maximum | 170 mm | TP1 MFI > 0.70 for frail older |
| Handrail | Height range | 865-965 mm | TP-II specification |
| Visual | Minimum contrast ratio (edges) | 70% | Owsley contrast data |

| Wayfinding | Maximum decision points before rest | 3 | Working memory × speed decline |
| Wayfinding | Redundant cue channels | ≥ 3 | Compensate for channel failure |
| Social | Intimate gathering space (per 20 residents) | 1 space for 4-8 people | Carstensen 5-layer priority |

### The Challenge Gradient Principle

Every parameter above is a FLOOR. Buildings should ALSO provide:

- **Optional challenge surfaces** alongside smooth paths (garden trails with varied terrain)
- **Stair access** alongside elevator access (with excellent handrails, good lighting, visible step edges)
- **Complex wayfinding routes** alongside simple direct routes
- **Larger social spaces** alongside intimate ones
- **Variable lighting** alongside consistently bright areas

The principle: **meet universal design thresholds everywhere, then offer challenge gradients for active aging.**

## Testable Predictions

### Prediction 1: Cascade Interaction

**Protocol**: Cross-sectional study of N=200 adults aged 65-90 in a standardized architectural environment. Measure: sensory profile (vision, hearing, proprioception, olfaction) + cognitive speed + environmental performance (wayfinding, threshold negotiation, social interaction quality).
**Prediction**: Multiplicative cascade model predicts environmental performance better (R² > 0.55) than any single sensory measure (R² < 0.25) or additive model (R² < 0.45).

### Prediction 2: Universal Design Thresholds Reduce Falls

**Protocol**: Pre-post study in residential care facility implementing the threshold parameters above. N=100 residents, 12-month follow-up.
**Prediction**: Falls reduced by 25-40%. Threshold-crossing incidents (TP2) show greatest improvement.

### Prediction 3: Nature View Restoration Increases with Age

**Protocol**: Attention restoration measured (UFOV recovery rate) after 10 min nature-view vs. wall-view exposure, in young (20-30) and older (65-80) adults.
**Prediction**: Older adults show 20-30% LARGER restoration benefit from nature view (Channel 3 amplification), despite reduced acuity (Channel 1 reduction).

### Prediction 4: Challenge Gradient Preserves Function

**Protocol**: Longitudinal study (18 months) comparing residential facilities with challenge-gradient design vs. fully accessible-only design. N=150 per group.
**Prediction**: Challenge-gradient group maintains or improves gait dynamics (MFI stability) while accessible-only group shows greater MFI decline (−0.05 to −0.10 over 18 months).

## Scope Exclusions

1. **Dementia-specific design**: Addressed conceptually (Chaudhury position statement) but not parameterized. Requires dedicated panel.
2. **Assistive technology**: Hearing aids, low-vision devices, mobility aids modify sensory input parameters but are beyond architectural scope.
3. **Acute illness and hospitalization**: Hospital design for acutely ill older adults involves medical equipment, infection control, and clinical workflow constraints beyond the template library's scope.
4. **End-of-life environments**: Palliative and hospice design has unique requirements (family presence, spiritual space, comfort over function) that merit a dedicated panel.
