### Figure 1: The Inverted-U Across History
**File**: `figure_1_historical_timeline.svg`

The inverted-U function has been independently rediscovered across 150 years of psychology, neuroscience, and design research. Each generation reformulated the principle in the theoretical language of its era—from Wundt's arousal intensity through Berlyne's collative variables to Friston's prediction error—yet the fundamental shape remained constant. The persistence of this function across theoretical frameworks suggests it reflects a genuine computational constraint rather than a measurement artifact. ATLAS (2026) provides the first formal unification: P(x) = exp(−(C(x) − C*)²/(2σ²)).

### Figure 2: The Formal Model P(x) with Parameter Variations
**File**: `figure_2_formal_model.svg`

The Goldilocks Principle's formal model captures individual and cultural variation with only two parameters. (A) The base model: preference peaks at optimal complexity C* and decays symmetrically for under- and over-stimulation. (B) Expertise shifts the optimum rightward—experienced observers prefer higher complexity. (C) Tolerance bandwidth σ controls how sharply preference drops away from the optimum: narrow σ produces specialists with strong preferences, wide σ produces generalists who tolerate broader ranges. (D) Cultural calibration: Japanese aesthetic traditions (wabi-sabi, ma) favor lower complexity with tight tolerance, while Baroque traditions favor higher complexity with broader acceptance. These differences arise from environmental statistics during development, not innate differences.

The inverted-U function appears across all five sensory modalities studied, with domain-specific optima calibrated to environmental statistics. Each panel shows preference as a function of stimulus intensity in one modality, with the shaded region indicating the Goldilocks zone (within one σ of the optimum). Visual complexity peaks at fractal dimension D ≈ 1.3–1.5, matching the fractal statistics of natural scenes. Thermal comfort peaks at adaptive neutral. Acoustic preference peaks at 50–60 dB LAeq. The consistency of the inverted-U shape across modalities—despite vastly different neural processing pathways—constitutes the strongest evidence for the cross-modal universality claim.

### Figure 6: Cultural Calibration Landscape
**File**: `figure_6_cultural_calibration.svg`

Cultural aesthetic traditions calibrate the Goldilocks optimum C* differently across sensory modalities, but all traditions exhibit the inverted-U shape. Japanese aesthetics (wabi-sabi, ma) places C* at relatively low complexity across modalities, favoring simplicity, spaciousness, and quiet. Baroque traditions shift C* rightward toward higher visual and acoustic complexity. West African design favors fractal spatial organization with high visual C* but moderate acoustic C*. Critically, no culture's preferences are monotonic—all show decreasing preference beyond their optimum. This universality of the inverted-U shape, despite variation in C* location, supports the claim that the Goldilocks Principle reflects a computational constraint of neural processing rather than a cultural convention.

### Figure 7: Processing Fluency Mechanism
**File**: `figure_7_processing_fluency.svg`

Processing fluency provides the psychological mechanism linking stimulus complexity to preference. Low-complexity stimuli are processed easily (high fluency) but generate little new learning, producing boredom. High-complexity stimuli overwhelm processing capacity (low fluency), producing frustration and aversion. Intermediate complexity produces the optimal balance: enough novelty to engage learning mechanisms but manageable enough for successful processing. The metacognitive signal of 'processing just right' is experienced as positive affect—the subjective experience of the Goldilocks zone. This mechanism explains why expertise shifts C* rightward: experts have greater processing capacity, so higher complexity still feels fluent.

### Figure 8: Theory Integration — Intellectual Surplus
**File**: `figure_8_intellectual_surplus.svg`

The Goldilocks Principle's intellectual surplus emerges at the intersection of four foundational theories. Each T1 framework (predictive processing, interoceptive-constructionist affect, neuromodulatory systems, individual experience with dual-process theory) explains a subset of the phenomena associated with optimal stimulation. Pairwise intersections produce important but known connections (e.g., prediction error → affect, allostasis → neuromodulation). The central region—where all four frameworks converge—is the domain of the Goldilocks Principle: the claim that cross-modal optimization under metabolic constraint is a universal computational principle. This intellectual surplus is empirically testable (do all modalities show the same functional form?) and practically consequential (architects can target optimal ranges across all sensory dimensions simultaneously).

### Figure 9: Design Implications Dashboard
**File**: `figure_9_design_dashboard.svg`

Actionable design targets derived from the Goldilocks Principle. Each horizontal bar shows the range of stimulus intensity for one modality, with the shaded Goldilocks zone marking the range of optimal preference. Architects and designers can use these targets directly: visual complexity at fractal dimension 1.2–1.5 (achievable through fenestration patterns, material textures, and vegetation); thermal comfort at adaptive neutral ± 1°C (requiring active climate control and natural ventilation); acoustic environments at 50–60 dB LAeq (achievable through spatial planning and material absorption). The key insight is that *all modalities should be optimized simultaneously*—a visually optimal building that is thermally uncomfortable will not produce the full Goldilocks effect.
