## Part 1: Individual Position Statements

### 1.1 Grant Hildebrand — Prospect-Refuge: The Architectural Evidence

Let me ground this discussion in what we actually observe about prospect-refuge preferences in buildings (Hildebrand, 1999; Appleton, 1975).

**Prospect** is defined by: long sightlines, elevated vantage points, open visual fields, views to the distance, transparent barriers (glass walls that provide visual access without physical exposure). In buildings: mezzanines overlooking atriums, window seats with panoramic views, balconies, rooftop terraces.

**Refuge** is defined by: enclosed or partially enclosed spaces, overhead cover, back-to-wall positioning, reduced visual exposure from behind, controllable access points. In buildings: window alcoves, booth seating, canopied areas, spaces under staircases, reading nooks.

The critical architectural insight: **the most preferred spaces provide BOTH simultaneously** — prospect FROM refuge. A window seat in a thick-walled building provides prospect (view out) from refuge (wall behind, ceiling above, niche enclosure). A mezzanine with a railing provides prospect (view over the atrium) from refuge (back to the wall, elevated position).

Frank Lloyd Wright intuitively understood this. His residential designs systematically combine low-ceilinged entry sequences (refuge) opening onto double-height living spaces with panoramic windows (prospect). The compression-release sequence produces a visceral response that I believe maps onto the neural transition from threat-vigilance to safety-assessment that Mobbs can describe.

**What architects consistently get wrong**: they confuse prospect with exposure. A glass box with floor-to-ceiling windows on all sides provides maximal prospect but ZERO refuge — you are visible from every direction, with no protected back. People in such spaces are physiologically stressed (I have seen the data from post-occupancy evaluations). The solution is to provide prospect *from* a protected position, not to maximize visual access at the expense of enclosure.

### 1.2 Russell Epstein — The PPA Processes Spatial Layout, Not Aesthetic Preference

The parahippocampal place area (PPA) responds to the spatial layout of scenes — it encodes geometric properties like openness, depth, mean distance, and enclosure (Epstein & Kanwisher, 1998; Epstein, 2008; Kravitz, Peng, & Baker, 2011). This is the neural substrate that processes prospect and refuge as spatial properties.

What the panel needs to understand: PPA activation is driven by spatial geometry, not by content. A photograph of a deep forest vista and a photograph of a long corridor produce similar PPA activation if they have similar spatial statistics (depth, openness). PPA doesn't know "nature" from "building" — it knows "open" from "enclosed," "deep" from "shallow," "navigable" from "blocked."

**Specific PPA responses relevant to prospect-refuge:**
- **Openness**: PPA activation increases with scene openness (more open = more PPA activity). Open landscapes, wide plazas, panoramic views → high PPA.
- **Navigability**: PPA responds to the affordance of movement through the scene — can you walk into this space? Navigable paths → high PPA.
- **Boundaries**: PPA is sensitive to spatial boundaries — walls, horizons, enclosures. It encodes WHERE the boundaries are, not whether they're pleasant.

The PPA works in concert with the **retrosplenial cortex** (RSC), which provides viewpoint-independent spatial representations (allocentric coding). Together, PPA + RSC form the scene processing network that feeds into the hippocampal cognitive map (T3).

**For the reduction**: Prospect activates the PPA (high openness, long sightlines → strong scene processing). Refuge is processed by a different system — the threat/safety circuit involving the amygdala, periaqueductal gray (PAG), and vmPFC. The preference for *combined* prospect-refuge requires both systems to be engaged: PPA processing a deep, open scene (prospect satisfied) while the threat circuit is in safety mode (refuge satisfied). The felt preference arises from this joint state.

### 1.3 Eleanor Maguire — Hippocampal Place Cells and the Cognitive Map of Prospect

Building on Epstein's scene processing, let me describe what happens when the scene representation reaches the hippocampus (Maguire, 2001; O'Keefe & Nadel, 1978).

The hippocampus contains **place cells** — neurons that fire when the animal is in a specific location. The collection of place cells forms a cognitive map of the environment. **Grid cells** (in entorhinal cortex) provide the metric framework — they encode distances and directions between places.

**Prospect in hippocampal terms**: When you have a wide view, you can see many locations from your current position. Your hippocampus activates place cell representations for all the visible locations — not just where you are, but where you could be. This is a form of *prospective coding* — the hippocampus is simulating possible futures (possible locations you could navigate to). A wide prospect activates more prospective place cells than a narrow view, providing richer spatial possibilities.

**Refuge in hippocampal terms**: When you are in an enclosed, protected position, the hippocampus encodes your current location as having limited access points. Grid cells represent the distance to boundaries. An enclosed position with few entry points has a specific grid cell signature: high boundary proximity, limited escape routes — but this is only threatening if the threat system is active. When the threat system is quiet (safe environment), the same enclosed position produces a sense of *containment* rather than *entrapment*.

**The prospect-from-refuge synthesis**: The hippocampal cognitive map simultaneously represents (1) your current safe position (enclosed, bounded, protected) and (2) the extensive visible space you could access. This combination — safety plus possibility — may be the cognitive-level description of what architects call a "good space." You know where you are, you feel protected, and you can see where you might go.

This connects to T3 (SN_COGNITIVE_MAP_001) directly: the cognitive map IS the prospect representation. And it connects to T23 (SN_CONTEXT_MEMORY_002): the hippocampus doesn't just encode current space, it constructs scenes (Hassabis & Maguire, 2007) — including the implied space beyond the visible prospect.

### 1.4 Dean Mobbs — The Predator Imminence Continuum: Refuge as Anti-Threat

My work on defensive behavior (Mobbs, Hagan, Dalgleish, Silston, & Prévost, 2015; Mobbs et al., 2007) provides the neural mechanism for refuge-seeking that the design theorists are describing intuitively.

The **predator imminence continuum** describes a graded set of defensive responses based on threat distance:

1. **Pre-encounter** (threat is possible but not detected): Behavioral strategy = cautious exploration, risk assessment. Neural substrate = hippocampus (contextual threat memory), vmPFC (threat appraisal), bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST — sustained anxiety). This is the state of walking through an unfamiliar neighborhood.

2. **Post-encounter** (threat detected but distant): Behavioral strategy = freeze, assess, plan escape. Neural substrate = amygdala (fear response), ventrolateral PAG (freezing), hippocampus (spatial escape route planning). This is the state of seeing a suspicious person across a dark parking lot.

3. **Circa-strike** (threat is immediate): Behavioral strategy = fight or flight, panic escape. Neural substrate = dorsolateral PAG (active defense), hypothalamus (autonomic mobilization). This is the state of direct confrontation.

**Refuge eliminates the need for defensive behavior** by making the environment "pre-pre-encounter" — no threat is possible because the position is physically protected. In neural terms: refuge reduces amygdala activation, reduces BNST sustained anxiety, and allows vmPFC to maintain tonic threat suppression. This is the SAME vmPFC → amygdala → HPA pathway that SRT Reduction 1 identified. Refuge contributes to stress recovery by reducing the threat level at the very first stage of the cascade.

**The prospect-refuge synthesis in threat terms**: Prospect provides *information* about potential threats (you can see them coming), which reduces BNST sustained anxiety (the worst kind of stress — ambiguous, sustained, uncertain threat). Refuge provides *protection* from potential threats, which reduces amygdala reactivity (even if you see a threat, you are protected). Together, prospect + refuge reduce both uncertainty-based anxiety (BNST) and fear-based arousal (amygdala).

**Architectural implication**: The worst spaces for threat neurobiology are those with neither prospect nor refuge — enclosed spaces with no view (can't see threats) and no protection (can't escape them). Underground parking garages, windowless corridors with multiple entry points, open plan offices with one's back to a busy thoroughfare. These spaces maintain chronic low-level BNST activation (sustained anxiety) that contributes to allostatic load.

### 1.5 Richard Taylor — Fractal Fluency: What the Evidence Actually Shows

I coined the term "fractal fluency" to describe the phenomenon that mid-range fractal patterns produce physiological relaxation and aesthetic preference (Taylor, 2006; Taylor, Spehar, Van Donkelaar, & Hagerhall, 2011; Taylor et al., 2021). Let me be precise about what we have and haven't established.

**What we have established:**
- Humans prefer visual patterns with fractal dimension D ≈ 1.3–1.5 across many studies (Spehar et al., 2003; Hagerhall et al., 2004). This preference is cross-cultural and emerges early in development.
- EEG studies show that viewing patterns with D ≈ 1.3 produces increased frontal alpha power (relaxation marker) compared to patterns with very low D (~1.1, too simple) or very high D (~1.9, too complex) (Hagerhall et al., 2008).
- Eye tracking shows that fractal patterns with D ≈ 1.3 produce the widest distribution of fixations — people scan the entire pattern evenly rather than fixating on specific features (Taylor et al., 2011). This "distributed gaze" pattern is consistent with the effortless, non-focused attention that ART calls "soft fascination."
- Natural scenes have fractal dimensions predominantly in the range D ≈ 1.2–1.6, with the most common values around 1.3 (Mandelbrot, 1982; Pentland, 1984). So the preferred fractal range matches the most common natural statistics.

**What remains speculative:**
- The *mechanism* by which mid-range fractals produce relaxation. My hypothesis is that the visual system is "tuned" to process fractal patterns with D ≈ 1.3 efficiently — this is the fractal dimension of the most common natural scenes the visual system evolved to process. Processing efficiency → low computational demand → relaxation. But this "tuning" hypothesis has not been directly demonstrated at the neural level.
- Whether the effect operates via early visual cortex (V1/V2 spatial frequency tuning) or via higher-level scene processing (PPA/RSC). The EEG data suggest a relatively early visual process (alpha power changes, not late cognitive components), but EEG cannot localize the source.
- The exact parameters: is 1.3 the optimum? Or is it a range? Does it depend on the specific spatial frequency content, or just the fractal dimension? Our data suggest a range rather than a point, with individual variation of about ±0.2.

**What the panel should know about architectural application:**
- Natural materials (wood, stone, textured plaster) have mid-range fractal dimensions. Synthetic materials (smooth plastic, polished metal, flat drywall) have very low fractal dimensions. This provides a simple design heuristic: use natural materials to get mid-range D.
- The fractal fluency effect operates at multiple scales simultaneously. A building can have fractal structure at the urban scale (complex skyline), the building scale (articulated facade), the room scale (textured walls, patterned flooring), and the material scale (wood grain, stone texture). Each scale contributes independently.
- Jackson Pollock's paintings, which have D ≈ 1.3–1.7, produce the same relaxation response as natural scenes (Taylor et al., 2011). This confirms that the mechanism is about fractal statistics, not about "nature" per se.

### 1.6 Nikos Salingaros — Biophilic Complexity: Beyond Fractals

Fractal dimension captures one aspect of biophilic complexity, but not all of it (Salingaros, 2003; 2006). My mathematical framework identifies several properties of natural and traditional architecture that produce positive responses:

1. **Scaling hierarchy**: Structures at multiple scales, with the size ratio between scales approximately constant (roughly 2.5:1 at each step). Traditional architecture has this — ornamental detail at small scales, window proportions at medium scales, building proportions at large scales. Modernist architecture deliberately eliminates it — smooth surfaces, no ornament, a single scale.

2. **Local symmetries**: Small-scale symmetries that don't extend globally. A single tile has bilateral symmetry; the tile field has translational symmetry; but the building facade has asymmetric composition. This hierarchy of symmetries at different scales is characteristic of natural forms and produces what I call "organized complexity."

3. **Contrast and color harmony**: Boundaries between visual elements should have appropriate contrast — too little contrast produces blandness; too much produces visual chaos. Natural scenes have a specific distribution of edge contrasts that is neither uniform nor extreme.

4. **Biomorphic forms**: Curved, organic shapes that reference biological growth patterns. These are not necessarily literal depictions of nature — they are geometric forms (parabolas, spirals, branching structures) that share mathematical properties with biological growth.

What ties these together: they are all properties that produce **moderate information content with high predictability** — the same "structured complexity" that Joye described for ART's Extent construct (Panel T2-A). The visual system finds these patterns engaging but not demanding. This is the visual equivalent of soft fascination.

**For the reduction**: Biophilic complexity is not a single mechanism but a collection of visual properties that converge on the same neural response: moderate prediction error + high processing fluency. The templates involved are T1 (prediction error) and T2 (Goldilocks complexity), with the specific visual statistics determined by the fractal, scaling, symmetry, and contrast properties I've described.

### 1.7 Colin Ellard — Walking Studies: Prospect, Refuge, and Complexity in Real Streets

In our psychogeography studies (Ellard, 2015; Ellard & Condia, 2020), we measure physiological and self-reported responses as people walk through urban environments with varying spatial and visual properties. Relevant findings:

**Prospect-related**:
- Walking into an open plaza from a narrow street produces a measurable EDA *decrease* (arousal reduction) within 15-30 seconds. Self-report: "relief," "openness," "freedom."
- Walking from an open space into a narrow alley produces an EDA *increase* (arousal) within 10-20 seconds. Self-report depends on context: if the alley feels safe (well-lit, visible exit), "cozy," "intimate." If unsafe (dark, no exit visible), "threatening," "trapped."
- The compression-release sequence (narrow → wide → narrow → wide) produces the strongest positive responses — greater than sustained openness or sustained enclosure. This supports Hildebrand's architectural sequencing principle.

**Complexity-related**:
- Facades with high visual complexity (ornament, varied materials, articulation) produce increased engagement (longer dwell times, more fixations) and positive affect compared to blank facades.
- The relationship is inverted-U: moderate complexity is preferred over both very low (blank wall) and very high (visual chaos of competing signs and colors).
- Active facades (ground-floor retail with transparent windows, changing displays) produce longer dwell times than passive facades (blank walls, shuttered storefronts) — by a factor of approximately 3-4x.

**The architectural sequencing finding is important**: It suggests that the biophilic response is not about a static optimal level of prospect, refuge, or complexity, but about *variation over time* — the brain's prediction machinery is engaged by *changes* in spatial properties, not by any single spatial state. This connects directly to T1 (prediction error): the positive response to compression-release is a prediction error response (expectations formed in the narrow space are violated by the wide space, producing moderate PE that feels rewarding).

## Part 2: Reduction 1 — Prospect-Refuge

### 2.1 Panel Discussion

**Opus**: The preliminary map proposed Prospect/Refuge → T3 + T5. T3 = SN_COGNITIVE_MAP_001 (cognitive map) and T5 = NM_THREAT_HPA_001 (threat/HPA). This captures the spatial and threat components but misses the scene processing (PPA), the defensive behavior circuitry (PAG/BNST), and the dynamic sequencing that Ellard describes. Let me ask the panel to build the full DAG.

**Epstein**: The prospect component is primarily processed by the PPA-RSC-hippocampal scene network:
- PPA encodes spatial layout properties: openness, depth, navigability
- RSC translates this into viewpoint-independent (allocentric) coordinates
- Hippocampus integrates into the cognitive map and activates prospective place cell representations

The key variable is **visible navigable space** — the volume of space you can see AND could potentially move through. PPA activation correlates with this measure. A panoramic window with a mountain view has high visible space but low navigable space (you can't walk through the window). A mezzanine overlooking a lobby has both high visible and high navigable space. The prediction: mezzanines should produce stronger prospect responses than windows.

**Maguire**: The hippocampal contribution is about *possibility space*. When you have a wide prospect, the hippocampus activates representations of many possible locations and routes — this is the forward-looking, prospective function. In our London taxi driver studies, extensive navigation experience enlarged the posterior hippocampus (where spatial representations are densest). The richness of the prospect determines how many prospective representations are activated.

This has a testable implication: people with richer hippocampal representations (more spatial experience, larger posterior hippocampus) may derive greater benefit from prospect, because they can construct more elaborate prospective scenes. Spatial expertise modulates the prospect response.

**Mobbs**: The refuge component is processed by the threat/defensive behavior system. Let me be very specific about the circuit:

1. **Amygdala** — evaluates threat level based on all available information (visual, auditory, contextual, memory-based). In a space with refuge (back-to-wall, overhead cover, limited access points), the amygdala's threat evaluation resolves to LOW because the spatial configuration reduces vulnerability.

2. **BNST** (bed nucleus of the stria terminalis) — mediates *sustained* anxiety from ambiguous threat. In a space WITHOUT refuge (exposed, back to open thoroughfare, many approach vectors), BNST is tonically active, producing the chronic low-level unease that people report in open-plan offices. In a space WITH refuge, BNST activity is reduced.

3. **vmPFC** — top-down regulation of amygdala and BNST. In a safe refuge, vmPFC maintains tonic suppression of threat circuits. This is the SAME vmPFC node identified in ART and SRT reductions — further confirming vmPFC as the shared bottleneck.

4. **PAG** (periaqueductal gray) — not typically engaged in normal architectural experience, but relevant in extreme cases (feeling trapped, claustrophobia). Ventrolateral PAG mediates freeze; dorsolateral PAG mediates active escape. A space that activates PAG is architecturally failing.

**Ellard**: The dynamic sequencing I described — compression-release — maps onto the threat/safety transition. Entering a narrow space activates mild threat circuitry (BNST, mild amygdala). Emerging into an open, safe prospect produces a *relief response* — threat circuitry deactivates, vmPFC reasserts control, and the contrast between threat and safety produces a positive affective signal (relief is rewarding — the offset of a negative state activates mesolimbic reward circuitry). This relief response may be the mechanism underlying Hildebrand's compression-release sequencing principle.

**Hildebrand**: Colin's explanation captures what I've observed architecturally for decades but couldn't explain mechanistically. The best buildings create a *journey* through varying prospect-refuge states. Wright's Fallingwater: you approach through low, compressed spaces (refuge, mild tension) and then the living room opens onto the waterfall vista (prospect, release). The sequence creates a narrative of spatial experience that is more powerful than any single spatial state.

**Taylor**: I want to connect this to fractal structure. Natural landscapes provide prospect-refuge at multiple scales simultaneously. A forest clearing: at the large scale, the clearing is prospect and the surrounding trees are refuge. At the medium scale, individual trees provide refuge and gaps between trees provide prospect. At the small scale, foliage is refuge and sightlines through branches are prospect. This multi-scale prospect-refuge nesting is a form of fractal structure — self-similar spatial organization at different scales.

## Part 3: Reduction 2 — Biophilic Complexity

### 3.1 Panel Discussion

**Salingaros**: Biophilic complexity is not a single variable but a syndrome of visual properties. Let me decompose it into components that can be individually reduced:

1. **Scaling hierarchy** — structures at multiple scales with consistent ratios (~2.5:1)
2. **Fractal dimension** — mid-range D ≈ 1.3–1.5 (Taylor's domain, separate reduction)
3. **Local symmetries** — small-scale symmetries that don't extend globally
4. **Edge contrast distribution** — moderate contrast between visual elements
5. **Biomorphic forms** — curves, organic shapes, biological growth references
6. **Material texture** — natural materials with inherent mid-range fractal D

**Taylor**: Properties 1 and 2 are closely related — a scaling hierarchy with consistent ratios IS fractal structure. But you're right that 3–6 add dimensions beyond fractal D alone.

**Salingaros**: The unifying principle is **organized complexity** — visual information that is simultaneously rich (high entropy) and structured (high mutual information between scales/regions). This is what distinguishes biophilic complexity from two failure modes:
- **Minimalism** (low entropy, low mutual information) — boring, sterile, the "sick building" aesthetic
- **Visual chaos** (high entropy, low mutual information) — overwhelming, stressful, the Times Square problem

**Epstein**: From the PPA perspective, organized complexity produces a specific response pattern. PPA activation is driven by spatial layout, but the *duration* and *distribution* of PPA engagement is modulated by visual complexity. High organized complexity produces sustained PPA engagement — the scene processing network keeps finding new structure to encode. Low complexity produces brief PPA engagement — there's nothing more to process. Chaos produces fragmented PPA engagement — the system can't find coherent structure.

**Bar**: I can connect this to predictive processing. Organized complexity produces **prediction errors that are consistently resolvable**. At each scale, you generate predictions based on the pattern at coarser scales. The predictions are slightly wrong (nonzero PE) but correctable (the error has structure that allows updating). This produces a cascade of small, satisfying prediction-error-resolution cycles — the computational equivalent of soft fascination.

Chaos produces prediction errors that are NOT resolvable — the errors are large and random, so prediction updating doesn't converge. This is computationally expensive and affectively negative. Minimalism produces no prediction errors — the predictions are immediately correct because there's nothing to predict. This is computationally trivial but boring.

**Altomonte**: I want to ground this in measurable building properties. Biophilic complexity in buildings comes from:
- **Natural daylight variation** — patches of light and shadow that change with time of day and weather. Fractal edge structure at the light-shadow boundary.
- **View richness** — views that include multiple depth planes, natural elements, sky, and dynamic content (clouds, vegetation movement, water).
- **Material palette** — natural materials with inherent visual complexity (wood grain D ≈ 1.3, stone texture D ≈ 1.4, brick D ≈ 1.2) vs. synthetic materials with low complexity (polished metal D ≈ 1.0, flat drywall D ≈ 1.0).
- **Spatial articulation** — rooms with varied ceiling heights, alcoves, niches, columns — spatial complexity at the room scale.

Each of these is measurable. Daylight can be quantified by luminance contrast ratio. Views can be scored on depth planes and content diversity. Materials can be characterized by fractal D. Spatial articulation can be measured by isovist analysis (visible area from any point).

## Part 4: Reduction 3 — Fractal Fluency

### 4.1 Panel Discussion

**Taylor**: Let me be very precise about what fractal fluency claims and what it doesn't.

**The core claim**: The human visual system processes fractal patterns with D ≈ 1.3 more efficiently than non-fractal patterns or fractals with very different D, and this processing efficiency produces measurable relaxation.

**The evidence chain**:
1. Preference: D ≈ 1.3 is preferred (Spehar et al., 2003). Robust. Multiple replications.
2. Physiology: D ≈ 1.3 produces increased frontal alpha (relaxation) (Hagerhall et al., 2008). Moderate. Few replications.
3. Eye movement: D ≈ 1.3 produces widest fixation distribution (Taylor et al., 2011). Moderate.
4. Mechanism: processing efficiency → relaxation. Hypothesized. NOT directly demonstrated.

**Aspinall**: For ecological measurement: fractal D of building facades and interiors can be measured from photographs using box-counting algorithms. This is a *physical property of the built environment* that can be specified in design and verified in construction. The chain: design specifies D → construction achieves D → measurement verifies D → occupant response measured. This is the most directly controllable environmental variable in the entire Article Eater framework.

**Salingaros**: I want to add a nuance. The optimal D depends on the *scale of observation*. At arm's length (material texture), D ≈ 1.3 is optimal. At room scale (wall patterns, window arrangements), D ≈ 1.4 may be optimal. At building scale (facade articulation), D ≈ 1.5 may be optimal. At urban scale (skyline), D ≈ 1.6–1.7 may be acceptable. This is because the visual processing at different scales engages different spatial frequency channels with different tuning properties.

**Taylor**: That's an interesting hypothesis but we don't have the data to confirm scale-dependent optima. Our studies used patterns at a single viewing distance. The multi-scale prediction is a research target, not an established finding.

## Part 7: Testable Predictions

### Prediction 1 (from Prospect-Refuge)
**The mezzanine effect**: Mezzanines overlooking atriums (high prospect + high navigable space) should produce stronger physiological recovery markers than windows with distant views (high prospect + low navigable space). Both provide prospect, but the mezzanine adds navigable possibility. **Test**: Compare RSA, EDA, and self-report between 15-minute breaks on a mezzanine vs. by a panoramic window in the same building.

### Prediction 2 (from Prospect-Refuge, dynamic)
**Compression-release sequencing**: A building entry sequence that moves through narrow → wide → narrow → wide spaces should produce greater net positive affect than a building with constant spatial dimensions. **Test**: Compare Ellard-style walking EDA/HRV profiles between a sequenced building and a constant-dimension building, controlling for total journey time and distance.

### Prediction 3 (from Fractal Fluency)
**Material-level fractal D effect**: Offices with natural materials (wood paneling D ≈ 1.3, exposed brick D ≈ 1.2) on visible surfaces should produce lower cortisol and higher RSA compared to offices with smooth synthetic materials (painted drywall D ≈ 1.0) at matched room dimensions and lighting. **Test**: Within-subject crossover design; workers spend one week in each office; daily cortisol and 5-minute seated HRV measured.

### Prediction 4 (from Biophilic Complexity)
**The blank wall effect**: Corridor walls with organized visual complexity (textured materials, varied pattern, subtle ornament) should produce faster walking pace and lower EDA than blank corridor walls. Faster pace = less avoidance; lower EDA = less stress. **Test**: Ellard-style walking study with matched corridor dimensions differing only in wall treatment.

### Prediction 5 (from System-level)
**Biophilia as ART enabler**: A workspace with high biophilic properties (natural materials, views, prospect-refuge configuration) should show GREATER ART restoration benefit from a 20-minute nature break than a workspace with low biophilic properties, because the biophilic workspace doesn't deplete directed attention as rapidly in the first place. **Test**: Factorial design: high vs. low biophilic workspace × nature break vs. indoor break, measured by cognitive test performance.
