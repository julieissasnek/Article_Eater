## Panel Charter

**Primary Question**: Through what distinct psychological and neural mechanisms does music evoke emotions, and how do these mechanisms relate to the prediction error architecture established in Panels M-I and M-II?

**Why This Panel Third**: Panels M-I and M-II established the computational machinery — how the brain predicts pitch, harmony, rhythm, and meter, and how violations of those predictions produce prediction error signals. But prediction error is not emotion. The question this panel must answer is: how does the brain TRANSFORM prediction errors (and other acoustic features) into the felt experiences we call musical emotions? This transformation is not a single process. Multiple mechanisms — some subcortical and automatic, some cortical and deliberative, some dependent on prediction and some independent of it — operate simultaneously to produce the rich emotional landscape of musical experience. A complete theory of music cognition requires their enumeration and their integration.

**Scope**: This panel covers the full taxonomy of mechanisms by which music evokes emotion: brainstem reflexes, rhythmic entrainment, evaluative conditioning, emotional contagion, visual imagery, episodic memory, musical expectancy, and aesthetic judgment. It covers the neural circuitry for music-evoked emotion (amygdala, striatum, vmPFC, insula, ACC). It covers the paradox of negative emotions in music (why we enjoy sad music). It does NOT cover musical syntax (Panel M-IV) or cross-cultural universals in musical emotion (Panel M-V), though connections to those panels will be flagged throughout.

**Presiding**: Opus (theory architect)

## Part 1: Individual Position Statements

### 1.1 Patrik Juslin — BRECVEMA: Eight Roads from Sound to Feeling

Let me present the framework that will organize our discussion (Juslin, 2013; Juslin & Västfjäll, 2008; Juslin, Liljeström, Västfjäll, & Lundqvist, 2010).

Music does not evoke emotion through a single mechanism. There are at least **eight distinct psychological mechanisms**, each with different evolutionary origins, neural substrates, developmental trajectories, information requirements, and speed of operation. I call these the BRECVEMA mechanisms:

**1. Brainstem reflex (B)**: The auditory brainstem responds to basic acoustic features — sudden loud sounds, rapid onset, extreme frequencies, dissonance — with automatic arousal responses. This is the startle reflex and its graded relatives. A cymbal crash, a sudden fortissimo, a shrieking dissonance — these produce physiological arousal (increased heart rate, skin conductance, muscle tension) regardless of musical context, training, or expectation. The brainstem reflex does not process "music" — it processes acoustic energy. It is fast (~50ms), automatic, and subcortical. Musically, it is the mechanism behind the raw physical impact of loud, sudden, or harsh sounds.

**2. Rhythmic entrainment (R)**: The auditory-motor coupling system documented in Panel M-II produces affective responses through the alignment (or misalignment) of internal oscillatory states with external rhythmic patterns. A fast, regular beat entrains motor and autonomic rhythms upward (arousal, activation, energy); a slow, gentle pulse entrains them downward (relaxation, calm). This is NOT a cognitive response to rhythm — it is a direct physiological coupling between acoustic periodicity and bodily oscillators (heart rate, respiration, motor cortex). Thaut's clinical evidence (Panel M-II) demonstrates the potency of this mechanism.

**3. Evaluative conditioning (E)**: Music acquires emotional associations through repeated pairing with emotionally significant events. The song that played during your wedding, the film score associated with a terrifying scene, the national anthem paired with patriotic ceremony — these pairings transfer the emotional valence of the event to the music itself. Evaluative conditioning is slow to form (requires repeated pairings), resistant to extinction, and highly individual. It is the primary mechanism behind music's capacity to evoke intensely personal emotions that make no sense to other listeners.

**4. Emotional contagion (C)**: The listener perceives the emotional expression in the music — the "sadness" of a slow minor-mode melody, the "joy" of a fast major-mode dance — and internally mirrors that perceived emotion. This is a form of empathy applied to music: the same mirror and simulation mechanisms that allow us to feel what other humans feel are activated by musical patterns that structurally resemble emotional vocal expression (slow tempo ≈ depressed speech; fast tempo ≈ excited speech; falling contour ≈ sadness; rising contour ≈ surprise). Emotional contagion is the mechanism most responsible for the everyday experience of "this music sounds happy/sad" and the corresponding feeling.

**5. Visual imagery (V)**: Music evokes mental images — landscapes, scenes, colors, movements — that carry their own emotional content. A sweeping orchestral passage evokes vast open spaces (positive, awe-inspiring). A low, creeping chromatic line evokes darkness and threat (negative, fearful). The imagery is not arbitrary — it is grounded in cross-modal correspondences (high pitch → height/brightness; low pitch → depth/darkness; tempo → speed of motion) that appear to be partially universal and partially culturally conditioned. The emotional response is to the IMAGE, not to the sound per se; music is the trigger for an imaginative process that generates emotion indirectly.

**6. Episodic memory (E)**: Music triggers recall of specific autobiographical episodes — hearing a song and being transported back to the summer you first heard it, the person you were with, the feelings of that time. This mechanism operates through the hippocampal-cortical memory system and is the basis of music's extraordinary power as a mnemonic cue. Music is an unusually potent trigger for autobiographical memory, possibly because musical encoding involves multi-modal associations (auditory, motor, emotional, contextual) that create richly interconnected memory traces (Janata, Tomic, & Rakowski, 2007).

**7. Musical expectancy (M)**: This is the mechanism established in Panel M-I — prediction error in harmonic, melodic, and rhythmic dimensions produces emotional responses that depend on the nature and magnitude of the violation. This mechanism requires learned statistical knowledge of a musical idiom and produces emotions through the ITPRA cascade (Huron's model). Musical expectancy is the mechanism most specific to music qua music: it depends on structural knowledge of musical organization rather than on general acoustic features, conditioning, or extra-musical associations.

**8. Aesthetic judgment (A)**: The listener evaluates the music as a work of art — judging its beauty, originality, craftsmanship, expressiveness, or meaning. This is the most cognitive, most deliberate, and slowest mechanism. It involves prefrontal cortex, draws on explicit musical knowledge and aesthetic criteria, and produces emotions like admiration, awe, or intellectual satisfaction. Aesthetic judgment can override all other mechanisms: a passage that produces negative brainstem reflex (harsh sound) and negative prediction response (unexpected) can produce positive aesthetic emotion (what a brilliant compositional choice!).

**The critical insight for the panel**: These eight mechanisms operate IN PARALLEL during any musical experience. A single musical moment — say, a sudden fortissimo chord that is harmonically unexpected, that reminds you of a film scene, and that you judge to be brilliantly placed — activates brainstem reflex (B), musical expectancy (M), episodic memory (E), and aesthetic judgment (A) simultaneously. The felt emotion is the RESULTANT of all active mechanisms. This is why musical emotion is so complex and so resistant to simple theories: it is never one mechanism, always several, and the combination differs across moments, pieces, listeners, and contexts.

**The connection to the prediction error framework**: Musical expectancy (M) is only ONE of the eight mechanisms, albeit the one most specific to musical structure. The other seven mechanisms do not depend on prediction error — they are driven by acoustic features (B, R), learned associations (E-conditioning, E-memory), cross-modal simulation (V, C), or deliberate evaluation (A). A complete theory of musical emotion MUST include all eight. Panel M-I addressed mechanism 7 in depth. Panel M-II addressed mechanism 2. This panel must integrate all eight into a unified account.

### 1.2 Stefan Koelsch — The Neural Architecture of Music-Evoked Emotions

Let me provide the neural substrate map that the BRECVEMA mechanisms operate within (Koelsch, 2014; Koelsch, 2010; Koelsch, Fritz, Cramon, Müller, & Friederici, 2006).

Music-evoked emotions engage a distributed network of brain regions, many shared with non-musical emotion processing but activated by music through distinctive pathways:

**Brainstem nuclei (locus coeruleus, raphé nuclei, reticular formation)**: Process basic acoustic features — sudden onsets, extreme frequencies, dissonance. Generate arousal responses (noradrenaline, serotonin). This is Juslin's brainstem reflex mechanism (B), and it is the fastest emotional pathway: sound → brainstem → autonomic response in ~50ms, before the signal even reaches cortex. Musical sound design exploits this: the crescendo to a cymbal crash, the sudden orchestral tutti, the bass drop in electronic music.

**Amygdala**: Responds to musical stimuli that signal potential threat or biological significance — dissonance (roughness, beating between close frequencies), unexpected loud sounds, certain timbral qualities (harsh, metallic). The amygdala's role in music is specifically tied to AVERSIVE responses: it activates more strongly for dissonant than consonant intervals (Blood, Zatorre, Bermudez, & Evans, 1999), for unexpected musical events, and for music rated as fearful or threatening. Critically, amygdala activation during music is modulated by the listener's familiarity with the musical style — unfamiliar styles produce more amygdala activation, suggesting that the amygdala's response reflects UNCERTAINTY about the emotional significance of the stimulus, not just its acoustic properties.

**Hippocampus**: Two functions in musical emotion. First, the episodic memory mechanism (E-memory in BRECVEMA) — the hippocampus retrieves autobiographical memories triggered by musical cues. Second, the novelty/familiarity detection function — the hippocampus signals whether the current musical input matches stored representations (familiar) or deviates from them (novel). This novelty signal feeds into both the prediction error system (Panel M-I) and the emotional response system (novel → orienting → heightened processing). The hippocampal contribution explains why personally familiar music produces the most intense emotional responses: the memory associations amplify and personalize the emotion.

**Ventral striatum (nucleus accumbens)**: The reward center — produces the pleasure signal. As established in Panel M-I (Salimpoor et al., 2011, 2013), the NAc releases dopamine during the most pleasurable musical moments (chills, peak emotional responses). The NAc receives input from auditory cortex (via the auditory-striatal pathway documented in M-I), from the amygdala (emotional significance), and from the hippocampus (memory associations). It integrates these inputs to produce a "reward value" signal for the current musical moment. Critically, NAc activation is proportional to the listener's WILLINGNESS TO PAY for the music (Salimpoor et al., 2013) — providing an objective behavioral measure of subjective musical value.

**Insula**: Processes interoceptive signals — the bodily sensations that constitute the "feeling" of emotion. When music produces a chill (piloerection, goosebumps), the skin sensation is processed by the insula, which translates the peripheral autonomic response into a conscious feeling. The insula is thus the interface between the body's response to music and the subjective experience of musical emotion. Sachs et al. (2016) showed that insula connectivity to auditory cortex predicts chill frequency — the better the insula "listens" to the body's musical responses, the more intensely the person feels musical emotions.

**Anterior cingulate cortex (ACC)**: Monitors conflict between emotional responses. When musical expectancy produces a negative prediction response but aesthetic judgment produces a positive evaluation — the situation described by Huron as the deceptive cadence effect — the ACC detects and manages this conflict. ACC activation during music correlates with the complexity of the emotional response, suggesting it coordinates the integration of multiple simultaneous emotional mechanisms (Juslin's parallel-mechanism principle).

**Ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) and orbitofrontal cortex (OFC)**: The familiar subjective value node — appearing now for the FOURTH time in the Article Eater project. vmPFC/OFC integrates prediction outcomes, reward signals, memory associations, and aesthetic evaluations into a unified subjective valuation of the musical experience. Patients with vmPFC lesions show impaired music preference judgments despite intact music perception (Belfi, Evans, Heskje, Bruss, & Tranel, 2017). This confirms the role established in architecture (aesthetic judgment, threat suppression), education (schema congruence), and music-pitch (prediction value): vmPFC is the convergence node where domain-specific processing becomes personal value.

**The critical synthesis**: Music-evoked emotion is not a single neural circuit — it is the simultaneous activation of MULTIPLE circuits, each implementing one or more of Juslin's BRECVEMA mechanisms:

| BRECVEMA Mechanism | Primary Neural Substrate | Speed | Requires Musical Knowledge? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brainstem reflex | Brainstem nuclei | ~50ms | No |
| Rhythmic entrainment | BG + motor cortex | ~100-500ms | No |
| Evaluative conditioning | Amygdala + hippocampus | ~500ms | No |
| Emotional contagion | Mirror system + insula | ~200-500ms | Minimal |
| Visual imagery | Visual cortex + PFC | ~1-5s | Minimal |
| Episodic memory | Hippocampus + DMN | ~1-5s | No |
| Musical expectancy | Auditory cortex → IFG → NAc | ~200ms-2s | Yes (implicit) |
| Aesthetic judgment | PFC + vmPFC/OFC | ~2-10s | Yes (explicit or implicit) |

The speed and knowledge requirements determine the temporal unfolding of musical emotion: fast, knowledge-independent mechanisms (B, R) produce the initial affective response; intermediate mechanisms (C, E-conditioning) add emotional coloring; slow, knowledge-dependent mechanisms (M, A) produce the most complex and music-specific responses. A complete emotional response to a single musical passage may unfold over 10 seconds as successive mechanisms engage.

### 1.3 David Huron — ITPRA Elaborated: The Temporal Architecture of Musical Emotion

Let me expand the ITPRA model introduced in Panel M-I and connect it to Juslin's BRECVEMA framework (Huron, 2006; Huron, 2019).

In M-I, I described the five response systems — Imagination, Tension, Prediction, Reaction, Appraisal — in terms of their temporal ordering. Now I want to specify their INTERACTIONS and their connections to Juslin's mechanisms.

**Imagination (I) ↔ Musical Expectancy + Visual Imagery + Episodic Memory**: The anticipatory imagination response draws on all prediction-based and memory-based mechanisms. When a listener anticipates the return of the chorus, they are generating a prediction (M), possibly imagining the sound (V), and potentially recalling past hearings (E). The imagination response is NOT purely predictive — it recruits multiple BRECVEMA mechanisms in the service of anticipation. The emotional quality of imagination depends on WHAT is anticipated: anticipating a joyful resolution produces positive anticipatory affect; anticipating a dissonant crash produces apprehensive arousal. The imagination response can extend over seconds to minutes (during an extended buildup, a long development section, or the approach to a familiar passage in a well-known piece).

**Tension (T) ↔ Rhythmic Entrainment + Brainstem Preparation**: The preparatory tension response involves the autonomic nervous system and the motor system. As the anticipated moment approaches, the body prepares — and this preparation has a rhythmic component. The motor system's entrainment to the beat (Panel M-II) means that anticipation has a temporal specificity: the tension response peaks at the beat where the anticipated event is expected to occur. Tension is not an abstract cognitive state — it is an embodied, rhythmically structured physiological preparation. This is why anticipation in music FEELS different from anticipation in other domains — it has the pulse of the beat in it.

**Prediction (P) ↔ Musical Expectancy**: The prediction response is Juslin's mechanism 7 in its purest form. At the moment the expected event occurs (or fails to occur), the prediction machinery generates a rapid positive (confirmed) or negative (violated) response. The magnitude depends on the precision-weighted prediction error (Panel M-I, Template M2). This response is fast (~200ms) and automatic — it precedes conscious processing and is the raw affective output of the prediction error computation.

**Reaction (R) ↔ Brainstem Reflex + Emotional Contagion**: The reaction response is independent of prediction — it is the brainstem's and the emotional mirror system's response to the acoustic properties of the event itself. A sudden loud chord produces a startle reaction (B) regardless of whether it was predicted. A wailing melodic line produces an empathic sadness response (C) regardless of whether the notes were expected. The reaction response can CONFLICT with the prediction response: a predicted dissonance (positive P, because confirmed) can produce a negative R (because the dissonance is acoustically aversive). This conflict is resolved in the next stage.

**Appraisal (A) ↔ Aesthetic Judgment + Evaluative Conditioning + Episodic Memory**: The appraisal response is the slowest, most cognitive, and most personally variable. It integrates the outputs of ALL earlier stages with personal knowledge, aesthetic criteria, and memory associations. The appraisal response is where Juslin's cognitive mechanisms (A, E-conditioning, E-memory) have their primary influence. "That was unexpected [negative P], but it was a brilliant harmonic choice [positive A, aesthetic judgment] and it reminds me of my favorite Coltrane album [positive A, episodic memory]." The appraisal response can reverse the valence set by earlier stages.

**The key theoretical contribution**: The ITPRA model and the BRECVEMA framework are COMPLEMENTARY, not competing. BRECVEMA specifies the MECHANISMS (the different routes from sound to feeling). ITPRA specifies the TEMPORAL ARCHITECTURE (the sequence in which different response systems engage within each mechanism and across mechanisms). A complete model of musical emotion requires both: the mechanism taxonomy (what processes are active) AND the temporal specification (when each process contributes, and how they interact over time).

**The ITPRA × BRECVEMA matrix**: At any moment in music, each active BRECVEMA mechanism may be at a different stage of the ITPRA temporal sequence. The expectancy mechanism might be in its Tension phase (approaching an anticipated cadence) while the episodic memory mechanism is in its Appraisal phase (just having recognized the passage and retrieved a memory). The felt emotion at that moment is the vector sum of all active mechanism × stage combinations. This is the formal basis for the extraordinary complexity of musical emotion — and for why it resists reduction to any single dimension.

### 1.4 Marcel Zentner — Musical Emotions Are Not Everyday Emotions

A fundamental methodological and theoretical point: the emotions evoked by music are NOT the same as the emotions evoked by everyday life events, and we must not confuse them (Zentner, Grandjean, & Scherer, 2008; Zentner & Eerola, 2010).

When psychologists study emotion, they typically rely on the "basic emotions" framework (Ekman's anger, fear, disgust, surprise, happiness, sadness) or on the dimensional model (valence × arousal). Both frameworks were developed for UTILITARIAN emotions — emotions that arise from events relevant to the organism's survival and well-being (threat → fear; loss → sadness; goal achievement → happiness). These are emotions that typically motivate ACTION: fear motivates escape, anger motivates confrontation.

Music evokes emotions, but they differ from utilitarian emotions in several critical respects:

**1. The emotion palette is different.** When I asked large samples of listeners to report the emotions they experienced during music listening — using free description rather than forced-choice categories — the dominant responses were: wonder, transcendence, tenderness, nostalgia, peacefulness, power, joyful activation, tension, and sadness. Note what is ABSENT: anger, fear, disgust, guilt, shame. Music rarely evokes these utilitarian negative emotions. And note what is PRESENT but absent from basic-emotion taxonomies: wonder, transcendence, nostalgia, tenderness, peacefulness. These are AESTHETIC emotions — responses to beauty, meaning, and significance that have no place in the basic-emotions framework.

**2. The action tendencies are different.** Utilitarian emotions produce specific action tendencies (fear → flee; anger → fight). Musical emotions produce contemplative or appreciative responses — absorption, goosebumps, tears of moved-ness, a desire to listen more closely. The exception is groove (Panel M-II), which produces a genuine action tendency (move/dance), but even groove is better described as "pleasurable motor engagement" than as a utilitarian emotion.

**3. The relationship to valence is paradoxical.** In everyday life, sadness is negative — people avoid it. In music, sadness is frequently SOUGHT OUT and experienced as pleasurable (Vuoskoski will elaborate this). A significant fraction of people's most cherished musical experiences involve "sad" music. This is incoherent under the utilitarian framework but perfectly coherent if musical emotions are aesthetic rather than utilitarian.

The **Geneva Emotional Music Scale (GEMS)** was developed to capture this distinctive emotional palette. GEMS identifies nine factors: wonder, transcendence, tenderness, nostalgia, peacefulness, power, joyful activation, tension, and sadness. These nine factors account for substantially more variance in music-evoked emotion reports than either basic emotions or valence-arousal dimensions (Zentner et al., 2008).

**For the panel and the broader project**: This has a direct implication for the prediction error account. If musical emotions are aesthetic rather than utilitarian, then the prediction error → emotion pathway (mechanism M in BRECVEMA) does not produce "surprise" in the ordinary sense (utilitarian surprise that motivates defensive action). It produces AESTHETIC surprise — the experience of encountering something unexpected that is beautiful, interesting, or meaningful. The vmPFC, which we have identified as the value computation node across architecture, education, and music, may be specifically computing AESTHETIC value — the felt significance of a prediction outcome as a feature of artistic or intellectual experience, not as a survival-relevant signal. This connects directly to the architecture panels' discussion of the aesthetic response to spatial complexity: the same vmPFC circuitry, evaluating prediction outcomes not for survival relevance but for aesthetic significance.

### 1.5 Jonna Vuoskoski — The Paradox of Pleasurable Sadness in Music

Perhaps no phenomenon more clearly demonstrates the inadequacy of simple emotion theories than the fact that people actively seek out and deeply enjoy sad music (Vuoskoski & Eerola, 2017; Vuoskoski, Thompson, McIlwain, & Eerola, 2012; Sachs, Damasio, & Habibi, 2015).

The paradox is stark: sadness is a negative emotion. In everyday life, people avoid situations that cause sadness. Yet self-reported experiences with music consistently show that sad music is among the most valued, most sought-after, and most emotionally powerful music. How can sadness be pleasurable?

**The dissociation between perceived and felt emotion**: The first key finding is that listeners can perceive sadness in music (recognize it as "sounding sad") without necessarily FEELING sad themselves. Perceived emotion and felt emotion are dissociable (Gabrielsson, 2002). A listener can recognize that a Chopin nocturne is sad while feeling moved, contemplative, or even peaceful — not sad in the utilitarian sense. The perceived emotion is a property attributed to the music; the felt emotion is the listener's personal response, which is mediated by additional mechanisms.

**The role of absorption**: When listeners DO report feeling genuinely sad during sad music, the experience differs qualitatively from everyday sadness in a crucial way: it is typically accompanied by high absorption — a state of deep, focused attention in which the listener is immersed in the musical experience and temporarily disengaged from their everyday concerns. Absorption transforms the quality of the sadness from aversive (everyday sadness, which you want to escape) to contemplative (musical sadness, which you want to stay in). Absorption is a trait variable — individuals high in trait absorption report more intense emotional responses to music generally, and more pleasure from sad music specifically.

**The empathy pathway**: Individual differences in emotional responses to sad music correlate with trait empathy (Vuoskoski et al., 2012). Highly empathic individuals report stronger emotional responses to sad music and more pleasure from it. This suggests that the enjoyment of sad music operates partly through Juslin's emotional contagion mechanism (C): the listener empathically mirrors the perceived sadness in the music, and the empathic response itself — the act of feeling WITH the music — is experienced as meaningful and rewarding, even when the specific emotion is negative.

**The prolactin hypothesis**: Huron (2011) proposed that sad music triggers the release of prolactin, a hormone associated with consolation and comfort that is released during crying and grief. Prolactin produces a soothing, comforting feeling that counteracts the sadness, producing a mixed emotional state: sadness-with-consolation, which is experienced as bittersweet or poignant rather than purely negative. The prolactin hypothesis, while not yet conclusively confirmed, provides a neurochemical mechanism for the phenomenological observation that musical sadness feels different from real-world sadness.

**The safety factor**: Musical sadness occurs within a context of SAFETY. The listener knows (implicitly or explicitly) that the sadness is not caused by actual loss. There is no real threat, no real deprivation, no real consequence. This safety context allows the listener to experience and explore the sadness without the defensive responses that everyday sadness triggers. It is "sadness with the sting removed" — the phenomenological quality without the functional consequences.

**For the panel**: The paradox of pleasurable sadness reveals that musical emotion operates in a fundamentally different register from utilitarian emotion — confirming Zentner's point. The prediction error framework (Panel M-I) contributes to this: sad music typically has moderate harmonic complexity and moderate prediction error, falling in the Goldilocks zone for aesthetic processing. But the pleasure of sad music CANNOT be explained by prediction error alone. It requires the additional mechanisms of emotional contagion (empathy with the perceived sadness), absorption (immersive disengagement from everyday concerns), the safety of aesthetic context, and possibly neurochemical modulators (prolactin). This is precisely why Juslin's multi-mechanism framework is necessary: no single mechanism accounts for the phenomenon.

### 1.6 Robert Zatorre — Dopamine, Anticipation, and the Reward Circuit for Musical Pleasure

Let me provide the neurochemical evidence for how musical emotion engages the brain's reward system (Salimpoor, Benovoy, Larcher, Dagher, & Zatorre, 2011; Salimpoor et al., 2013; Zatorre & Salimpoor, 2013; Zatorre, 2015).

Our PET and fMRI studies have identified a two-phase dopaminergic response during musical pleasure:

**Phase 1 — Anticipation (caudate nucleus)**: In the seconds leading up to a peak emotional moment — the moment the listener identifies as producing "chills" or maximal pleasure — dopamine is released in the caudate nucleus (dorsal striatum). This anticipatory dopamine corresponds to the building of expectation: the listener's predictive model (Panel M-I) is generating increasingly confident predictions about what is about to happen, and the precision-weighted anticipation of a highly valued prediction outcome produces reward BEFORE the outcome occurs. This is the neural basis of Huron's Tension and Imagination stages: the pleasure of anticipation, the "shivers of expectation."

**Phase 2 — Consummation (nucleus accumbens)**: At the moment of peak emotion — the chill itself — dopamine is released in the nucleus accumbens (ventral striatum). This consummatory dopamine corresponds to the resolution of the anticipation: the predicted event either confirms the expectation (producing the reward of successful prediction) or violates it in a way that is resolvable and aesthetically valued (producing the reward of successful prediction error processing). This is the neural basis of Huron's Prediction and Appraisal stages.

**The critical finding**: The same two-phase dopamine pattern occurs during OTHER anticipation-reward sequences (food anticipation, monetary anticipation). Music has HIJACKED a reward circuit that evolved for survival-relevant rewards and repurposed it for aesthetic experience. The auditory-striatal pathway identified in Panel M-I (Loui's work on musical anhedonia) is the conduit: auditory prediction signals reach the striatum, which responds as if the prediction outcomes were biologically relevant — because the reward circuit does not distinguish between survival-relevant and aesthetically relevant outcomes.

**Willingness to pay**: In Salimpoor et al. (2013), we showed that the degree of activation in the NAc during musical pleasure predicts the amount of money participants are willing to pay for the music. Furthermore, the strength of functional CONNECTIVITY between auditory cortex and NAc during first-time listening predicted reward value. This means the brain computes the reward value of music in real time during first exposure, through the interaction between auditory prediction (what is happening in the music?) and reward valuation (how rewarding is this prediction outcome?). The reward signal is not based on familiarity or association — it is based on the ONLINE processing of musical structure. This is the neurochemical confirmation of the prediction error → reward pathway: structure → prediction → PE → reward signal → felt pleasure.

**For the panel**: The dopamine findings demonstrate that musical pleasure has a genuine neurochemical substrate — it is not "just" subjective report. The two-phase structure (anticipation/caudate → consummation/NAc) maps directly onto Huron's ITPRA temporal model and provides the biochemical implementation of the prediction-to-pleasure pipeline. The musical expectancy mechanism (M in BRECVEMA) produces reward through this specific dopaminergic pathway. Whether the OTHER BRECVEMA mechanisms (brainstem reflex, emotional contagion, episodic memory) engage the same dopaminergic system or use alternative affective pathways is an open question — and a critical one for this panel.

### 1.7 Tuomas Eerola — Bottom-Up Features: Acoustic Cues to Musical Emotion

I want to complement the top-down prediction account with the BOTTOM-UP evidence: specific acoustic and musical features that reliably predict emotional responses, independent of expectation and learning (Eerola & Vuoskoski, 2011; Eerola, Friberg, & Bresin, 2013; Gabrielsson & Lindström, 2010).

Large-scale empirical studies, including meta-analyses across hundreds of experiments, reveal consistent mappings between musical features and perceived/felt emotions:

**Tempo**: The most powerful single predictor of perceived valence and arousal. Fast tempo (>120 BPM) → perceived as happy, excited, energetic. Slow tempo (<76 BPM) → perceived as sad, tender, peaceful. This mapping is cross-culturally robust and appears early in development (even infants respond differentially to fast and slow tempi). The mechanism is likely a combination of rhythmic entrainment (Juslin's R — fast tempo entrains arousal upward) and emotional contagion (C — fast tempo resembles excited speech prosody).

**Mode**: Major mode → perceived as happy, bright. Minor mode → perceived as sad, dark. This is one of the most replicated findings in music emotion research, though it is more culture-dependent than tempo: listeners from cultures without the major/minor distinction do not show the mapping, suggesting it is learned rather than innate (though there is ongoing debate — see Bowling, Gill, Choi, Prinz, & Purves, 2010, for the view that major/minor mimics the spectral properties of happy vs. sad speech).

**Loudness and dynamics**: Loud → high arousal, power, anger. Soft → low arousal, tenderness, sadness. Large dynamic contrasts (crescendi, sudden fortissimi) → excitement, surprise, awe. This maps partially onto Juslin's brainstem reflex mechanism (B) for extreme loudness, and partially onto emotional contagion (C) for dynamic patterns that resemble vocal expression.

**Timbre**: Bright, sharp timbres (trumpet, distorted guitar) → high arousal, excitement or aggression. Warm, soft timbres (cello, flute, acoustic guitar) → tenderness, sadness, peacefulness. Harsh, noisy timbres → tension, anger, fear. Timbre is processed partially by the brainstem (roughness detection → aversive response) and partially by auditory cortex (spectral pattern → emotional categorization).

**Pitch height and register**: High pitch → brightness, lightness, tension. Low pitch → darkness, gravity, power. This mapping has both acoustic (high frequency → more energy to process) and cross-modal (high pitch → spatial height → openness) components.

**Melodic contour**: Rising contour → tension, excitement, questioning. Falling contour → resolution, sadness, statement. Arch contour (rise then fall) → narrative arc, completion. These mappings parallel speech prosody patterns across languages, supporting the emotional contagion mechanism (C).

**The computational approach**: My work with Friberg has attempted to build regression models that predict emotional ratings from acoustic features alone — without any knowledge of musical structure, prediction error, or learned associations. These models achieve surprisingly good prediction accuracy: typically R² = 0.60-0.80 for dimensional (valence, arousal) ratings, meaning that 60-80% of the variance in emotional response can be predicted from acoustic features alone (Eerola et al., 2013). This is a remarkably strong bottom-up signal.

**The implication for the prediction error account**: These findings do NOT contradict the prediction error framework — they COMPLEMENT it. Acoustic features provide the bottom-up emotional coloring that is INDEPENDENT of prediction. A slow, minor-mode, soft passage will be perceived as sad regardless of whether the specific notes were predicted or surprising. But the INTENSITY and SPECIFICITY of the emotional response — the difference between "this sounds vaguely sad" and "this passage breaks my heart" — depends on prediction error, personal memory, and aesthetic evaluation. The acoustic features set the baseline emotional character; the prediction error and cognitive mechanisms modulate the intensity and personal significance.

**The dimensional vs. categorical debate**: The field has not resolved whether musical emotions are best described by continuous dimensions (valence × arousal × tension) or discrete categories (Zentner's nine GEMS factors, or Juslin's basic emotions). My position is that dimensions capture the ACOUSTIC signal (features map onto valence and arousal continuously) while categories capture the LISTENER'S EXPERIENCE (which is shaped by mechanism, context, and memory). The two approaches describe different levels of the same phenomenon — and both are needed.

### 1.8 Matthew Sachs — Musical Chills: The Pinnacle of Musical Pleasure

Let me close the position statements with the phenomenon that most vividly demonstrates the power of musical emotion: the chill response — frisson, goosebumps, shivers down the spine, sometimes tears (Sachs, Ellis, Schlaug, & Loui, 2016; Sachs et al., 2015; Blood & Zatorre, 2001; Panksepp, 1995).

Musical chills are transient, intense pleasurable responses that occur during specific moments in music. They are accompanied by measurable autonomic responses: piloerection (goosebumps), changes in skin conductance, altered heart rate, and sometimes lacrimation (tears). They are the most intense moments of musical pleasure that listeners report, and they provide a window into the neural mechanisms of peak aesthetic experience.

**Who experiences chills**: Not everyone. Approximately 55-86% of listeners report experiencing musical chills (estimates vary by methodology), and the frequency varies enormously across individuals. In our structural connectivity study (Sachs et al., 2016), we found that individuals who experience frequent chills have denser white matter connectivity between the posterior superior temporal gyrus (auditory cortex), the anterior insula, and the medial prefrontal cortex, compared to individuals who rarely experience chills. The critical pathway is auditory cortex → insula → mPFC: the more efficient this pathway, the more readily auditory processing translates into interoceptive feeling (insula) and personal valuation (mPFC).

**The neural signature**: Blood and Zatorre (2001) established that musical chills produce increased activity in ventral striatum, midbrain (ventral tegmental area), thalamus, anterior cingulate cortex, and orbitofrontal cortex — the same reward network activated by food, sex, and drugs of abuse. Simultaneously, activity DECREASES in the amygdala and ventromedial prefrontal cortex during chills. The amygdala deactivation suggests that chills occur when the music has been evaluated as non-threatening (consistent with Vuoskoski's "safety" context); the vmPFC pattern is complex and may reflect a shift from evaluative processing to pure reward experience.

**What triggers chills**: Musical features associated with chill onset include: sudden dynamic increases (crescendo to fortissimo), entry of a new voice or instrument (the soprano entering above the choir), harmonic violations that resolve unexpectedly (the deceptive cadence that turns out to be more beautiful than the expected resolution), unexpected modulations, appoggiaturas and suspensions (melodic notes that clash with the harmony and then resolve). Panksepp (1995) identified that many chill triggers share the acoustic profile of a "cry" — a high-pitched, piercing sound that may activate the separation-distress system (GRIEF/PANIC circuit in Panksepp's affective neuroscience). The chill, on this account, is triggered by a simulated social signal (a musical "cry") that activates the attachment system, producing the complex mix of sadness and pleasure that characterizes many chills.

**The connection to prediction error**: Chills tend to occur at moments of high prediction error that are simultaneously high in aesthetic value — precisely the Prediction-negative × Appraisal-positive combination that Huron identified as the source of the most powerful musical emotions. But chills also involve Juslin's brainstem reflex (the sudden dynamic change), emotional contagion (the "cry" signal), and sometimes episodic memory (the passage that reminds you of a significant life event). Chills are the ultimate convergence event: multiple BRECVEMA mechanisms firing simultaneously, multiple ITPRA stages reaching their maximal intensity at the same moment, all feeding into the dopaminergic reward cascade that Zatorre has documented.

**For the panel**: Musical chills are not a curiosity — they are the EXISTENCE PROOF that music engages the brain's deepest reward and emotion circuits. The fact that a sequence of sounds, with no biological relevance, can produce an autonomic cascade indistinguishable from the body's response to biologically significant events (threat, attachment, social bonding) demands explanation. The explanation, I propose, is the convergence of multiple emotion mechanisms — brainstem reflex, emotional contagion, musical expectancy, aesthetic judgment, sometimes episodic memory — at a single temporal moment, each contributing its own affective signal, combining into a peak experience that overwhelms the system into a full-body response.

## Part 2: Cross-Examination and Debate

### 2.1 The Central Debate: How Many Mechanisms Are Really Independent?

**Eerola**: I want to challenge the independence assumption in the BRECVEMA framework. Juslin, you present eight mechanisms as though they are separate routes from sound to feeling. But in practice, they are massively correlated. The acoustic features that produce brainstem reflexes (sudden loud sounds) are the same features that violate predictions (musical expectancy) and resemble emotional vocal expressions (emotional contagion). When a crescendo builds to a crashing fortissimo chord that is harmonically unexpected, is the emotional response driven by B (loud crash), M (unexpected harmony), C (dramatic vocal-like expression), or A (aesthetic admiration of the compositional choice)? You cannot separate them empirically because the musical features that activate one mechanism typically activate several.

**Juslin**: That is precisely the point. I do not claim the mechanisms are empirically separable in every musical moment. I claim they are CONCEPTUALLY distinct — different evolutionary origins, different neural substrates, different developmental trajectories. The brainstem reflex exists in organisms with no cortex; aesthetic judgment requires prefrontal cortex that is fully mature only in human adulthood. They CAN be dissociated experimentally: a sudden loud white noise burst activates B but not M (there is no musical prediction to violate); a very soft, expected chord change in a familiar piece can activate M but not B (there is prediction but no acoustic startle). The mechanisms are dissociable IN PRINCIPLE, even when they co-occur in rich musical contexts.

**Koelsch**: The neural evidence supports Juslin's dissociability claim. The brainstem response to acoustic features (50ms, subcortical) is temporally and anatomically separable from the harmonic prediction error response (200ms, IFG/auditory cortex) and from the reward response (seconds, NAc/vmPFC). Different mechanisms → different latencies → different brain regions. They converge in the felt experience, but the convergence happens AFTER distinct initial processing stages.

**Huron**: I would add that the ITPRA model provides a temporal dissociation test. The Reaction (R) response in ITPRA corresponds to mechanisms that respond to the acoustic properties of the event itself (Juslin's B, C, and partly R-entrainment). The Prediction (P) response corresponds to musical expectancy (M). The Appraisal (A) response corresponds to aesthetic judgment (A) and memory-based mechanisms (E-conditioning, E-memory). These occur at different times after the musical event — so even when the same event activates multiple mechanisms, their contributions can be tracked through the temporal unfolding of the physiological and neural response.

### 2.2 Prediction Error vs. Acoustic Features: Which Drives Musical Emotion?

**Eerola**: My regression models explain 60-80% of emotional variance from acoustic features alone. If prediction error were the primary driver of musical emotion, these bottom-up models should fail — because the same acoustic features can produce different prediction errors depending on context. Yet they succeed. This suggests that the bottom-up signal is more powerful than the top-down prediction signal.

**Zatorre**: But your models predict PERCEIVED emotion (what does this music sound like?) much better than FELT emotion (what does this music make you feel?). The gap between perceived and felt emotion — Vuoskoski's dissociation — is precisely where prediction error operates. A slow minor-mode passage always SOUNDS sad (high perceived sadness, well predicted by acoustic features). But whether it MAKES YOU FEEL sad, moved, contemplative, or nothing at all depends on prediction error, personal memory, absorption, and aesthetic evaluation — all factors that acoustic features alone cannot capture.

**Vuoskoski**: Exactly. The between-listener variance in felt emotion is ENORMOUS — much larger than the between-listener variance in perceived emotion. Most listeners agree that a passage sounds sad. But their felt responses range from intense moved-ness to pleasant contemplation to indifference to genuine distress. The acoustic features predict the consensus (what most people hear); the listener-specific mechanisms (expectancy, memory, empathy, absorption) predict the individual response.

**Eerola**: Fair point. I would propose a two-stage model: (1) Acoustic features produce a RAPID, DEFAULT emotional categorization (the "this sounds sad" response), operating through brainstem reflex, entrainment, and contagion mechanisms. (2) Prediction error, memory, and aesthetic evaluation MODULATE this default, amplifying or attenuating the felt emotion and adding personal specificity. Stage 1 accounts for the success of my regression models; Stage 2 accounts for the individual variation that my models miss.

**Juslin**: I accept that synthesis. It maps cleanly onto BRECVEMA: mechanisms B, R, and C operate bottom-up and produce the acoustic-feature-driven default. Mechanisms M, A, E-conditioning, and E-memory operate top-down and produce the individual modulation. V (visual imagery) sits in between — partly triggered by acoustic features (cross-modal correspondences) and partly shaped by individual imagination.

### 2.3 The Paradox of Pleasurable Negative Emotions: Competing Accounts

**Vuoskoski**: I presented the absorption and empathy account of pleasurable sadness. But there are competing theories, and I want us to adjudicate.

**Huron**: My account is the ITPRA conflict model. Sad music produces a negative Prediction response (the melodic descent, the minor mode, the slow tempo — all produce a mild aversive response because they resemble indicators of negative biological states). But the Appraisal response is positive: "I am safe, this is art, this is beautiful, the composer achieved something extraordinary." The net emotion is determined by the relative magnitudes of P (negative) and A (positive). When A > P, the experience is pleasurable despite the sad coloring. The prolactin hypothesis provides a mechanism: the negative P triggers a consolatory hormonal response that produces the "bittersweet" quality.

**Sachs**: My neural data add another layer. During sad music that is experienced as pleasurable, we see co-activation of the reward network (NAc, vmPFC) AND the sadness/grief network (ACC, anterior insula). These are not competing — they are simultaneously active. The brain is literally experiencing reward and sadness at the same time. This is not possible in utilitarian emotion, where sadness and pleasure are opposing states. It is possible in aesthetic emotion because the safety context removes the action-relevant component of sadness, leaving only the experiential/interoceptive component, which can co-exist with reward.

**Zentner**: And this is why GEMS identifies "sadness" and "transcendence" as separate factors that can co-occur. Listeners can report high scores on both simultaneously — something incoherent under the basic-emotions framework but perfectly natural in aesthetic experience. The moved-ness or poignancy of great sad music is not "sadness" or "pleasure" — it is a COMPOUND aesthetic emotion that has no utilitarian equivalent.

**Koelsch**: The neural anatomy supports compound emotions. The insula processes the bodily sensation of being moved (tears forming, throat tightening). vmPFC processes the aesthetic value (this is beautiful). ACC processes the conflict (sadness + pleasure). These three regions are co-active during peak musical experiences involving nominally "negative" emotions. The compound emotion is an emergent property of simultaneous activation in circuits that normally do not co-activate during utilitarian emotional processing.

### 2.4 The vmPFC Convergence: Aesthetic Value Across Domains

**Zatorre**: I want to explicitly flag the cross-domain pattern. vmPFC appears as the value computation node in EVERY domain the Article Eater project has examined:

| Domain | vmPFC Function | Key Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Architecture | Aesthetic preference, threat suppression | Preferred spatial complexity activates vmPFC |
| Education | Schema congruence detection | vmPFC mediates encoding of schema-congruent information |
| Music: Pitch (M-I) | Prediction value integration | vmPFC activation correlates with musical preference |
| Music: Rhythm (M-II) | Groove value | vmPFC active during pleasurable groove |
| Music: Emotion (M-III) | Aesthetic emotion integration | vmPFC integrates prediction, reward, memory, and aesthetic judgment |

Is this the same computation, or different computations that happen to share a brain region?

**Koelsch**: I believe it is the SAME computation at an abstract level: vmPFC computes the SUBJECTIVE VALUE of the current experience by integrating (1) prediction outcomes from domain-specific cortices, (2) reward signals from the striatum, (3) memory-based associations from the hippocampus, and (4) self-relevant meaning from the DMN. What changes across domains is the INPUT — spatial patterns in architecture, conceptual schemas in education, auditory sequences in music. The COMPUTATION — integrating multiple signals into a single value estimate — is domain-general.

**Huron**: This is the architectural bottleneck from the earlier panels. Every prediction system in the brain — visual, auditory, conceptual — feeds its prediction error signals into a common value computation node (vmPFC). This node answers the question that matters to the organism: "Is this experience, on balance, good or bad for me?" In the aesthetic domain, "good for me" does not mean survival-relevant — it means meaningful, beautiful, interesting, moving. vmPFC does not distinguish between survival value and aesthetic value at the computational level. This is WHY music can hijack reward circuits: the value computation treats aesthetic prediction outcomes with the same machinery it applies to biological ones.

**Sachs**: And the individual differences in auditory cortex → insula → mPFC connectivity that predict chill frequency may reflect individual variation in the EFFICIENCY of this value pipeline. Some brains compute aesthetic value from auditory input more rapidly and more intensely, because the pathway from auditory processing to interoceptive feeling to subjective valuation is more densely connected. Musical sensitivity is, at least partly, a connectivity phenotype.

## Part 3: Templates — Musical Emotion Mechanisms

### Template M12: BRECVEMA Multi-Mechanism Framework

**Core claim**: Music evokes emotion through at least eight distinct psychological mechanisms that operate in parallel, each with different evolutionary origins, neural substrates, developmental trajectories, speed of activation, and dependence on musical knowledge. The felt emotion at any moment is the resultant of all currently active mechanisms.

**Mechanisms and substrates**:
- B (Brainstem reflex): Brainstem nuclei → autonomic response. Speed: ~50ms. Knowledge required: None.
- R (Rhythmic entrainment): BG + motor cortex → autonomic and motor coupling. Speed: ~100-500ms. Knowledge: None.
- E (Evaluative conditioning): Amygdala + hippocampus → conditioned association. Speed: ~500ms. Knowledge: None (experience-dependent).
- C (Emotional contagion): Mirror system + insula → empathic mirroring. Speed: ~200-500ms. Knowledge: Minimal.
- V (Visual imagery): Visual cortex + PFC → imagery-mediated emotion. Speed: ~1-5s. Knowledge: Minimal.
- E (Episodic memory): Hippocampus + DMN → memory-triggered emotion. Speed: ~1-5s. Knowledge: None (experience-dependent).
- M (Musical expectancy): Auditory cortex → IFG → striatum → PE-mediated emotion. Speed: ~200ms-2s. Knowledge: Yes (implicit statistical learning).
- A (Aesthetic judgment): PFC + vmPFC/OFC → evaluative emotion. Speed: ~2-10s. Knowledge: Yes (implicit or explicit).

**Formal structure**:
- Emotion(t) = Σ_i w_i(t) × mechanism_i(t), where i ∈ {B, R, E_c, C, V, E_m, M, A}
- w_i(t) = weighting of mechanism i at time t (depends on listener, context, and musical features)
- Mechanisms activate at different latencies and may produce same-signed or opposite-signed affective signals
- Compound emotions arise when mechanisms produce conflicting valence signals simultaneously

**Key insight**: Musical emotion is irreducibly multi-mechanism. Single-mechanism theories (emotion = prediction error; emotion = acoustic features; emotion = memory) each capture one pathway and miss the rest. The BRECVEMA framework integrates them.

**Maturity**: ★★★ (Comprehensive taxonomy with strong face validity; empirical isolation of individual mechanisms still limited; formal model of mechanism interaction not yet specified)

**Key references**: Juslin (2013); Juslin & Västfjäll (2008); Juslin et al. (2010)

### Template M13: Neural Architecture of Music-Evoked Emotion

**Core claim**: Music-evoked emotions engage a distributed network of brain structures — brainstem, amygdala, hippocampus, ventral striatum, insula, ACC, vmPFC/OFC — each processing a different aspect of the emotional response, operating at different timescales, and corresponding to different BRECVEMA mechanisms.

**Neural substrate (processing hierarchy)**:
1. Brainstem nuclei (LC, raphé, reticular formation): Acoustic reflex, arousal modulation (~50ms)
2. Amygdala: Threat/significance detection, aversive responses to dissonance and unfamiliarity (~100-200ms)
3. Hippocampus: Episodic memory retrieval, novelty/familiarity detection (~500ms-2s)
4. Ventral striatum (NAc): Reward signal, dopamine release during pleasure (~1-5s)
5. Insula: Interoceptive processing, bodily feeling of emotion (~500ms-2s)
6. ACC: Conflict monitoring between competing emotional signals (~1-5s)
7. vmPFC/OFC: Subjective value integration, aesthetic evaluation (~2-10s)

**Formal structure**:
- Fast pathway (subcortical): Sound → brainstem → amygdala → autonomic response (B, R mechanisms)
- Intermediate pathway: Sound → auditory cortex → IFG → hippocampus + striatum (M, E mechanisms)
- Slow pathway (cortical): Sound → auditory cortex → PFC → vmPFC/OFC (A mechanism)
- Integration: vmPFC receives convergent input from all pathways and computes unified subjective value
- Emotional experience = vmPFC value signal + insula interoceptive signal + ACC conflict signal

**Clinical dissociations**:
- vmPFC lesion: Intact music perception, impaired music preference (Belfi et al., 2017)
- Musical anhedonia: Intact prediction (ERAN normal), absent reward (NAc disconnected) (Mas-Herrero et al., 2014)
- Amygdala lesion: Impaired recognition of "scary" music, preserved recognition of "happy" and "sad" (Gosselin et al., 2005)

**Maturity**: ★★★★ (Strong neuroimaging, lesion, and pharmacological evidence; the full temporal hierarchy needs more direct testing)

**Key references**: Koelsch (2014); Blood & Zatorre (2001); Salimpoor et al. (2011, 2013); Belfi et al. (2017)

### Template M14: Aesthetic Emotions vs. Utilitarian Emotions

**Core claim**: Music-evoked emotions constitute a distinctive class — aesthetic emotions — that differ from utilitarian (everyday survival-relevant) emotions in their phenomenological palette, action tendencies, relationship to valence, and neural dynamics. Standard emotion frameworks (basic emotions, valence-arousal) are insufficient for capturing musical emotional experience.

**Distinguishing features**:
- Emotion palette: Aesthetic emotions include wonder, transcendence, tenderness, nostalgia, peacefulness (absent from basic-emotion taxonomies). Utilitarian emotions like anger, fear, disgust are rarely evoked by music.
- Action tendencies: Utilitarian emotions motivate specific actions (flee, fight, withdraw). Aesthetic emotions motivate contemplation, absorption, continued engagement.
- Valence paradox: Nominally "negative" aesthetic emotions (sadness, tension, melancholy) are frequently experienced as pleasurable. Utilitarian sadness is aversive.
- Neural dynamics: Aesthetic emotions involve simultaneous co-activation of reward and negative-affect circuits — a pattern not seen in utilitarian emotion processing.

**Formal structure**:
- Aesthetic emotion = f(acoustic_features, prediction_error, personal_significance, safety_context)
- Safety context: The implicit knowledge that the emotion source is art (no real threat, loss, or consequence) → reduces action tendency → permits exploration of negative emotional states
- Compound emotion: vmPFC(value) + insula(feeling) + ACC(conflict) → aesthetic emotions are frequently compounds (sadness + beauty → poignancy; surprise + admiration → awe; tension + resolution → catharsis)

**Measurement**: Geneva Emotional Music Scale (GEMS) — 9 factors: wonder, transcendence, tenderness, nostalgia, peacefulness, power, joyful activation, tension, sadness. Explains more variance in music-evoked emotion reports than basic emotions or dimensional models.

**Maturity**: ★★★ (Strong psychometric evidence for GEMS; theoretical framework emerging but not yet formalized; cross-cultural validation needed)

**Key references**: Zentner et al. (2008); Zentner & Eerola (2010); Juslin (2013); Menninghaus et al. (2015)

### Template M15: The Paradox of Pleasurable Negative Emotions in Music

**Core claim**: Listeners actively seek out and derive pleasure from music that evokes nominally negative emotions (especially sadness), through a combination of mechanisms: (1) dissociation between perceived and felt emotion; (2) absorption that transforms aversive sadness into contemplative sadness; (3) empathic engagement (contagion mechanism) experienced as meaningful; (4) safety of aesthetic context; (5) possible neurochemical modulation (prolactin); and (6) positive aesthetic appraisal that overrides negative prediction/reaction responses.

**Neural substrate**:
- Co-activation of reward network (NAc, vmPFC) and negative-affect network (ACC, anterior insula) during pleasurable sad music
- Amygdala deactivation (safety context: no real threat)
- Hippocampal activation (memory associations amplify emotional response)
- Possible prolactin release (consolation response to simulated grief signal)

**Formal structure**:
- Felt_valence = Σ [w_brainstem(negative) + w_contagion(negative) + w_prediction(variable) + w_appraisal(positive) + w_memory(variable) + w_absorption(positive_modulator)]
- When w_appraisal + w_absorption > |w_contagion + w_brainstem|, net experience is pleasurable despite negative emotional content
- Individual differences: High trait empathy → stronger w_contagion; high trait absorption → stronger w_absorption; high openness → stronger w_appraisal
- The paradox resolves: "pleasurable sadness" is not contradictory because aesthetic and utilitarian emotional systems are partially independent

**Key individual difference predictors**: Trait empathy, trait absorption, openness to experience, musical training. These predict the MAGNITUDE of pleasure derived from sad music.

**Maturity**: ★★★ (Multiple converging lines of evidence; the prolactin hypothesis still unconfirmed; formal model of mechanism interaction untested)

**Key references**: Vuoskoski & Eerola (2017); Vuoskoski et al. (2012); Sachs et al. (2015); Huron (2011); Eerola et al. (2018)

### Template M16: Musical Chills — Convergent Peak Emotional Experience

**Core claim**: Musical chills (frisson) represent moments of maximal convergence across multiple emotion mechanisms, producing autonomic cascades (piloerection, skin conductance spikes, tears) mediated by the auditory → insula → striatum → vmPFC reward pathway. Individual differences in chill frequency are predicted by structural connectivity along this pathway.

**Neural substrate**:
- Auditory cortex → posterior insula (interoceptive relay) → anterior insula (conscious bodily feeling)
- Auditory cortex → ventral striatum (dopaminergic reward) → vmPFC (subjective value)

- During chills: Increased activity in NAc, midbrain (VTA), thalamus, ACC, OFC; decreased activity in amygdala

**Formal structure**:
- Chill probability ∝ Π mechanism_intensity_i for all simultaneously active mechanisms
- Multiplicative (not additive): chills require CONVERGENCE — multiple mechanisms at high intensity simultaneously
- Chill trigger features: crescendo (B), new voice entry (B + C), harmonic violation with beautiful resolution (M + A), familiar emotionally significant passage (E_memory), "cry" acoustic profile (C)
- Anticipatory dopamine (caudate) builds during approach; consummatory dopamine (NAc) fires at chill moment
- Chill ≈ system reaching saturation point → autonomic overflow (piloerection, tears)

**The two-phase dopamine model**:
- Phase 1 (anticipation): Caudate dopamine ∝ precision × entropy (Huron's Tension + Imagination)
- Phase 2 (consummation): NAc dopamine ∝ |PE| × resolvability × aesthetic_value (Huron's Prediction + Appraisal)
- Chill occurs when Phase 1 + Phase 2 dopamine exceeds individual threshold

**Maturity**: ★★★★ (Strong PET, fMRI, and connectivity evidence; autonomic correlates well-documented; multiplicative convergence model is conceptual, not yet formally tested)

**Key references**: Sachs et al. (2016); Blood & Zatorre (2001); Salimpoor et al. (2011, 2013); Panksepp (1995)

### Template M17: Acoustic Feature → Emotion Mapping (Bottom-Up Pathway)

**Core claim**: Specific acoustic and musical features reliably predict emotional responses (both perceived and felt) through bottom-up pathways that are partially independent of prediction error, personal memory, and aesthetic evaluation. These features produce a rapid, DEFAULT emotional characterization that is then modulated by top-down mechanisms.

**Feature-emotion mappings (cross-culturally robust)**:
- Tempo: Fast → happy/excited/energetic; Slow → sad/tender/peaceful
- Mode: Major → happy/bright; Minor → sad/dark (partially culture-dependent)
- Loudness: Loud → power/arousal/anger; Soft → tenderness/sadness/intimacy
- Timbre: Bright/sharp → excitement/aggression; Warm/soft → tenderness/sadness
- Register: High → brightness/tension; Low → gravity/power/darkness
- Contour: Rising → tension/excitement; Falling → resolution/sadness; Arch → completion
- Dynamics: Large contrasts → excitement/surprise/awe

**Neural substrate**:
- Brainstem: Processes loudness, onset rapidity, roughness (dissonance) → arousal modulation
- Auditory cortex: Extracts tempo, mode, timbre, contour → emotional categorization
- Motor cortex: Entrains to tempo → arousal coupling
- Mirror/simulation system: Matches acoustic patterns to vocal expression templates → contagion

**Formal structure**:
- Perceived_emotion = β₁(tempo) + β₂(mode) + β₃(loudness) + β₄(timbre) + β₅(register) + β₆(contour) + ε

- Stage 1 (bottom-up, fast): Acoustic features → BRECVEMA mechanisms B, R, C → default emotional characterization
- Stage 2 (top-down, slow): Prediction error (M), memory (E), aesthetic judgment (A) → individual modulation
- Stage 1 explains between-piece variance (why sad music sounds different from happy music)
- Stage 2 explains between-listener variance (why the same piece moves one listener and not another)

**Maturity**: ★★★★ (Large empirical literature; regression models well-validated; the Stage 1/Stage 2 distinction is theoretically motivated but not yet formally validated as a process model)

## Part 4: Panel Synthesis — How Music Becomes Emotion

### The Unified Account

The eight panelists converge on a multi-mechanism, multi-stage account of musical emotion that substantially extends the prediction error framework of Panels M-I and M-II:

**Musical emotion is irreducibly multi-mechanism** (Juslin, Eerola). No single process — not prediction error, not acoustic feature processing, not memory, not aesthetic evaluation — accounts for the full range of emotional responses to music. The BRECVEMA framework identifies eight distinct mechanisms, each with different neural substrates, timescales, and knowledge requirements. At any given moment during music listening, several mechanisms are simultaneously active, and the felt emotion is the resultant of their combined signals. Single-mechanism theories of musical emotion are necessarily incomplete.

**The mechanisms operate within a hierarchical neural architecture** (Koelsch, Zatorre). The emotional processing of music engages a distributed network from brainstem to prefrontal cortex, with each level contributing a different aspect of the emotional response. Fast, subcortical mechanisms (brainstem reflex, entrainment) provide the initial affective coloring within the first 100ms. Intermediate mechanisms (emotional contagion, memory retrieval, prediction error) add complexity over the next 200ms-2s. Slow cortical mechanisms (aesthetic judgment, subjective valuation) produce the final evaluative response over 2-10s. The temporal unfolding of musical emotion is not a single event but a cascade, with successive mechanisms contributing at successive timescales.

**Prediction error is ONE mechanism among several, but it is the most music-specific** (Huron, Koelsch). The musical expectancy mechanism (M in BRECVEMA) depends on learned statistical knowledge of a musical idiom and produces emotions through the ITPRA temporal cascade. It is the mechanism that distinguishes musical emotion from other forms of auditory emotion (speech prosody, environmental sounds). But it accounts for only a fraction of the total emotional variance — the fraction that depends on structural knowledge. The other mechanisms, especially acoustic feature mapping (B, R, C) and personal associations (E-conditioning, E-memory), contribute the rest.

**Musical emotions constitute a distinctive class — aesthetic emotions** (Zentner, Vuoskoski). The emotional palette of music (wonder, transcendence, tenderness, nostalgia, poignancy, power, joyful activation) overlaps imperfectly with everyday utilitarian emotions. Most critically, music can evoke nominally negative emotions (sadness, tension, melancholy) that are experienced as pleasurable — a paradox under utilitarian emotion theories but coherent within the aesthetic emotion framework. The paradox of pleasurable sadness is resolved by the combination of dissociated emotion (perceiving vs. feeling), absorption (transforming aversive into contemplative experience), empathic engagement (meaningful mirroring of perceived expression), the safety of aesthetic context, and positive aesthetic appraisal that overrides negative affective signals.

**Musical chills reveal the convergence architecture** (Sachs, Zatorre). Peak musical experiences (frisson, goosebumps, tears) occur when multiple BRECVEMA mechanisms converge at a single temporal moment — a sudden dynamic increase (B) at an unexpected harmonic juncture (M) in a passage with powerful emotional expression (C) that reminds the listener of a significant personal experience (E-memory) and is judged to be aesthetically magnificent (A). The multiplicative convergence of mechanisms produces a reward signal that exceeds the threshold for autonomic overflow, producing the full-body chill response. The two-phase dopaminergic mechanism (anticipatory caudate → consummatory NAc) provides the neurochemical substrate. Individual differences in the structural connectivity of the auditory → insula → mPFC pathway determine who experiences these peak states and how frequently.

**vmPFC is confirmed for the fifth time as the subjective value node** (Koelsch, Zatorre, Sachs). Across architecture (aesthetic preference for spatial complexity), education (schema congruence evaluation), music-pitch (prediction value), music-rhythm (groove value), and now music-emotion (aesthetic emotion integration), vmPFC consistently appears as the convergence point where domain-specific processing is integrated with reward, memory, and self-relevant meaning to produce subjective value. The computation is domain-general: integrate prediction outcomes + reward signals + memory associations + self-relevant meaning → subjective value of current experience. The inputs are domain-specific. This is the Article Eater project's most robust cross-domain finding.

### The Cross-Domain Consolidation: Emotion as Prediction Value

| Mechanism | Architecture | Education | Music: Pitch (M-I) | Music: Rhythm (M-II) | Music: Emotion (M-III) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Prediction type | Spatial patterns | Conceptual schemas | Harmonic/melodic | Temporal/metric | Multi-mechanism integration |
| Emotional response | Aesthetic preference, awe/unease | Curiosity, satisfaction, frustration | Surprise, pleasure, tension | Groove, energy, release | Full aesthetic emotion palette |
| Goldilocks zone | D ≈ 1.3 | Desirable difficulty | Moderate IC | Moderate syncopation | Moderate multi-mechanism activation |
| Value computation | vmPFC | vmPFC | vmPFC | vmPFC | vmPFC |
| Negative → positive | Complexity → beauty | Difficulty → mastery | Surprise → awe | Syncopation → groove | Sadness → poignancy |

The overarching principle emerging across domains: **moderate prediction error, processed in a safe evaluative context, produces positive aesthetic/intellectual value**. The specific mechanisms that generate and process the prediction error differ across domains, but the value computation converges on the same node (vmPFC), and the optimal zone follows the same inverted-U function. Musical emotion is the richest instantiation of this principle because it activates the most mechanisms simultaneously — but it is the same principle operating in architecture (spatial predictions that produce aesthetic responses), education (conceptual predictions that produce curiosity and satisfaction), and now music.

### 5 Testable Predictions

1. **BRECVEMA mechanisms will have dissociable neural signatures in time-resolved fMRI/EEG**: A paradigm that manipulates acoustic features (loud vs. soft), harmonic predictability (expected vs. unexpected), and personal familiarity (known vs. unknown piece) should produce temporally separable neural responses: brainstem reflex at ~50-100ms (brainstem BOLD), prediction error at ~200ms (IFG/auditory cortex BOLD), memory retrieval at ~500ms-1s (hippocampal BOLD), and aesthetic evaluation at ~2-5s (vmPFC BOLD). The temporal ordering should follow the BRECVEMA speed hierarchy. *Status: Predicted; individual mechanisms have been neuroimaged separately, but the full temporal cascade within a single paradigm has not been tested.*

2. **Chill probability will be predicted by a multiplicative (not additive) model of mechanism convergence**: The probability of a chill at a given musical moment should be better fit by a product of mechanism intensities (B × M × C × A) than by a sum (B + M + C + A). This tests the convergence hypothesis: chills require multiple mechanisms at high intensity simultaneously, not one mechanism at very high intensity. *Status: Predicted; no formal test of multiplicative vs. additive models of chill occurrence.*

3. **Pleasurable sadness in music will be modulated by oxytocin or prolactin**: Pharmacological manipulation of prolactin (or oxytocin, which is related to consolation and social bonding) should increase the pleasure derived from sad music without affecting the pleasure of happy music. Conversely, blocking these hormones should reduce the specific pleasure of sad music while leaving other musical pleasures intact. *Status: Not yet tested; pharmacological studies of musical emotion are rare.*

4. **GEMS emotion factors will map onto distinct BRECVEMA mechanism profiles**: Wonder and transcendence should be associated primarily with aesthetic judgment (A) and musical expectancy (M). Nostalgia should be associated primarily with episodic memory (E-memory). Joyful activation should be associated primarily with rhythmic entrainment (R) and brainstem reflex (B). Each GEMS factor should have a characteristic mechanism signature. *Status: Predicted; no study has jointly measured GEMS factors and BRECVEMA mechanism contributions.*

5. **Auditory → insula → vmPFC white matter connectivity will predict aesthetic emotion intensity across domains, not only music**: If the auditory → insula → vmPFC pathway mediates aesthetic value computation generally (not just for music), then individuals with denser connectivity along this pathway should show stronger aesthetic responses to architecture, visual art, and poetry as well as music. Musical chill frequency should correlate with aesthetic sensitivity in other domains. *Status: Sachs et al. (2016) established the music finding; cross-domain generalization not yet tested.*
