## Panel Charge

This panel addresses the gap identified in Document 33 for **A3 Spatial Configuration & Layout** (coverage ★★ Partial). The gap analysis found that while T3 (cognitive mapping) and T14 (navigational stress) provide a foundation, the formal mathematical framework of **Space Syntax** — the most developed quantitative tool for analyzing spatial configuration — has no representation in the template library. And the connection between spatial configuration and social encounter — a central concern of architectural design — is touched on by the Social Brain templates (T48–T52) but not connected to spatial metrics.

This panel is charged with producing 4–6 mechanistic templates that formalize: (1) how spatial configuration determines cognitive map formation and navigational prediction, (2) how Space Syntax metrics operationalize spatial prediction and prediction error, (3) how the architectural promenade orchestrates multi-modal PE over time, (4) how spatial configuration shapes social encounter and co-presence, (5) how visual access (isovists, visual graph analysis) determines environmental monitoring and threat assessment, and (6) how transitions between spaces produce cross-modal PE events. These templates must connect to the existing infrastructure — particularly T3 (cognitive map), T5 (threat/safety), T14 (navigational stress), T25 (directed attention), AX3 (awe), AX6 (multi-modal PE integration), and the newly produced L1–L5 and MAT1–MAT5 templates.

Additionally, this panel must address the **light-space interaction** flagged by Panel L-I: spatial configuration determines how daylight enters and distributes through a building (room depth, orientation, window placement, atrium design), so L-templates operate on a luminous environment that spatial configuration creates. And the **material-space interaction** from Panel MAT-I: the architectural promenade involves transitions between material zones (stone corridor → wood-paneled library → glass atrium), and the cross-modal PE at each transition depends on both the spatial sequence and the material change.

## Panel Composition

### Space Syntax and Spatial Network Analysis

**1. Bill Hillier** (University College London, Bartlett School of Architecture; founder, Space Syntax Laboratory)
Creator of Space Syntax — the mathematical and computational framework for analyzing spatial configuration based on graph-theoretic properties of spatial networks. His foundational work demonstrated that spatial configuration alone (independent of signage, landmarks, or building aesthetics) predicts pedestrian movement patterns, social encounter rates, and wayfinding success (Hillier & Hanson, 1984, ~5,000 citations; Hillier, 1996, ~3,000 citations). Space Syntax metrics — integration (how connected a space is to all other spaces), connectivity (how many spaces are directly accessible), depth (how many topological steps separate two spaces), and intelligibility (how well local properties predict global structure) — are direct operationalizations of spatial prediction, though Hillier did not use that framework.
*Role*: Establishes the formal mathematics of spatial configuration and demonstrates that these mathematics predict human behavior. Provides the bridge from architectural layout to cognitive prediction: integration IS a measure of how predictable a spatial network is; depth IS a measure of navigational prediction error; intelligibility IS a measure of how well local information supports global spatial inference.

**2. Alan Penn** (University College London, Bartlett School of Architecture; Director, Space Syntax Laboratory)
Extended Space Syntax into computational analysis and visual graph analysis (VGA). His work connects architectural space to visibility — what you can see from any point in a plan — and demonstrates that visual access patterns predict movement, social encounter, and spatial cognition. Co-developer with Alasdair Turner of visibility graph analysis tools that decompose architectural plans into isovist fields (the total visible area from any point) and analyze their graph-theoretic properties.
*Role*: Provides the visual-access dimension of spatial configuration. Isovists are directly relevant to the PE framework: the isovist at any point defines the RANGE of the visual prediction — what the brain can predict from where it currently is. Small isovists (enclosed, occluded) → narrow prediction range → high uncertainty about what lies beyond. Large isovists (open, panoramic) → wide prediction range → low uncertainty. Transitions between small and large isovists are PE events.

**3. Ruth Dalton** (Northumbria University, Department of Architecture and Built Environment)
Research on spatial cognition in buildings, wayfinding behavior, and the cognitive processing of spatial configuration. Her work connects Space Syntax metrics to cognitive science — demonstrating, for example, that people's mental maps of buildings correlate with Space Syntax integration values rather than with metric distance, and that spatial decisions at choice points are influenced by the visual connectivity of the options (Dalton, 2003, ~300 citations; Dalton & Bafna, 2003, ~200 citations).
*Role*: Bridges between Space Syntax (a mathematical description of space) and cognitive science (how the brain represents and navigates space). Provides evidence that spatial cognition operates on topological and configurational properties, not just Euclidean geometry — which is why Space Syntax predicts behavior better than simple distance metrics.

### Spatial Cognition and Navigation Neuroscience

**4. John O'Keefe** (University College London, Sainsbury Wellcome Centre; Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 2014)
Discoverer of place cells in the hippocampus — neurons that fire when an animal is in a specific location, forming the neural substrate of the cognitive map (O'Keefe & Dostrovsky, 1971, ~5,000 citations; O'Keefe & Nadel, 1978, ~10,000 citations). His work established that the hippocampus constructs an allocentric (world-centered) spatial representation — a map of the environment — that supports navigation, memory, and prediction. The Nobel Prize recognized this as one of the foundational discoveries in cognitive neuroscience.
*Role*: Establishes the neural substrate that spatial configuration acts upon. Place cells are the mechanism by which the brain encodes "where am I?" — and architectural layout determines what pattern of place cell activation a given building produces. Complex, irregular layouts produce richer place cell patterns (more distinct spatial representations); repetitive, undifferentiated layouts produce impoverished patterns (similar place cell firing across different locations → spatial confusion).

**5. May-Britt Moser and Edvard Moser** (Norwegian University of Science and Technology, Kavli Institute for Systems Neuroscience; Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 2014)
Discoverers of grid cells, border cells, and head-direction cells in the entorhinal cortex — the neural coordinate system that supports the hippocampal cognitive map (Hafting, Fyhn, Molden, Moser, & Moser, 2005, ~3,000 citations). Grid cells fire in a regular hexagonal lattice pattern as the animal moves through space, providing a metric for self-motion integration (path integration). Border cells fire when the animal is near an environmental boundary (wall, edge). Head-direction cells encode facing direction.
*Role*: Provide the neural coordinate system that architectural boundaries, corridors, and spatial volumes activate. Walls are border-cell activators. Room proportions affect the spacing and regularity of grid cell firing. Corridors constrain head-direction cell dynamics to a limited range. The Mosers' work establishes that the brain's spatial representation is not a passive recording but an active prediction system: grid cells predict where the animal SHOULD be based on path integration, and mismatches between predicted and actual location produce spatial prediction error that drives map updating.

**6. Christoph Hölscher** (ETH Zürich, Chair of Cognitive Science; formerly University of Freiburg)
Leading researcher on wayfinding in complex buildings. His work specifically addresses HOW people navigate multi-level, non-trivial architectural environments — hospitals, airports, university buildings — and what spatial properties make buildings easy or difficult to navigate (Hölscher, Meilinger, Vrachliotis, Brösamle, & Knauff, 2006, ~300 citations; Hölscher, Brösamle, & Vrachliotis, 2012, ~200 citations). Has connected wayfinding difficulty to specific spatial features: floor plan complexity, vertical circulation legibility, sightline availability, and landmark distribution.
*Role*: Provides the applied spatial cognition perspective — what actually makes buildings hard to navigate, and why. Bridges between the formal mathematics of Space Syntax (Hillier) and the neural mechanisms of spatial cognition (O'Keefe, Mosers). Identifies the specific architectural features that generate navigational PE: ambiguous choice points, repetitive corridors, inconsistent floor-to-floor geometry, hidden stairs.

**7. Colin Ellard** (University of Waterloo, Department of Psychology; author, *Places of the Heart*)
Research on the psychological and physiological effects of urban and architectural environments. His work uses mobile sensing (GSR, heart rate) and experience sampling to measure emotional responses to spatial environments in real time as people move through them. Has documented that spatial openness, complexity, and visual access produce measurable autonomic and affective responses that change step by step as the spatial configuration changes (Ellard, 2015, ~100 citations).
*Role*: Provides the real-time measurement perspective — how emotional response tracks spatial configuration moment by moment as a person moves through a building. His methodology captures the TEMPORAL dynamics of spatial PE: the emotional spike when turning a corner into a vast atrium, the creeping anxiety in a repetitive corridor, the relief of arriving at a recognizable landmark.

## Position Statements

### SPACE SYNTAX AND THE MATHEMATICS OF SPATIAL PREDICTION

**Position Statement 1: Bill Hillier — Spatial Configuration as a Prediction Structure**

Space Syntax began as a theory of how spatial configuration generates social life. The central discovery is that the pattern of movement through a building or city can be predicted — with remarkable accuracy — from the spatial configuration alone, without knowing anything about the destinations people are seeking, the attractiveness of different locations, or the visual qualities of the environment (Hillier & Hanson, 1984, ~5,000 citations; Hillier, 1996, ~3,000 citations). The metric that predicts movement is **integration**: a measure of how few direction changes are needed to reach a space from all other spaces in the system. High-integration spaces (few turns from everywhere) attract more movement. Low-integration spaces (many turns, topologically remote) attract less.

Why does this work? Because integration measures the PREDICTABILITY of a space within the spatial network. A highly integrated space is one that the navigator can reach from many starting points by simple, direct routes — it is easy to predict that you will pass through this space, and easy to find your way back to it. A segregated space is one that can only be reached by complex, indirect routes — hard to predict, hard to find, hard to return to. People move through spatial networks along lines of highest predictability, because doing so minimizes the cognitive effort of navigation.

The PE translation is immediate: **integration is an inverse measure of navigational prediction error.** High integration → low PE (you expect to find this space and you do, easily). Low integration → high PE (you are uncertain whether you will find this space, and the route is harder than expected). The correlation between integration and movement density is the behavioral consequence of a cognitive PE minimization strategy.

But it goes deeper. Space Syntax also defines **intelligibility** — the correlation between local connectivity (how many spaces you can see from where you are) and global integration (how connected you actually are to the whole system). In a highly intelligible building, local visual information accurately predicts global spatial structure — what you can see tells you where you are in the whole building. In an unintelligible building, local information is misleading — a well-connected node may be locally prominent but globally remote, or a space that looks like a dead end may actually be a shortcut. Intelligibility is a direct measure of how well the visual system's local spatial prediction matches the global spatial reality. High intelligibility → low prediction error between local perception and global structure. Low intelligibility → high PE → the navigational stress and disorientation that T14 describes.

**Position Statement 2: Alan Penn — Isovists and Visual Prediction Range**

The isovist — the total volume of space visible from a given point — is the spatial unit that defines what the visual system can predict at any moment (Benedikt, 1979, ~1,500 citations; Turner, Doxa, O'Sullivan, & Penn, 2001, ~1,200 citations). When you stand in a building, your visual system has access to a specific region of space: everything within the isovist. Beyond the isovist boundary — around corners, behind walls, through doorways not yet reached — you cannot see, and the brain must predict based on spatial inference.

Isovist analysis decomposes architectural plans into fields of visual access. Key properties include: **isovist area** (how much space is visible — larger area → wider prediction range → more confident spatial model), **isovist perimeter** (including occluding edges, which define the boundaries where prediction must take over from perception), **isovist compactness** (how regular the visible region is — a compact, circular isovist provides uniform prediction range in all directions; an elongated, irregular isovist provides high prediction accuracy in some directions and low in others), and **isovist drift** (how the isovist shifts as you move — rapid drift means the visual prediction is changing quickly, requiring continuous spatial updating).

Visual Graph Analysis (VGA) treats every point in a building plan as a node and connects nodes that can see each other, creating a visibility graph. The graph-theoretic properties of this network predict behavior: nodes with high visual connectivity (many lines of sight) attract more movement and are experienced as "open" and "public"; nodes with low connectivity (few sightlines, many occluding edges) feel "private" or "hidden." The clustering coefficient of the visibility graph predicts how predictable the local spatial structure is from any point — high clustering → redundant visual information → confident local prediction; low clustering → sparse, non-redundant visual information → uncertain prediction.

For the PE framework: the isovist IS the prediction horizon. Moving through a building is a continuous process of updating the prediction horizon — as you move, some previously predicted spaces become visible (the prediction is tested) and new occluded spaces enter the prediction range (new predictions are generated). A building with smoothly changing isovists (gentle curves, gradual reveals) produces continuous, moderate PE — the prediction horizon shifts but never snaps. A building with sharp isovist transitions (turning a corner to encounter an entirely new spatial volume) produces discontinuous, high-magnitude PE — the prediction horizon changes abruptly and the brain must rapidly construct a new spatial model.

**Position Statement 3: Ruth Dalton — Spatial Cognition Is Topological, Not Euclidean**

A critical finding from spatial cognition research is that people's mental representations of buildings are topological — they encode the CONNECTIONS between spaces (which room leads to which) rather than the metric distances between them (Dalton, 2003, ~300 citations). This is why Space Syntax, which operates on topological properties (depth, connectivity, integration), predicts behavior better than metric models (distance, area).

The implication for the PE framework is that the brain's spatial prediction is also primarily topological. When navigating a building, the brain predicts "the next space should be the main corridor" (a topological prediction) rather than "the next space should be 4.7 meters to the north" (a metric prediction). When the topological prediction is confirmed (you turn the corner and there IS the main corridor), PE is low. When it is violated (you turn the corner and find an unexpected stairwell), PE is high — and it is high because the TOPOLOGICAL structure of your cognitive map is wrong, not because of any metric error.

This explains why repetitive corridors are so disorienting: they provide adequate metric information (you can judge distances) but inadequate topological information (every corridor-corridor junction looks the same, so your cognitive map cannot distinguish which junction you are at). The result is topological PE — your position in the spatial graph is uncertain even though your position in metric space is perfectly determined. This is the mechanism behind T14 (navigational stress): the stress comes not from being lost in absolute space but from being lost in configuration space — unable to locate yourself in the building's topological structure.

### SPATIAL COGNITION AND NAVIGATION NEUROSCIENCE

**Position Statement 4: John O'Keefe — Place Cells, Cognitive Maps, and Architectural Space**

The hippocampal cognitive map is not a passive recording of visited locations. It is an active prediction system. Place cells fire not only when the animal IS at their preferred location but also when the animal EXPECTS to be at that location based on its current trajectory (O'Keefe & Nadel, 1978, ~10,000 citations; O'Keefe, 1976, ~2,000 citations). The hippocampus generates spatial predictions — "given my current position and heading, the next place I should encounter is X" — and these predictions are tested against incoming sensory evidence. When the prediction matches (you arrive where expected), the spatial model is confirmed. When it fails (you encounter an unexpected space, or fail to encounter an expected one), the hippocampal prediction error drives map updating.

The architectural relevance is that building layout determines the structure and reliability of the hippocampal map. A building with distinctive spaces — rooms that differ in shape, size, material, light, and acoustic character — produces a rich hippocampal map with many distinct place-cell ensembles. Each space activates a unique pattern, and navigation is reliable because position in the building maps uniquely to a hippocampal state. A building with undifferentiated spaces — identical rooms, repetitive corridors, uniform materials and lighting — produces an impoverished hippocampal map where multiple physical locations activate similar place-cell patterns. The result is spatial ambiguity: the hippocampus cannot distinguish where you are because the sensory evidence is the same everywhere.

This is why landmarks matter for navigation: they provide sensory evidence that disambiguates otherwise similar spatial locations. A red door, a distinctive light fixture, a window with a view, a change in floor material — each provides a sensory signal that helps the hippocampus distinguish one location from another. The landmark is not merely a visual signpost; it is a sensory signal that activates a unique place-cell ensemble, anchoring the cognitive map to a specific position. Buildings that provide adequate landmarks at decision points (where the navigator must choose a direction) support strong cognitive maps with low navigational PE. Buildings that fail to provide landmarks at decision points produce weak maps with high navigational PE.

**Position Statement 5: May-Britt Moser and Edvard Moser — Grid Cells, Boundaries, and the Metric of Space**

Grid cells in the medial entorhinal cortex provide the brain's internal coordinate system — a hexagonal lattice of firing locations that tiles the environment and provides the metric scaffold on which the cognitive map is built (Hafting et al., 2005, ~3,000 citations; Moser, Rowland, & Moser, 2015, ~1,000 citations). Grid cells support path integration: the ability to estimate position by integrating self-motion signals (vestibular, proprioceptive, visual flow) without external landmarks. Border cells fire when the animal is near an environmental boundary — a wall, ledge, or edge — and anchor the grid cell coordinate system to the physical structure of the environment.

For architecture: **walls are border-cell activators.** Every wall, partition, column, and edge in a building produces a border-cell firing event that helps the brain calibrate its spatial coordinate system. Rooms with clear boundaries (four walls, a distinct floor and ceiling) produce strong border-cell signals that anchor the grid. Open-plan spaces with few boundaries produce weak border-cell signals and the grid cell coordinate system drifts — the brain loses metric confidence about position and distance. This drift is the neurophysiological basis of the often-reported experience that open-plan offices feel "disorienting" despite being visually transparent — you can SEE everything but your metric spatial system lacks the boundary signals it needs to maintain positional certainty.

Room proportions affect grid cell firing patterns: square rooms produce symmetric grid fields; elongated rooms stretch the grid along the long axis; L-shaped rooms produce distorted grid fields near the bend (Krupic, Bauza, Burton, Barry, & O'Keefe, 2015, ~300 citations). The implication is that room geometry is not merely an aesthetic choice — it determines the structure of the brain's internal spatial representation. Regular geometries (rectangular, circular) produce regular, predictable grid patterns. Irregular geometries (non-orthogonal, curved, multi-angled) produce irregular grid patterns that require more computational effort to maintain and update — more spatial PE.

The vertical dimension is less well-characterized but emerging: grid cells appear to represent vertical space differently from horizontal space, with lower resolution and less regularity (Hayman, Verriotis, Jovalekic, Fenton, & Jeffery, 2011, ~300 citations). This may explain why multi-level wayfinding is disproportionately difficult: the brain's coordinate system is optimized for horizontal navigation and degrades in the vertical dimension. Stairwells and elevators, which translate between floors, are points of maximal spatial PE because the brain must recompute its position in three dimensions with a coordinate system that is less reliable vertically.

**Position Statement 6: Christoph Hölscher — What Makes Buildings Hard to Navigate**

Laboratory neuroscience tells us WHAT the brain computes during navigation. My applied research asks WHY specific buildings defeat these computations (Hölscher et al., 2006, ~300 citations; Hölscher et al., 2012, ~200 citations; Carlson, Hölscher, Shipley, & Dalton, 2010, ~200 citations). The findings identify specific architectural features that generate navigational PE:

**Floor plan complexity**: Buildings with high axial-line counts (many corridors, many turns) produce more navigational errors than buildings with simple plans. But complexity interacts with intelligibility: a complex-but-intelligible building (many spaces, but local visibility accurately predicts global structure) can be navigated successfully. A complex-and-unintelligible building (many spaces, and local visibility is misleading) produces severe wayfinding failure.

**Vertical circulation ambiguity**: In multi-story buildings, the single largest source of navigational error is confusion about vertical position — which floor am I on? — particularly when the floor plans are similar across levels. The stairwell or elevator removes the navigator from the spatial network (you cannot see the building while in transit between floors) and deposits them at a new location with the expectation that they can re-orient. When the arrival floor looks identical to the departure floor, re-orientation fails and the navigator must reconstruct their position from scratch.

**Decision point ambiguity**: Wayfinding errors cluster at "choice points" — intersections where multiple routes are available. The navigational PE at a choice point is proportional to the UNCERTAINTY about which route leads to the destination. If one option is visually more integrated (wider, brighter, more connected), it attracts movement and the decision is low-PE. If the options are visually equivalent (same width, same depth, same lighting), the decision is high-PE and errors increase.

**Landmark poverty**: Buildings that provide distinctive visual features at decision points reduce navigational PE dramatically. The feature need not be signage (which is a cognitive, not perceptual, aid); it can be any distinctive sensory event — a material change, a light change, a spatial widening, a view to the outside, a sound (fountain, music, crowd). The landmark anchors the cognitive map at the decision point, allowing the navigator to say "I am at the marble-floor intersection" rather than "I am at one of twelve identical corridor junctions."

**Position Statement 7: Colin Ellard — Real-Time Emotional Response to Spatial Configuration**

When you move through a building, your emotional state is not constant — it tracks spatial configuration moment by moment. My work uses mobile psychophysiology (galvanic skin response, heart rate variability) and continuous self-report to capture this real-time emotional trajectory (Ellard, 2015, ~100 citations; Ellard & Milne, 2019, ~50 citations).

The findings show that spatial transitions produce measurable autonomic responses within 2–5 seconds. Entering a large, open volume from a constrained corridor produces a GSR spike (arousal increase) and a shift in self-reported affect (typically positive — "relief," "opening up," "spaciousness"). The magnitude of the response scales with the CONTRAST between the spaces — moving from a 2.5m ceiling corridor to a 15m atrium produces a larger response than moving from a 3m corridor to a 5m room. This contrast-dependent scaling is exactly what a PE model predicts: the response magnitude is proportional to the mismatch between the spatial prediction (formed in the corridor) and the spatial reality (encountered in the atrium).

Conversely, entering a constrained space from an open one produces arousal increase with negative valence — "closing in," "constriction," "heaviness." But this response habituates faster than the opening response, suggesting an asymmetry in spatial PE processing: spatial expansion produces stronger and more sustained emotional responses than spatial contraction. This may reflect an evolutionary bias — spatial opening reveals new information (potential resources, escape routes, threats to assess) while spatial closing reduces information (fewer variables to process, the space becomes known).

The architectural promenade exploits these dynamics. A skilled architect (Le Corbusier, Ando, Siza, Holl) designs a sequence of spatial contrasts that produce a specific emotional trajectory over time — compression → release → compression → revelation → compression → release → climax. The visitor's autonomic response tracks this trajectory step by step. The experience is temporal and dynamic, like music, and the PE framework captures WHY the sequence produces emotion: each transition is a spatial prediction error event, and the sequence of errors is composed for emotional effect.

### ARCHITECTURAL THEORY AND THE PROMENADE

**Position Statement 8: Juhani Pallasmaa — Threshold, Compression, and Bodily Space**

The experience of moving through a building is not primarily visual. It is proprioceptive, kinesthetic, and haptic — the body navigates, not the eye (Pallasmaa, 2005, ~3,000 citations; Pallasmaa, 2011, ~500 citations). The **threshold** — the boundary between one spatial condition and another — is the fundamental unit of spatial-sequential experience. A doorway is a threshold: one side is one spatial world (scale, light, material, temperature, sound), and the other side is another. Crossing the threshold requires the body to adapt — adjust gait, adjust posture, adjust sensory expectations. The threshold is where prediction error peaks, because the entire multi-modal prediction set must update simultaneously.

**Compression-release** is the architect's oldest technique for amplifying threshold PE. Le Corbusier understood it intuitively: approach the Villa Savoye through the pilotis (low ceiling, forest of columns → enclosed, constrained spatial prediction) → ascend the ramp (gradual spatial opening, increasing light) → arrive on the roof terrace (sudden sky, panorama, breeze → maximum spatial PE: the prediction was "interior" and the reality is "exterior-on-a-rooftop"). The compression builds the prediction; the release violates it. The intensity of the release is proportional to the intensity of the compression.

But there is a bodily dimension that PE language must not lose. The body FEELS compression — the shoulders tighten, the head ducks slightly, the gait shortens. The body FEELS release — the shoulders drop, the spine extends, the breathing deepens. These are not metaphors; they are measurable postural and respiratory changes that track spatial proportion. Architecture is experienced through the body's motor and proprioceptive systems, not only through the visual and auditory systems. The spatial PE at a threshold is simultaneously visual (new vista), acoustic (new reverberation), thermal (new air temperature), haptic (new floor material), proprioceptive (new postural demand), and motor (new gait pattern). It is AX6 (multi-modal PE integration) in its purest architectural form.

**Position Statement 9: Steven Holl — Parallax, Enmeshed Experience, and Spatial Composition**

Architecture is not experienced in plan view. It is experienced through movement — the continuous parallax of forms, surfaces, and light as the body moves through space (Holl, 2000, ~300 citations). When I design a building, I am composing a temporal experience: a sequence of perceptual events that unfold as the visitor moves along a path. The path IS the medium; the building IS the score.

In the Kiasma Museum in Helsinki, the visitor's path curves through a space that is simultaneously symmetrical in plan and asymmetrical in section — the walls lean, the ceiling slopes, the light enters from unexpected angles. The result is that the visual prediction (based on the apparent plan symmetry) is continuously and subtly violated by the sectional reality. The visitor feels that the space is "right" (because the plan proportions are balanced) and "unusual" (because the section proportions are not) simultaneously. This is a calibrated PE: moderate enough to sustain interest, not so large as to produce disorientation.

The concept of **enmeshed experience** means that spatial sequence cannot be separated from material sequence, light sequence, acoustic sequence, or thermal sequence. When you move from the entrance hall (concrete floor, compressed ceiling, indirect light) to the gallery (oak floor, double-height, zenithal daylight), you are not experiencing a spatial transition AND a material transition AND a light transition separately — you are experiencing a single, integrated transition that crosses all modalities simultaneously. The architect composes all of these together, not independently. The PE framework must model this as a SINGLE multi-modal PE event at the transition, not as four parallel single-modal events.

**Position Statement 10: Peter Eisenman — Architecture Against Prediction**

Most architecture seeks to confirm the body's expectations. Floors are flat. Walls are vertical. Corridors lead somewhere. Rooms have doors. My work asks: what happens when architecture deliberately violates these expectations? (Eisenman, 1999, ~500 citations; Eisenman, 2003, ~300 citations).

In the Wexner Center for the Arts, a buried grid — the historical street grid of Columbus, Ohio — intersects the building's grid at a 12.25° angle. The resulting spaces have non-orthogonal walls, skewed geometries, and structural elements that do not align with the apparent spatial logic. The body's expectation of orthogonality (an extremely deep prediction, reinforced by virtually every built environment encountered since birth) is continuously violated. The result is not disorientation (the building is perfectly navigable) but cognitive alertness — a sustained low-level spatial PE that keeps the mind engaged with the spatial structure rather than habituating to it.

In the Holocaust Memorial in Berlin, 2,711 concrete stelae of varying heights are arranged on a tilting ground plane. As you walk into the field, the stelae rise above your head, the ground drops, and you lose sight of the surroundings. The spatial prediction (I am in an open field in a city) is progressively violated (I am in a labyrinth of stone pillars with no visible exit, the ground is not level, I am disoriented). The PE is high, deliberate, and emotionally charged — the disorientation produces the anxiety and vulnerability that the memorial intends to evoke.

For the PE framework: there must be a region beyond the Goldilocks zone where high spatial PE does not produce the awe of AX3 (which involves vastness and schema accommodation) but produces a different class of response — intellectual engagement, critical awareness, deliberate discomfort. Not all high PE is negative (the Goldilocks model does not claim this); high PE can be intentionally productive when it serves a cognitive or emotional purpose. The template should distinguish between (a) excessive PE from bad design (repetitive corridors, confusing layouts → navigational stress, T14), (b) excessive PE from sacred/sublime spaces (cathedral naves, mountain vistas → awe, AX3), and (c) excessive PE from deliberate architectural challenge (Eisenman, Libeskind, Hadid → cognitive alertness, critical reflection).

**Position Statement 11: Roger Ulrich — View, Spatial Access, and Health Recovery**

The "View Through a Window" study (Ulrich, 1984, ~6,000 citations) demonstrated that surgical patients whose hospital rooms overlooked trees recovered faster, required less pain medication, and had fewer post-surgical complications than patients whose rooms overlooked a brick wall. This finding has been replicated and extended across healthcare settings, workplaces, and educational environments (Ulrich et al., 2008, ~3,000 citations).

The spatial-configuration dimension that this panel must formalize: the view from a hospital room is determined by the building's spatial configuration — the room's position on the floor plate, its orientation, the window size and height, and what lies within the visual field defined by the window's geometry. The same hospital can have rooms with nature views and rooms with wall views depending on floor plan layout and site orientation. The health effect is not a property of "having a window" — it is a property of the specific spatial-configurational relationship between the patient's position and the external environment.

Stress Recovery Theory (SRT) proposes that nature views activate an evolved positive affective response that counteracts stress (Ulrich, 1983, ~5,000 citations). Panel T2-C reduced biophilia to T1 + T2 + T5. But the spatial dimension is underspecified: SRT requires not just "nature" but visual ACCESS to nature, which is a spatial-configurational property. The isovist from the patient's bed position, extended through the window, defines the visual prediction range — and when that isovist includes natural elements (vegetation, sky, water), the occupant receives continuous low-PE confirmatory signals (familiar natural patterns) that reduce allostatic load. When the isovist terminates at a brick wall 3 meters away, the visual prediction range is minimized and the occupant receives no restorative visual signals.

**Position Statement 12: Jan Gehl — Spatial Configuration and Social Life**

The central finding of fifty years of public life research is that spatial configuration creates the CONDITIONS for social encounter — it does not determine the encounter itself (Gehl, 1971/2011, ~4,000 citations; Gehl, 2010, ~3,000 citations). People choose whether to interact, but architecture determines whether the choice is available. A building (or street, or plaza) that puts people in visual and physical proximity, that provides comfortable edges for lingering, that offers visual interest at the human scale, and that allows both engagement and withdrawal creates the conditions for rich social life. A building that isolates people in private cells connected by featureless corridors creates the conditions for social poverty.

The metrics that predict social activity are spatial: **visual permeability** (can people see each other across the space?), **edge density** (are there places to pause at the boundary between public and private?), **path convergence** (do routes naturally bring people together?), and **optional stopping** (are there reasons to pause — a bench, a view, a coffee cart — rather than simply transit?). These are all spatial-configurational properties that can be measured from a plan.

For the PE framework: social encounter is a form of social PE — encountering another person means encountering an agent whose behavior must be predicted (T48–T52 social brain). Spatial configurations that increase social encounter rates increase the frequency of social prediction events. But the VALENCE of these events depends on context: in a village square or office break room, social encounters are expected, desired, and low-PE (you predicted you might meet someone, and you did). In a hospital corridor at night or a parking garage, social encounters are unexpected, unwanted, and high-PE (you did NOT predict meeting someone here, and the encounter triggers T5 threat assessment). The spatial configuration determines both the FREQUENCY and the CONTEXT of social PE events, and context determines whether the PE is positive (social reward) or negative (social threat).

**Position Statement 13: Julio Bermudez — Extraordinary Architectural Experiences and Spatial Revelation**

My research on Extraordinary Architectural Experiences (EAEs) surveyed over 2,000 architects and non-architects about their most profound experiences of buildings (Bermudez, 2015, ~100 citations). The most frequently cited spatial characteristics of EAE-triggering buildings are:

**Spatial revelation**: the moment of suddenly encountering an unexpectedly vast, dramatic, or beautiful space. The most-cited specific EAEs — arriving in the Pantheon, entering Chartres, stepping into the Alhambra's Court of the Lions, emerging onto the terrace of Fallingwater — all involve a spatial transition from a constrained approach to a revelatory space. The emotional intensity correlates with the CONTRAST between the approach and the revelation.

**Spatial complexity with order**: EAE-triggering buildings are not simple, but they are not chaotic. They have complex spatial structures organized by clear principles — geometry, proportion, rhythm, axis. The occupant senses both the complexity (this is a rich environment) and the order (this complexity has meaning, it is organized by an intelligence). This is the Goldilocks zone at the spatial scale: enough complexity to sustain engagement, enough order to support prediction.

**Light-space interaction**: Nearly every cited EAE involves dramatic light — a shaft from an oculus, light filtering through stained glass, zenithal daylight flooding a white volume. The spatial revelation is typically also a luminous revelation. This confirms L-I's finding that light PE and spatial PE converge at peak architectural experiences.

**Bodily involvement**: EAEs are not passive visual experiences. They involve the body — climbing stairs, passing through narrow passages, turning corners, ascending into light. The approach sequence prepares the body (and the prediction system) for the revelation. Without the approach, the revelation is diminished. This supports the architectural promenade thesis: the sequence IS the experience.

The connection to AX3 (awe) is direct. EAEs typically produce the classic awe signature: perceived vastness, need for accommodation (existing spatial schemas are insufficient), small-self (the space dwarfs the individual), and absorption (the person loses track of time and self-focused cognition, consistent with DMN deactivation). But the EAE data add a crucial detail: the awe is not produced by the revelation space ALONE — it is produced by the SEQUENCE of compression → revelation. The same Pantheon interior viewed in a photograph produces admiration; ARRIVING in the Pantheon through the narrow portico produces awe. The temporal structure matters.

## Cross-Examination and Debate

### Debate 1: Does Space Syntax Need PE, or Does PE Need Space Syntax?

**Challenge**: Space Syntax already predicts behavior (movement, wayfinding, social encounter) from spatial configuration without invoking prediction error. Adding a PE interpretation to integration, depth, and intelligibility may add theoretical flavor but does it add predictive power?

**Hillier**: Space Syntax predicts WHAT happens (which spaces attract movement) but not WHY it happens neurally or emotionally. The PE translation adds the WHY: movement follows integration because the brain minimizes navigational prediction error. More importantly, the PE translation connects spatial behavior to EMOTIONAL response, which Space Syntax alone does not predict. Integration predicts movement density; it does not predict how PLEASANT the movement experience is. The PE framework adds the valence dimension: moderate integration → moderate navigational PE → pleasant wayfinding; very high integration → very low PE → boring, oversimplified spatial experience; very low integration → high PE → stressful, disorienting.

**Penn**: The PE framework also explains WHY intelligibility matters for experience rather than just for wayfinding accuracy. In a highly intelligible building, the visual system's local prediction closely matches the global spatial reality. This cross-level prediction confirmation produces a deep sense of spatial coherence — "this building makes sense." In an unintelligible building, local vision systematically misleads about global structure, producing a persistent, low-level spatial PE that occupants experience as unease or confusion even when they can find their way.

**O'Keefe**: The neuroscience adds something Space Syntax cannot provide on its own: the mechanism. Place cells, grid cells, and border cells are the neural implementation of the spatial prediction that Space Syntax metrics describe. Integration corresponds to the density and reliability of place cell ensembles along a path. Depth corresponds to the number of sequential hippocampal predictions required to navigate between two points. Intelligibility corresponds to the accuracy of the visual cortex → hippocampus prediction (can what you see predict where you are in the cognitive map?). The PE framework makes these correspondences explicit.

**Panel consensus**: **PE adds explanatory power to Space Syntax, and Space Syntax provides quantitative operationalizations for spatial PE.** The relationship is bidirectional and genuinely productive. Space Syntax without PE can predict behavior but not emotion. PE without Space Syntax can explain emotional responses but cannot quantify spatial prediction from architectural plans. Together, they provide a framework that connects architectural drawings to emotional experience through specified neural mechanisms.

### Debate 2: Is the Architectural Promenade a Single Template or a Meta-Template?

**Challenge**: The architectural promenade — the deliberate sequencing of spatial experiences for emotional effect — involves MULTIPLE mechanisms (spatial PE, luminance PE, material PE, acoustic PE, thermal PE) orchestrated over time. Is this one template or a design principle that coordinates existing templates?

**Holl**: From a design perspective, it is a single compositional act. I do not design the spatial sequence, then the light sequence, then the material sequence separately and hope they align. I compose a single experiential trajectory that integrates all modalities. The template should reflect this unity.

**Ellard**: From a measurement perspective, the emotional response is unitary — a single GSR trace, a single valence-arousal trajectory. The body does not produce separate spatial-PE and luminance-PE emotional responses; it produces a single integrated emotional response to the transition. This argues for a single template that describes the multi-modal transition event.

**Pallasmaa**: The threshold is the phenomenological unit. At a threshold, EVERYTHING changes — space, light, material, sound, temperature, proprioceptive demand. The template should model the threshold as a multi-modal PE event, not as five parallel PE events.

**Hillier**: But the spatial component IS separable. Space Syntax predicts behavior from spatial configuration ALONE — you do not need to know the materials, the lighting, or the acoustics to predict movement patterns. This means there IS a spatial-specific PE component. The promenade template should acknowledge both the separable spatial component and the integrated multi-modal transition.

**Panel consensus**: **The architectural promenade should be formalized as a single template that describes the temporal orchestration of multi-modal PE, with the spatial component as the structural backbone.** Spatial sequence determines WHEN transitions occur (because you must move through space to encounter them) and WHAT changes (because each space has its own multi-modal character). The promenade template coordinates existing templates (L1 for light transitions, MAT1/MAT3 for material transitions, AX1/AX2 for acoustic transitions) but adds the temporal-compositional dimension: how the SEQUENCE of transitions is designed for cumulative emotional effect. This is the architecture-as-composition thesis formalized.

### Debate 3: Does Eisenman's "Architecture Against Prediction" Falsify the Goldilocks Model?

**Challenge** (from Eisenman's position statement): If high spatial PE can be intentionally productive — producing intellectual engagement and critical awareness rather than distress — then the inverted-U Goldilocks model (which predicts that PE above the optimal zone always reduces pleasure) is wrong.

**Eisenman**: The Holocaust Memorial produces high PE and high emotional intensity — but the intensity is not "pleasure" and it is not "awe" in the AX3 sense (there is no perceived vastness or schema accommodation). It is anxiety, vulnerability, disorientation. These are deliberately produced emotions, appropriate to a memorial, and the architecture is successful BECAUSE it exceeds the Goldilocks zone. If the model cannot accommodate intentional high-PE design, it is architecturally incomplete.

**Ellard**: The physiological data may resolve this. In my experience, deliberate-disorientation spaces produce high arousal (GSR elevation) with NEGATIVE valence (self-reported unease). This IS beyond the Goldilocks zone — but in the predicted direction. The Goldilocks model predicts that beyond-optimal PE produces negative affect, and it does. What Eisenman adds is that negative affect can be architecturally intentional and contextually appropriate.

**Bermudez**: The EAE data support a distinction between three beyond-Goldilocks regimes: (1) **Awe** (AX3): very high PE with perceived vastness → positive valence through schema accommodation, DMN reconfiguration. (2) **Sublime discomfort** (Eisenman): very high PE with spatial disorientation → negative valence that serves a cognitive purpose (memorial, critical reflection). (3) **Bad design** (T14): very high PE with navigational failure → negative valence that serves no purpose. The template must distinguish these.

**Hölscher**: The critical distinction is AGENCY. In the Holocaust Memorial, the visitor CHOOSES to enter and can leave at any time. The spatial PE is high but the visitor has control. In a confusing hospital or airport (T14), the navigator is FORCED to navigate and cannot escape the spatial PE. The same objective PE level produces different emotional responses depending on whether the occupant has agency over their exposure.

**Panel consensus**: **The Goldilocks model is not falsified by Eisenman; it is EXTENDED.** The model correctly predicts that beyond-optimal PE produces negative valence. Eisenman demonstrates that negative valence can be intentionally designed and contextually appropriate. The template should include a modifier for AGENCY (chosen vs. forced exposure) and PURPOSE (memorial/critical vs. functional navigation) that determines whether beyond-Goldilocks PE is experienced as (a) sublime/productive or (b) stressful/dysfunctional. Both are beyond the Goldilocks zone; the difference is context, not mechanism.

### Debate 4: How Does Vertical Space Differ From Horizontal Space?

**Challenge**: The cognitive neuroscience of navigation is overwhelmingly based on horizontal movement in single-level environments. Multi-story buildings introduce vertical transitions (stairs, elevators, ramps) that may involve different neural mechanisms. Are the templates adequate for three-dimensional buildings?

**E. Moser**: Grid cells in rodents are less regular and less reliable in the vertical dimension (Hayman et al., 2011). Head-direction cells maintain a single horizontal reference frame and do not update cleanly across vertical transitions. This means the brain's spatial coordinate system degrades when vertical movement is required — which predicts that multi-story buildings are inherently more difficult to navigate than single-story buildings of equivalent plan complexity.

**Hölscher**: This is exactly what we find empirically. Multi-story wayfinding errors are disproportionately concentrated at vertical transitions — specifically, at the moment of arriving on a new floor after using stairs or an elevator. The navigator must recompute their position and heading in a new horizontal plane, using a coordinate system that may have been disrupted by the vertical transit. If the floor plans are similar across levels, this recomputation often fails.

**Dalton**: Space Syntax has struggled with verticality. The original formalism operates on 2D plans. Extensions to 3D exist (using convex spaces connected by stairs/elevators) but are less well-validated. The templates should be explicit that the spatial prediction mechanisms are primarily horizontal, with vertical transitions imposing an additional PE cost that the current neuroscience does not fully characterize.

**Holl**: Architecturally, the ramp is the most interesting vertical element because it preserves continuous spatial updating — the grid cell system can track position continuously as the ramp rises, unlike stairs (intermittent steps that fragment the spatial flow) or elevators (complete removal from the spatial network for the duration of transit). Le Corbusier's preference for ramps over stairs was intuitive, but the neuroscience suggests a spatial-prediction reason: ramps minimize vertical PE because they allow continuous path integration.

**Panel consensus**: **The templates should include vertical transition as a spatial PE modifier.** Horizontal spatial prediction is well-modeled by place cells, grid cells, and Space Syntax. Vertical transitions impose an additional PE cost due to grid cell degradation in the vertical dimension. The cost is highest for elevators (complete spatial discontinuity), moderate for stairs (intermittent spatial sampling), and lowest for ramps (continuous spatial tracking). Multi-story buildings are inherently higher-PE than single-story buildings, all else equal, and design strategies that minimize vertical PE (distinctive floor plans, visible vertical circulation, ramps where feasible) reduce navigational stress.

## Panel Synthesis: What the Four Templates Collectively Reveal

### Opening Remarks from the Chair

**Hillier**: Four templates: SC1 (spatial integration and navigational PE), SC2 (isovist dynamics and visual prediction), SC3 (the architectural promenade), SC4 (spatial configuration and social encounter). Before we adjourn, the panel should address what these reveal about how spatial configuration functions in the larger framework.

### Space Is the Scaffold

**Penn**: The most important finding is that spatial configuration is the SCAFFOLD on which all other architectural experiences are hung. Light (L1–L5) operates within spaces that spatial configuration defines. Materials (MAT1–MAT5) are encountered along paths that spatial configuration creates. Acoustics (AX1–AX4) propagate within volumes that spatial configuration shapes. Social encounters (SC4) occur at confluences that spatial configuration generates. The spatial templates are not parallel to the sensory templates — they are PRIOR to them. You must be in a space before you can perceive its light, materials, and sound.

**Holl**: From a design perspective this is obvious — I design the spatial sequence first, then the light, material, and acoustic character of each space. The sequence determines the temporal order of sensory experience. But from a template-framework perspective it is important: SC3 (the promenade) is not just another template alongside L1 and MAT1. It is the TEMPORAL ORGANIZER that determines when and how all other templates are activated as the visitor moves.

**O'Keefe**: Neurally, the hippocampal cognitive map IS the scaffold. Place cells encode position, grid cells encode displacement, and border cells encode boundaries. Every other brain system that responds to architectural features — visual cortex processing luminance (L1), insular cortex processing thermal comfort (MAT2), auditory cortex processing reverberation (AX2) — receives input that is modulated by spatial context via hippocampal projections. The brain's spatial representation is not one sense among many; it is the context within which all senses operate.

### The Three Scales of Spatial Prediction

**Dalton**: Our four templates operate at three nested scales:

**Micro-scale (SC2)**: The isovist — what you can see right now. Prediction range = visual field. Updating rate = continuous with movement. PE events = each occluding edge transition. This is the moment-by-moment perceptual experience of space.

**Meso-scale (SC3)**: The promenade — the sequence of spaces you move through. Prediction = the multi-modal character of the next zone. Updating rate = each threshold crossing (every 10–60 seconds of walking). PE events = each transition. This is the temporal-compositional experience of architecture.

**Macro-scale (SC1, SC4)**: The spatial network — the building's topological structure. Prediction = where am I in the building, how do I reach my destination, who will I encounter? Updating rate = gradual as the cognitive map builds. PE events = route discoveries, disorientation episodes, unexpected social encounters. This is the navigational and social experience of architecture.

These scales are nested, not independent: the macro-scale cognitive map determines expectations about the meso-scale sequence, which determines expectations about the micro-scale isovist. A navigator who KNOWS the building (strong macro-scale map) will predict the sequence of spaces correctly (low meso-scale PE) and will anticipate each isovist before encountering it (low micro-scale PE). A first-time visitor has no macro-scale map, so every meso-scale transition and micro-scale isovist change is a PE event.

**Hölscher**: This nesting explains why familiarity transforms architectural experience. On the first visit to a complex building (a hospital, an airport, a museum), PE is high at all three scales — you are navigating, sequencing, and perceiving simultaneously. On repeated visits, the macro-scale and meso-scale PE diminish (you know the building), and the micro-scale perceptual experience becomes more prominent — you notice the light, the materials, the acoustic character that you were too cognitively loaded to appreciate on the first visit.

### Where the Evidence Is Strong, Where It Is Contested

**Hillier**: SC1's core claim — that integration predicts movement — is one of the most replicated findings in architectural research. Hundreds of studies across cultures and building types confirm it. The PE INTERPRETATION of integration is new, but the empirical base it rests on is solid.

**Penn**: SC2's isovist analysis is well-established mathematically and has been validated against behavioral data (movement, visual attention). The PE interpretation — isovists as prediction horizons — is theoretically compelling and consistent with the neuroscience (visual cortex prediction from occluded regions) but has not been directly tested experimentally. A study measuring spatial prediction accuracy as a function of isovist properties would be informative.

**Ellard**: SC3's architectural promenade has strong phenomenological support (every architect and architecture student can identify spatial sequences that produce emotional effects) and my psychophysiology data confirm that spatial transitions produce measurable autonomic responses. But the COMPOSITIONAL claim — that the sequence of transitions is designed like a musical score, with the same temporal optimization principles — is theoretical. We do not have a quantitative model of optimal threshold density or optimal compression-release ratios.

**Gehl**: SC4's claim that spatial configuration determines social encounter conditions is supported by fifty years of observational research. The relationship between spatial integration and pedestrian density is empirical. The PE interpretation — that social encounters are social PE events whose valence depends on spatial context — is new but entirely consistent with the social brain literature. The open-plan office case (high visual connectivity → chronic social monitoring → attention fatigue) is well-documented by Kim and de Dear (2013) among many others.

## Testable Predictions and Critical Experiments

### Panel Discussion: What Should We Predict, and What Would Falsify Us?

**Prediction 1: The Integration-Emotion Correlation**

**Hillier**: SC1 predicts that spatial integration correlates not only with movement density (established) but with EMOTIONAL valence of the navigation experience (new). Map a building's integration values and survey wayfinding occupants' emotional states at each location. SC1 predicts: positive affect correlates with moderate integration; anxiety correlates with low integration; boredom or inattention correlates with very high integration (oversimplified spatial experience, e.g., a single long corridor). This is the emotional Goldilocks zone applied to spatial configuration.

**Prediction 2: The Isovist-Threat Correlation**

**Penn**: SC2 predicts that isovist area at an occupant's position correlates inversely with physiological threat markers. Equip occupants with GSR monitors and map their physiological arousal onto the building plan. SC2 predicts: elevated GSR in small-isovist locations (enclosed, many occluding edges) and reduced GSR in large-isovist locations (open, panoramic). The critical control: a space with a large isovist but high social density should show elevated GSR (from SC4 social monitoring) despite the spatial openness — the two predictions conflict, and the relative magnitudes reveal which is dominant.

**Prediction 3: The Compression-Release Magnitude Function**

**Ellard**: SC3 predicts that the emotional response at a spatial release (opening from compression to expansion) scales with the compression-release CONTRAST — specifically, with the ratio of isovist areas (post-transition / pre-transition). Measure GSR and valence at spatial transitions in a building with varying compression-release ratios. SC3 predicts a positive but decelerating function: doubling the isovist ratio produces a measurable but not doubled emotional response (because the PE system adapts to moderate compression and does not generate proportionally more prediction for more extreme compression).

**Prediction 4: The Vertical PE Cost**

**Hölscher**: SC1's vertical modifier predicts that multi-story wayfinding is disproportionately difficult relative to the added spatial complexity. Test: compare wayfinding performance in a single-story building (plan complexity X) with performance in a multi-story building with the SAME total plan complexity distributed across floors. SC1 predicts the multi-story building produces significantly more navigational errors, longer completion times, and higher self-reported stress — even though the total spatial complexity is identical — because of the vertical PE cost (grid cell degradation at floor transitions).

**Prediction 5: The Edge Condition Social Effect**

**Gehl**: SC4 predicts that adding edge conditions (alcoves, window seats, setbacks, benches along corridors) to a building increases both the frequency and the QUALITY of social interaction — specifically, by converting forced encounters (in movement spaces) to chosen encounters (at edges). Retrofit a building corridor with edge conditions and measure social interaction before and after. SC4 predicts: total social encounters increase modestly, but QUALITY indicators (conversation duration, self-reported social satisfaction, laughter frequency) increase substantially — because edge conditions convert social PE from forced-unpredictable (negative valence) to chosen-predictable (positive valence).

## Open Questions, Unfinished Business, and Panel SC-II Prospectus

### What a Second Spatial Panel Would Do

**Hillier**: Three priorities.

**SC-II Priority 1: Calibrate SC3 (Architectural Promenade) — The Threshold Density Function**

SC3 is theoretically complete but parametrically empty. We know spatial transitions produce emotional responses; we do not know the optimal rate of transitions, the ideal compression-release ratio, or the quantitative relationship between isovist contrast and emotional magnitude.

**What SC-II would produce**: Quantitative functions relating threshold density to emotional engagement (the temporal Goldilocks curve for spatial transitions), compression-release ratios to GSR magnitude, and multi-modal convergence at thresholds to emotional intensity.

**Panelists who would return**: Ellard (psychophysiology of spatial transitions), Holl (design-compositional expertise), Bermudez (EAE database for calibration anchors).

**Additional panelist needed**: Someone with VR-based spatial experience research — possibly Kuliga (ETH Zürich, VR wayfinding and spatial cognition) or Banaei (VR-based emotional response to architectural spaces).

**SC-II Priority 2: Validate SC1 Integration-Emotion Prediction**

The integration-movement correlation is established; the integration-EMOTION correlation (our Prediction 1) is untested. This is the critical prediction that distinguishes the PE interpretation from the behavioral one.

**What SC-II would produce**: Empirical data on the correlation between Space Syntax integration values and occupant emotional states at specific locations in real buildings.

**Panelists who would return**: Hillier (Space Syntax metrics), Ellard (mobile psychophysiology), Dalton (spatial cognition and behavior).

**SC-II Priority 3: Three-Dimensional Space Syntax**

Our vertical-PE modifier (SC1) and the debates about multi-story navigation reveal that the current framework is primarily two-dimensional. A second panel should address how spatial prediction works in three dimensions and whether the Mosers' finding of degraded vertical grid cell resolution leads to testable architectural predictions.

**Panelists who would return**: Hölscher (multi-story wayfinding), Dalton (3D Space Syntax extensions), E. Moser (vertical grid cell neuroscience).

### Scope Exclusions — Work That Belongs Elsewhere

**Gehl**: Urban-scale spatial configuration — street networks, public space systems, city morphology — is beyond this panel's scope. It uses the same Space Syntax tools but involves different scale-specific mechanisms (transport, land use, urban economics). A future Urban Spatial panel could address this.

**Pallasmaa**: The phenomenology of SPECIFIC spatial types — the courtyard, the cloister, the staircase, the corridor, the threshold — deserves deeper treatment than SC3 can provide. Each type has its own phenomenological literature and its own PE signature. A future Spatial Typology panel could catalogue these.

### Estimated Impact of SC-II on Coverage

If SC-II accomplishes Priorities 1–2, A3 coverage would consolidate at ★★★★ with SC3 gaining empirical calibration. Priority 3 (3D Space Syntax) would extend coverage to multi-story buildings specifically.
