**Date**: 2026-03-03

## Overall Assessment

This is an excellent academic presentation. It already follows several of the principles codified in the new Academic Presentation Service, particularly narrative threading (Principle 4) and scaffolded complexity (Principle 2). The talk poses a genuine intellectual puzzle in its opening (why do open offices fail?), builds through composed predictions, and maintains a coherent argumentative thread across 25 slides. The speaker notes are detailed and carry the full narrative — this is exactly how academic presentations should work.

**Strengths**:
- Sentence headlines on most content slides (Principle 1: assertion-evidence)
- Excellent scaffolding: concrete example (open offices) → mechanism → formalism → composed predictions → system architecture
- Speaker notes are outstanding — full explanations with citations, qualifications, and rhetorical guidance
- The composed predictions (slides 11, 13, 15, 19) are the strongest slides and demonstrate what the system uniquely provides
- Narrative threading is coherent: the audience always knows where they are in the argument
- The closing (slide 24) circles back to the audience context (Clark's lab)

## Specific Improvements

### Content/Structure

1. **Slide 1 (Title)**: Currently topic-label format. Consider rephrasing as a question: "Can computational epistemology make environmental psychology mechanistic?" or "What happens when you compose 16 theories of mind-environment interaction?" — this creates forward momentum per Principle 4.

2. **Slide 2 (Diagram)**: Image-only slide with no headline. Needs a sentence headline stating what the diagram shows. Currently violates Principle 1 (assertion-evidence).

3. **Slide 5 (Theories and Derivation)**: This is the most information-dense slide — 16 theories + a worked example. Consider splitting into two slides: (a) the 16 theories in their two groups, (b) the worked example showing PP → Template T4. Currently risks cognitive overload (Principle 5).

4. **Slide 7 (BN explanation)**: This is a "standard background" slide. For an audience at Clark's lab, they likely know BNs well. Consider condensing to half a slide or noting "you know this, but let me set up the contrast with what we need."

5. **Slide 20 (Coverage table)**: Pure table with star ratings. This violates Principle 1 — no visual evidence, just data in text form. Consider a radar chart or heat map showing coverage depth by domain. The numbers (52+ templates) deserve visual emphasis.

6. **Slide 25 (Closing)**: Currently a summary with three assertions. Good structure, but could be stronger by returning to the opening example (open offices) and showing how the system answered the question posed in slide 3. The circularity creates rhetorical closure.

### Visual Design

7. **Data visualization**: Several slides describe data (effect sizes, study counts) in text rather than showing it visually. Slides 3, 16, and 18 would benefit from small data visualizations integrated into the layout — even simple bar charts showing effect sizes would make the evidence more compelling (Principle 3).

8. **Mechanism diagrams**: Slides 4, 10, 11, and 14 describe mechanisms in text columns. These would be significantly stronger as flow diagrams with labeled arrows. The multi-channel convergence slide (12) especially — wood's five sensory channels would be powerful as a radial diagram.

9. **Consistency**: The color scheme and typography should be uniform. Currently some slides use colored boxes, others use plain text areas. The "Cognitive Science" palette from the Academic Presentation Service (midnight primary, amber accent) would work well given the audience.

### Speaker Notes

The speaker notes are the presentation's greatest asset. No changes recommended — they're appropriately detailed, cite sources, include rhetorical guidance, and carry the full intellectual content.

### Recommended Priority

If time permits for a V4 revision:
1. Add sentence headlines to slides 2 and 20 (quick fix, high impact)
2. Split slide 5 into two slides (moderate effort, reduces cognitive load)
3. Add mechanism flow diagrams to slides 4, 10-12 (significant effort, high visual impact)
4. Add small data visualizations to slides 3, 16, 18 (moderate effort, strengthens evidence display)
5. Revise title slide to pose a question (quick fix, improves opening)
