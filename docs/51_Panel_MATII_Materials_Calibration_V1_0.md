## Channel Separation for MAT4, Thermal Dose-Response for MAT1, and Cross-Modal Congruence Testing for MAT3

## Panel Charge

Panel MAT-I (Doc 37) produced five mechanistic templates: MAT1 (thermal adaptive PE), MAT2 (contact thermal PE), MAT3 (multi-modal material identity integration), MAT4 (natural material convergence — wood as canonical case), and MAT5 (material-cultural conditioning). Together with OLF1 (olfactory transition PE, Doc 46), these templates provide Article Eater with a mechanistic vocabulary for evaluating findings about materials and surfaces. The system can now identify which mechanism a material-related finding invokes — whether a study about wood is primarily engaging visual fractal fluency (T1 via MAT4 Channel 1), haptic warmth (MAT2 via Channel 2), acoustic absorption (AX1 via Channel 3), olfactory biophilic association (OLF1 via Channel 4), or cultural conditioning (MAT5 via Channel 5).

But as with L-I, the MAT-I templates are structurally complete and parametrically empty. When Article Eater encounters a finding reporting that "wood surfaces reduced stress by 15% relative to laminate," it can identify the plausible mechanisms but cannot determine their relative contributions. It cannot tell whether the study's laminate control adequately isolates the visual channel from the haptic. It cannot specify whether the thermal contact PE threshold was exceeded in the experimental conditions. It cannot quantify the olfactory contribution. These are calibration questions.

MAT-II's charge parallels L-II's: tune the framework that MAT-I built. Specifically:

1. **Separate MAT4 channels** — Which of wood's five channels (visual grain, haptic warmth, acoustic absorption, olfactory VOCs, biophilic association) contributes the most to the stress-reduction and preference effects? This directly parallels L-II's L3 channel-weight estimation.

2. **Calibrate MAT1 thermal dose-response** — What surface temperatures, air temperatures, and radiant asymmetries define the Goldilocks boundaries for thermal PE? Fanger's PMV model and de Dear's adaptive model provide the engineering framework; MAT-II must translate these into PE terms with explicit boundaries for comfort, pleasure, stimulation, and discomfort.

3. **Test MAT3 cross-modal congruence** — MAT3 predicts that material congruence across modalities (seeing wood AND feeling warmth AND hearing absorption) produces fluency, while incongruence (seeing wood but feeling cold plastic) produces PE. This prediction must be experimentally tested.

4. **Calibrate MAT2 contact thermal PE** — The C-tactile afferent pathway for affective touch is well-characterized neuroscientifically (McGlone et al., 2014). But the architectural implication — that TOUCHING surfaces matters for occupant wellbeing — needs dose-response specification: how much surface contact is needed? How long? Which body parts?

5. **Assess olfactory contribution** — OLF1 establishes that smell operates at transitions and encodes arrivals. MAT-II should assess how material odor contributes to material identity (MAT3) and whether eliminating the olfactory channel from wood (sealed/coated wood) measurably reduces the MAT4 convergence benefit.

6. **Extend to stone, concrete, and metal** — MAT4 formalizes wood as the canonical natural material convergence case. MAT-II should assess whether stone, concrete, and metal have their own convergence profiles or whether they are better described as partial-channel materials.

## Position Statements

### MAT4 CHANNEL SEPARATION: WHICH OF WOOD'S FIVE CHANNELS MATTERS MOST?

**Miyazaki**: My laboratory has conducted the closest existing approximation to the MAT4 channel-separation experiment. We have measured autonomic nervous system responses — heart rate variability, blood pressure, salivary cortisol — to wood stimuli presented through isolated sensory channels. The results are consistent but not yet definitive.

For visual wood: seeing real hinoki cypress panels (vs. white painted panels) produces a small but reliable parasympathetic activation — increased HF-HRV component, decreased systolic blood pressure (Tsunetsugu et al., 2007). The effect size is modest: d ≈ 0.3–0.5 on autonomic measures over 90-second exposures. The visual channel appears to engage primarily through fractal grain statistics (T1) and color warmth (COL2), consistent with MAT4 Channel 1.

For olfactory wood: smelling hinoki essential oil produces a LARGER parasympathetic response than seeing wood — decreased prefrontal cortex activity measured by near-infrared spectroscopy, increased HF-HRV, subjective feelings of comfort and natural-ness (Ikei et al., 2017). The effect size on autonomic measures is d ≈ 0.4–0.7. This surprised us. The olfactory channel may be more potent than the visual channel for stress reduction specifically, possibly because olfactory projections reach the amygdala without thalamic relay — a faster, less cognitively mediated pathway.

For tactile wood: touching unfinished hinoki surfaces (vs. marble, stainless steel, or vinyl) produces distinctive affective responses — subjects report warmth, comfort, and naturalness — with corresponding autonomic changes (Sakuragawa et al., 2005). The thermal conductivity difference is real: wood at room temperature feels warmer because its low thermal conductivity (λ ≈ 0.12–0.17 W/mK for softwoods) means it extracts heat from the hand slowly, while metal (λ ≈ 15–50) and stone (λ ≈ 1.5–3.0) extract heat rapidly. This is MAT2's contact thermal PE operating through the haptic channel.

What we have NOT done is the factorial combination. We have not measured the response to seeing wood + smelling wood + touching wood in a 2×2×2 design that would reveal whether the channels are additive, super-additive, or redundant. This is MAT-II's single most important calibration target.

**Spence**: I want to push Miyazaki on the interpretation. Your laboratory typically tests single-channel exposure for 90 seconds in a controlled booth. Architectural experience involves CONTINUOUS multi-modal exposure over hours. The laboratory paradigm isolates channels beautifully but may not capture the sustained integration that matters architecturally. We know from cross-modal research that congruent stimuli are processed more fluently than incongruent ones (Spence, 2011) — the warmth of wood's appearance PRIMES the expectation of tactile warmth, and confirmation of that expectation produces processing fluency that neither channel achieves alone. This is MAT3's congruence mechanism, and it only operates when channels are COMBINED.

**Pallasmaa**: And there is a temporal dimension that the laboratory misses entirely. In architecture, you do not smell wood for 90 seconds and then it stops. You LIVE with wood. The olfactory channel adapts — within 15 minutes, most people stop consciously smelling the VOCs (Dalton, 2000). But the visual and haptic channels do not adapt at the same rate. The multi-channel experience over hours has a different structure than the multi-channel experience over seconds. MAT-II must specify TIMESCALE-DEPENDENT channel weights: the olfactory channel may dominate during the first minutes of exposure (arrival, consistent with OLF1's transition function) but attenuate to near-zero contribution at steady state, while the visual and haptic channels sustain their contribution.

### MAT1 THERMAL DOSE-RESPONSE: WHERE ARE THE GOLDILOCKS BOUNDARIES?

**de Dear**: Let me be precise about what we know and what we lack. The adaptive comfort model (ASHRAE Standard 55, EN 16798-1) specifies the ACCEPTABLE temperature range — the range within which 80% or 90% of occupants report thermal acceptability. For naturally ventilated buildings, this range is roughly:

**Comfort band**: T_operative = 0.31 × T_running_mean_outdoor + 17.8°C (±3.5°C for 80% acceptability)

This means in a climate with mean outdoor temperature of 25°C, the comfort band is approximately 22.5–29.5°C. The PE framework asks a different question: where within this band is the occupant NEUTRAL (no PE), where are they experiencing POSITIVE PE (thermal pleasure, stimulation), and where are they experiencing NEGATIVE PE (discomfort)?

The adaptive model tells us that occupants are not simply seeking a thermal steady state — they accept and even PREFER some degree of thermal variability. De Dear (2011) formalized this as "thermal alliesthesia": the pleasure of thermal sensation depends not on the absolute temperature but on the DIRECTION of deviation from a (potentially uncomfortable) prior state. Entering a cool room from a hot outdoors feels pleasurable; entering the same cool room from a cold outdoors feels unpleasant. The PE framework captures this precisely: alliesthesia IS prediction error operating in the thermal domain.

But what I cannot specify — and what MAT-II must determine — is the SHAPE of the dose-response curve for thermal PE. Is thermal PE a step function (uncomfortable → comfortable → uncomfortable, with no gradient)? A linear gradient? An inverted-U with peak pleasure at some optimal deviation from neutral? The adaptive model was designed for building engineering — it specifies acceptable conditions, not optimal ones. The PE framework needs the optimization curve.

**McGlone**: The contact thermal dimension adds a second dose-response that the ambient thermal model ignores. When you TOUCH a surface, the thermal exchange depends on the surface's thermal effusivity (the square root of the product of thermal conductivity, density, and specific heat capacity). Wood has low effusivity (~300–500 J m⁻² K⁻¹ s⁻¹/²), stone is intermediate (~1,500–2,500), and metal is high (~7,000–15,000). At the same room temperature, these materials feel different because they extract heat from the skin at different rates.

The CT-afferent system is optimally responsive to stimuli at skin temperature (~32°C). A surface that maintains near-skin-temperature contact — like wood — produces a sustained, comfortable, mildly pleasant haptic signal. A surface that rapidly cools the skin below 28°C — like metal or stone at room temperature — triggers different mechanoreceptors (cold-sensitive Aδ fibers) that produce a qualitatively different sensation: alerting, mildly aversive, NOT processed through the affective-touch pathway.

The dose-response I can specify for contact thermal PE:
- **Pleasurable range**: surface contact temperature 28–36°C (centered on skin temperature 32°C)
- **Neutral range**: 24–28°C and 36–38°C
- **Aversive range**: below 24°C or above 38°C
- **Optimal stroke velocity for affective touch**: 1–10 cm/s, peak at ~3 cm/s (Löken et al., 2009)

These ranges predict that wood at typical indoor temperatures (20–25°C) sits at the edge of the pleasurable range for initial contact, warming toward skin temperature within seconds due to low conductivity. Metal and stone at the same temperatures sit in the neutral-to-aversive range and STAY there because they conduct heat away continuously.

### MAT3 CROSS-MODAL CONGRUENCE: WHAT HAPPENS WHEN CHANNELS DISAGREE?

**Spence**: MAT3's central prediction is testable and specific: when visual, haptic, thermal, acoustic, and olfactory properties of a material are CONGRUENT (all consistent with the same material identity), processing is fluent and affectively positive. When they are INCONGRUENT (e.g., a surface that looks like wood but feels like plastic), a prediction error is generated that produces a characteristic negative affective response — typically described as "uncanny," "cheap," or "fake."

The experimental paradigm is straightforward. I would design it as a series of material evaluation tasks:

**Congruent condition**: Real wood panel — looks like wood, feels like wood (warm, textured), sounds like wood (dull thud when tapped), smells like wood (if unfinished).
**Visual-haptic incongruence**: Wood-grain printed laminate — looks like wood but feels like plastic (smooth, thermally cold, no grain texture).
**Visual-thermal incongruence**: Wood panel heated or cooled to unnatural temperature — looks like wood, has wood texture, but feels thermally wrong.
**Visual-olfactory incongruence**: Real wood panel in a room with artificial (non-wood) scent.
**Full incongruence**: A surface that looks like wood but has the haptic, thermal, acoustic, and olfactory properties of metal.

The dependent measures should include: (a) explicit ratings (naturalness, pleasantness, quality, willingness-to-pay); (b) implicit measures (reaction time to classify the material — congruent materials should be classified FASTER); (c) physiological measures (skin conductance response at the moment of incongruent touch contact — the PE response).

MAT3 predicts a specific pattern: (1) congruent condition produces highest pleasantness and fastest classification; (2) each incongruent condition produces progressively more negative affect as the number of incongruent channels increases; (3) visual-haptic incongruence produces the LARGEST single-channel penalty because the vision-touch link has the highest prior precision (Spence, 2011) — we use visual appearance to predict tactile properties more than any other cross-modal pairing; (4) the full-incongruence condition produces the strongest PE and most negative response.

**Lindauer**: I can quantify the physical properties that determine congruence. For any material, I can specify:
- **Visual**: spectral reflectance, surface roughness (Ra), gloss level, fractal dimension of grain pattern
- **Haptic**: surface roughness (measured by profilometry), friction coefficient, thermal conductivity (λ)
- **Thermal**: thermal effusivity (b), which determines contact temperature trajectory
- **Acoustic**: sound absorption coefficient (α), impact sound level (Lp)
- **Olfactory**: VOC emission rate (μg/m³/h) and composition profile

The congruence "signature" for wood is: moderate roughness (Ra 5–50 μm), low gloss (<30 GU), fractal grain (D ≈ 1.2–1.4), low thermal conductivity (λ 0.10–0.20), moderate friction (μ 0.3–0.5), moderate absorption (α 0.05–0.15 at 500Hz), and characteristic terpene VOC profile. When all these properties co-occur, the brain receives convergent evidence for "wood" across all channels. When any property deviates — as in laminate (wrong thermal conductivity, wrong roughness, no VOCs) — the brain detects the mismatch.

### THE OLFACTORY CONTRIBUTION: DOES SEALED WOOD LOSE ITS BENEFIT?

**Miyazaki**: This question has direct architectural consequence. Most architectural wood is finished — varnished, lacquered, oiled, or sealed. Finishing dramatically reduces VOC emission: untreated hinoki emits ~50–100 μg/m³/h of α-pinene and other terpenes; polyurethane-sealed hinoki emits <5 μg/m³/h (Risholm-Sundman et al., 1998). If the olfactory channel contributes substantially to MAT4's convergence benefit, then modern finishing practices may be systematically degrading one of the most potent channels.

My laboratory data suggests the olfactory channel contributes roughly 25–35% of the total wood-specific physiological benefit during initial exposure (first 5 minutes). This estimate comes from comparing responses to visual-only wood (seeing wood panels behind glass) versus visual + olfactory wood (seeing panels in a room with wood scent) versus olfactory-only wood (smelling wood extract in a neutral visual environment). The olfactory channel produces the fastest autonomic response (onset within 2–3 breaths, vs. 10–15 seconds for visual), consistent with the direct amygdala projection pathway.

But — and this is the temporal qualification Pallasmaa raised — this 25–35% contribution represents INITIAL exposure. After 15–20 minutes of continuous exposure, the olfactory contribution drops to near zero due to adaptation (Dalton, 2000). The SUSTAINED contribution of smell to material experience is therefore an arrival effect (OLF1's transition function) rather than a steady-state effect. This has practical implications: the olfactory channel of wood contributes most at TRANSITIONS — entering a wood-paneled room, opening a wooden cabinet, handling a wooden object. The visual and haptic channels carry the sustained experience.

**Majid**: I need to challenge the universality assumption here. My cross-cultural research demonstrates that olfactory perception and categorization vary enormously across cultures. The Jahai people of the Malay Peninsula have a rich, abstract vocabulary for smell that English lacks entirely (Majid & Burenhult, 2014). Western cultures are relatively "olfactory impoverished" — we lack precise language for smells and typically identify odors by source ("smells like wood") rather than quality. Japanese culture occupies an intermediate position, with stronger olfactory attention than Western cultures (the "kōdō" incense appreciation tradition) but less abstract olfactory language than some Southeast Asian cultures.

The implication for MAT4: the olfactory channel's contribution may be systematically larger in cultures with greater olfactory attention and smaller in olfactory-impoverished cultures. If Miyazaki's 25–35% estimate comes from Japanese participants, it may overestimate the olfactory contribution for Western populations and underestimate it for some Southeast Asian populations. MAT-II should specify the olfactory channel weight as CULTURALLY MODERATED, with preliminary values for at least Western, Japanese, and high-olfactory cultures.

### EXTENDING BEYOND WOOD: STONE, CONCRETE, AND METAL

**Zumthor**: Wood is the canonical case because it scores well on every channel. But my buildings demonstrate that stone, concrete, and metal can create equally powerful material experiences through DIFFERENT channel profiles.

At Therme Vals, the Valser quartzite stone creates an experience that is — if I may use the phenomenological vocabulary — "geologically embodied." The stone is thermally cool to the touch (high effusivity), visually layered (the sedimentary strata create a natural linear texture), acoustically reflective (creating the distinctive sound environment of the thermal baths), and almost entirely non-olfactory. It engages three channels strongly (visual, haptic-thermal, acoustic) but not olfactory. And the QUALITY of the haptic-thermal experience is different from wood — where wood says "warm, alive, organic," stone says "cool, ancient, geological." Both can be experientially positive, but through different affective pathways.

At Bruder Klaus Field Chapel, the concrete interior retains the scorch marks of the burned-out tree trunks that formed the mould. This surface is haptic in a different way — rough, irregular, with a carbon-charcoal smell that persists years after construction. It is arguably the only building I know where concrete has a significant olfactory channel.

**Pallasmaa**: Zumthor's examples demonstrate what MAT-II must formalize: each material class has a characteristic CHANNEL PROFILE — a vector of weights across the five MAT channels that describes how that material engages the senses. Wood's channel profile is [high visual, high haptic-warm, moderate acoustic, moderate olfactory, moderate cultural]. Stone's profile is [high visual, high haptic-cool, high acoustic, zero olfactory, high cultural-geological]. Metal's profile is [moderate visual, high haptic-cold, high acoustic, zero olfactory, moderate cultural-industrial]. Concrete's profile is [moderate visual, moderate haptic, moderate acoustic, low olfactory, high cultural-modern].

The MAT4 template currently formalizes only wood's convergence. MAT-II should produce companion channel profiles — not full templates at first, but structured assessments — for stone, concrete, and metal. This would allow Article Eater to evaluate findings about ANY material against its expected channel profile.

## Cross-Examination and Calibration Debates

### Debate 1: Is Visual Appearance the Dominant Channel, or Have We Been Fooled by Experimental Convenience?

**Spence**: Let me be provocative. Nearly every study showing material preference effects uses VISUAL stimuli — photographs of wood, images of stone, renderings of interiors. Even Miyazaki's visual-only condition involves real panels, but the participant is seated behind a desk looking at a wall. The experimental literature is overwhelmingly visual because vision is easy to manipulate in a laboratory. But this means our evidence base is systematically biased toward Channel 1 (visual) and systematically undersamples Channels 2–5 (haptic, thermal, olfactory, acoustic). We may THINK the visual channel dominates because we have measured the visual channel most.

**McGlone**: I agree completely. The CT-afferent system has been studied almost exclusively in the context of interpersonal touch — a mother stroking a child's arm, a partner's caress. The idea that ARCHITECTURAL surfaces engage the same affective-touch pathway is theoretically motivated but almost untested. We know the CT afferents respond to any gentle, skin-temperature stroking stimulus regardless of whether the source is a human hand or a material surface. But nobody has conducted the architectural study: do people who routinely touch wooden surfaces (handrails, desks, countertops) show different affective responses than people who routinely touch laminate or metal?

**de Dear**: The thermal literature has the opposite bias. We have mountains of data on ambient thermal comfort — PMV studies in thousands of buildings across dozens of climates. But almost none of it addresses MATERIAL-SPECIFIC thermal experience. The thermal comfort models treat the building as an air-conditioning system; the surfaces are invisible. The radiant asymmetry standards (ISO 7730) specify limits for thermal discomfort from cold windows or hot ceilings but say nothing about the thermal PLEASURE of touching a warm wooden floor versus a cold tile floor. The contact thermal channel is architecturally ubiquitous and empirically invisible.

**Panel assessment**: The channel-weight estimates from existing data are tentative precisely because the evidence base has a vision bias. The 2×2×2 factorial design (visual × haptic × olfactory, with thermal controlled) is the single most important experiment MAT-II can recommend. Until that experiment exists, channel weights should be reported with explicit confidence intervals reflecting the asymmetry in available evidence.

### Debate 2: Are Material Preferences Cultural or Universal? The Wood Warmth Question

**Majid**: Let me sharpen the challenge. When Western subjects describe wood as "warm" and stone as "cold," they are using a METAPHORICAL extension of thermal vocabulary. But the physical reality is more nuanced: wood at 20°C DOES feel warmer than stone at 20°C (due to thermal effusivity), but this thermal difference is insufficient to explain the full magnitude of the preference. The word "warm" carries AFFECTIVE connotations — warm as in welcoming, familiar, homely — that are culturally constructed rather than physically determined.

In Japanese culture, wood associations include "alive" (生きている), "breathing" (呼吸する), and "aging beautifully" (美しく老いる) — a vocabulary that connects wood to temporal and biological metaphors rather than thermal ones. In many Scandinavian cultures, wood is associated with cultural identity, national tradition, and forest heritage. In Indian architecture — as David's fieldwork suggests — wood associations differ from Western ones: the wood-warmth metaphor is weaker in a culture where thermal comfort means COOL rather than warm for much of the year.

**Miyazaki**: But the PHYSIOLOGICAL responses in my laboratory are not culturally constructed. When Japanese subjects see or smell wood, their autonomic nervous system shifts toward parasympathetic dominance. This is not a metaphor — it is a measurable physiological change. The question is whether the same physiological response occurs in subjects from cultures with different wood associations.

**Majid**: That is exactly the question, and we do not have the data. Your subjects are Japanese. Japan has the world's strongest cultural relationship with wood — wooden temples, wooden houses, wooden baths, wooden crafts, the entire aesthetic tradition of wabi-sabi centered on wood aging. A Japanese person's physiological response to wood is the product of a lifetime of culturally shaped material experience. An urban Brazilian or Indian might show a completely different autonomic profile.

**Spence**: The experimental resolution is a cross-cultural replication of Miyazaki's wood-channel studies, sampling at minimum: (a) Japanese participants (Miyazaki's existing data), (b) Scandinavian participants (strong wood culture, different from Japanese), (c) Middle Eastern participants (stone-dominant architecture, limited wood tradition), (d) urban Indian participants (David's research context, high sensory intensity culture). If the autonomic responses converge across cultures, the physiological channels (MAT2, haptic-thermal) are universal and only the cultural-associative channel (MAT5) varies. If the autonomic responses DIVERGE, then even the supposedly "low-level" channels are culturally shaped — a finding that would require restructuring the entire MAT template system.

**Panel assessment**: The cultural universality question cannot be resolved by MAT-II alone but must be explicitly flagged. Current channel weights should be labeled "Western/Japanese populations" with the expectation that cross-cultural data will modify them. MAT5 (cultural conditioning) should be treated as a MULTIPLICATIVE moderator rather than an additive channel — it scales the weights of the other channels up or down based on cultural exposure history.

### Debate 3: Does Multi-Modal Material Convergence Require Conscious Attention, or Does It Operate Preattentively?

**Spence**: This is methodologically crucial for architectural application. If material convergence requires conscious attention — if you need to NOTICE the wood grain and CONSCIOUSLY register the warmth to get the benefit — then the effect only matters when people deliberately interact with surfaces. But if convergence operates preattentively — if the brain integrates material signals automatically even when attention is directed elsewhere (reading, conversation, work) — then the material environment matters CONTINUOUSLY, even when people are not thinking about it.

The multisensory integration literature supports preattentive processing. Congruent cross-modal signals are integrated automatically by the brain; you do not need to attend to both channels for the integration to occur (Driver, 2001). The binding of visual and auditory signals in the McGurk effect is automatic and cannot be suppressed by instruction. If material cross-modal integration follows the same principles, then the convergence benefit of wood operates as a BACKGROUND signal — continuously present, continuously integrated, continuously affecting the occupant's affective state without conscious mediation.

**McGlone**: The CT-afferent pathway provides a neuroanatomical argument for preattentive processing. CT afferents project to the posterior insular cortex, which is an interoceptive and affective processing area, NOT the primary somatosensory cortex (S1) that handles discriminative touch. This means affective touch information enters the brain through a DIFFERENT pathway than discriminative touch — one that is associated with emotional processing rather than conscious tactile perception. A person leaning against a wooden handrail may not consciously register the surface temperature or texture, but the CT-afferent pathway processes it anyway.

**Pallasmaa**: This aligns perfectly with the phenomenological argument. Atmosphere in architecture is precisely the experience of environmental qualities that are registered BELOW conscious awareness. ## Calibration Sessions

### Calibration Session 1: MAT4 Channel-Weight Estimation

**Spence** (Chair): Following L-II's methodology for L3 channel-weight estimation, we will attempt preliminary channel weights for MAT4 based on available evidence and structured expert judgment. The five channels, with our best estimates of their contribution to wood's overall wellbeing benefit:

**Channel 1: Visual grain (fractal fluency via T1)**

Evidence base: Strongest empirical base. Studies showing preference for wood-grain patterns with fractal dimension D ≈ 1.3 (Taylor et al., 2011). Occupant satisfaction studies correlating visible wood percentage with satisfaction ratings (Nyrud & Bringslimark, 2010, ~150 citations). Miyazaki's visual-only condition shows d ≈ 0.3–0.5 autonomic effect.

Weight estimate: **0.30** (±0.08)

Rationale: The visual channel has the most evidence, operates continuously (not just at transitions or during contact), and can be engaged without physical proximity. But it is also the channel most easily replicated by imitation materials (high-quality wood-grain laminates), suggesting that its unique contribution to REAL wood benefit may be smaller than its total contribution.

**Channel 2: Haptic warmth and texture (CT-afferent pathway via MAT2)**

Evidence base: Moderate. McGlone's CT-afferent literature provides the neural mechanism. Sakuragawa et al. (2005) show tactile preference for wood. Contact is intermittent — only during hand contact with surfaces.

Weight estimate: **0.20** (±0.10)

Rationale: Strong theoretical basis from the CT-afferent literature but limited architectural evidence. The intermittent contact pattern (touching handrails, desks, doors — perhaps 5–10% of waking time in a building) reduces the duty cycle compared to visual exposure (100% of waking time while eyes are open in the space).

**Channel 3: Acoustic absorption (via AX1)**

Evidence base: Weak. Wood absorbs sound moderately (α ≈ 0.05–0.15 depending on type and finishing), contributing to room acoustics. No studies isolate the acoustic contribution of wood surfaces to wellbeing independently from other acoustic treatments.

Weight estimate: **0.10** (±0.08)

Rationale: The acoustic channel of wood is real but small relative to dedicated acoustic treatments (fabric, mineral fiber). Its contribution is primarily through the ABSENCE of harsh reflections rather than a positive acoustic quality. A wood-paneled room sounds different from a concrete room, but the acoustic difference is confounded with visual, thermal, and olfactory differences in every existing study.

**Channel 4: Olfactory (terpene VOCs via OLF1)**

Evidence base: Moderate-to-strong for initial exposure, weak for sustained exposure. Miyazaki estimates 25–35% contribution during first 5 minutes. Adaptation reduces contribution to near-zero after 15–20 minutes.

Weight estimate: **0.15** (±0.10) — but this is a TIME-AVERAGED weight

Rationale: The olfactory channel is potent but transient. Its contribution is best modeled as a transition effect (high at arrival, near-zero at steady state) rather than a constant weight. For an occupant who enters a wood-paneled room once and stays for hours, the time-averaged olfactory contribution is small. For an occupant who TRANSITIONS through multiple wood-containing spaces during a day, the cumulative olfactory contribution is larger. This connects directly to OLF1's transition function: the olfactory channel of MAT4 resets each time the occupant leaves and re-enters.

**Channel 5: Biophilic-cultural association (ecological familiarity via MAT5)**

Evidence base: Moderate but culturally variable. Kellert's biophilic design framework posits universal attraction to natural materials. Majid's cross-cultural work challenges the universality claim. The biophilic contribution is theoretically present but its magnitude is culturally conditioned.

Weight estimate: **0.25** (±0.12) — culturally variable; this is a Western/Japanese estimate

Rationale: This is the most uncertain channel weight because it is the most culturally dependent. In cultures with strong wood traditions (Japan, Scandinavia, Pacific Northwest), the biophilic-cultural contribution may be higher. In cultures where stone or earth is the dominant natural building material, wood's biophilic signal may be weaker.

**Total**: 0.30 + 0.20 + 0.10 + 0.15 + 0.25 = 1.00

**Super-additivity estimate**: Based on L-II's finding that L3 daylight convergence shows ~15–25% super-additivity, and given that material convergence involves more sensory modalities (5 vs. L3's 5 channels but with 3 truly independent senses), MAT-II estimates super-additivity of **20–30%** for real wood versus the sum of individual channel contributions.

**Prediction**: A full factorial study (visual × haptic × olfactory, with acoustic and cultural controlled) would show that the three-channel combination produces a wellbeing effect 20–30% larger than the sum of the three individual-channel effects. If the super-additivity is less than 10%, the convergence model adds little beyond individual channels. If greater than 40%, the interaction terms dominate and individual channel weights are misleading.

### Calibration Session 2: MAT1 Thermal Goldilocks Boundaries

**de Dear**: Let me translate the adaptive comfort model into PE terms. The key insight is that thermal PE has TWO components that must be calibrated separately:

**Component 1: Ambient thermal PE** (deviation from adaptive neutral)

The adaptive neutral temperature is the temperature the occupant EXPECTS based on recent thermal history: T_n = 0.31 × T_running_mean_outdoor + 17.8°C

PE is the deviation from this expectation. The Goldilocks boundaries:

| Zone | Deviation from T_n | PE Magnitude | Affective Valence | Architectural Implication |
|------|-------------------|--------------|-------------------|--------------------------|
| Neutral | ±1°C | Near-zero | Neutral | Standard HVAC target |
| Positive PE (alliesthesia) | ±1–3°C | Moderate | Positive IF directionally corrective | Design for controlled thermal variation |
| Tolerance | ±3–5°C | Moderate-high | Neutral to mildly negative | Acceptable in naturally ventilated buildings |
| Discomfort | >±5°C | High | Negative | Design failure |

The critical insight: the "Positive PE" zone produces PLEASURE only when the deviation is DIRECTIONALLY CORRECTIVE — cooling when the prior state was warm, warming when the prior state was cold. This is de Dear's (2011) alliesthesia formalization. A building that maintains strict thermal neutrality (deviation ≈ 0) eliminates thermal PE entirely — no discomfort but also no thermal pleasure. A building that allows controlled thermal DRIFT or provides thermally diverse zones (a cool stone corridor, a warm wood-paneled library) creates opportunities for positive thermal PE at transitions.

**Component 2: Contact thermal PE** (surface-to-skin heat flow)

| Zone | Surface Effusivity (b) | Contact Sensation | PE Type | Material Examples |
|------|----------------------|-------------------|---------|-------------------|
| Warm-pleasant | < 500 J m⁻² K⁻¹ s⁻¹/² | Warm, maintained near skin temp | Positive (CT-afferent activation) | Wood, cork, fabric, leather |
| Neutral | 500–1,500 | Cool initially, moderating | Low PE | Plaster, drywall, most plastics |
| Cool-alerting | 1,500–3,000 | Cool, sustained | Moderate negative (or positive in hot climates) | Stone, ceramic tile, concrete |
| Cold-aversive | > 7,000 | Cold, sustained | High negative | Metal (steel, aluminum) |

**de Dear**: Note the climate-culture interaction. In hot climates — India, Southeast Asia, Middle East — contact with high-effusivity materials (stone, tile) is DESIRED because the cooling effect is thermally corrective (positive alliesthesia). The "cold-aversive" zone for a Scandinavian occupant in winter is the "cool-pleasurable" zone for an Indian occupant in summer. The PE framework handles this correctly because the sign of the PE depends on the direction of deviation from the EXPECTED thermal state, not on the absolute temperature.

**Schechtner**: Can we put instrumentation on this? My sensor platform can measure surface temperatures continuously, but we need occupant-level measurement of contact frequency and duration. I would propose a study protocol: instrumenting a mixed-material building (wood and stone surfaces) with surface temperature loggers and video-tracking occupant touch behavior. We could estimate per-occupant contact thermal PE exposure over a full workday and correlate it with end-of-day wellbeing assessments.

### Calibration Session 3: MAT3 Cross-Modal Congruence Testing Protocol

### Opening Remarks from the Chair

**Spence**: MAT-II has accomplished something that clarifies both the specific and the general. Specifically, we now have preliminary channel weights for wood (0.30 visual / 0.20 haptic / 0.10 acoustic / 0.15 olfactory / 0.25 biophilic-cultural) and initial channel profiles for stone, concrete, and metal. But the general insight is more important.

### Material Experience Is Continuous, Preattentive, and Cumulative

**McGlone**: The convergence of evidence from the CT-afferent literature, the multisensory integration literature, and the phenomenological argument points to a single conclusion: material experience operates BELOW the threshold of conscious attention for most of the time occupants spend in a building. You do not consciously register the warmth of the wooden handrail, the acoustic softness of the carpeted corridor, or the thermal bite of the steel door handle. But your brain processes all of these signals through affective pathways (insular cortex, amygdala) that modulate your mood, stress level, and sense of comfort without conscious mediation.

This has a radical implication for architectural practice: material selection is not an aesthetic choice that matters only to the extent that people NOTICE it. It is an environmental health variable that operates continuously, like air quality or lighting level. A building with thermally diverse materials (warm wood, cool stone) provides a richer material-sensory environment than a building with uniform materials (all drywall, all laminate), even if no occupant ever consciously registers the difference.

**Pallasmaa**: This vindicates the phenomenological argument I have made for thirty years. The critique of "ocularcentrism" was always about this: architects design for the eye because the eye is the conscious sense, the sense that produces demonstrable preference judgments in surveys and photographs. But the body lives in the building continuously, and the body registers materials through touch, temperature, sound, and smell long after the eye has habituated. A building designed only for visual impact is like a meal designed only for visual presentation — it may photograph well but it nourishes poorly.

### The Convergence Triad Is Now Complete — with Calibration Differences

**Spence**: With MAT-II's channel-weight estimates, the convergence triad — L3 (daylight), MAT4 (wood), VIEW1 (nature views) — is now parameterized at the same level of specificity. Comparison:

This temporal complexity makes material experience harder to measure but potentially richer in its cumulative effect over a full day in a building.

## Testable Predictions and Critical Experiments

### Prediction 1: The Wood Channel Factorial

**Miyazaki**: The highest-priority experiment. A 2×2×2 factorial design (visual wood vs. non-wood × haptic wood vs. non-wood × olfactory wood vs. non-wood), with acoustic and ambient thermal controlled, measuring autonomic responses (HRV, blood pressure, cortisol), explicit preference, and reaction-time measures of processing fluency.

**Specific predictions**:
- Each channel produces a main effect on autonomic parasympathetic activation (d ≈ 0.3–0.5 for visual, d ≈ 0.3–0.6 for haptic, d ≈ 0.4–0.7 for olfactory)
- The three-channel combination produces an effect that exceeds the sum of main effects by 20–30% (the super-additivity test)
- The visual × haptic interaction is the largest two-way interaction (because vision-touch has the highest prior precision for material evaluation)
- Olfactory contribution shows a TIME × CHANNEL interaction: olfactory main effect is large at 0–5 minutes and near-zero at 20+ minutes

**Falsification**: If no super-additivity is observed (combined ≤ sum of parts), then MAT4's convergence model is wrong and wood benefits are adequately explained by individual channels operating independently. If the olfactory channel shows NO time-dependent adaptation, then OLF1's transition model is wrong for material odors.

### Prediction 2: Cross-Modal Congruence Penalty

**Spence**: The MAT3 congruence experiment. Participants interact with material samples varying in visual-haptic-thermal congruence (8 conditions from full congruence to full incongruence).

**Specific predictions**:
- Full congruence (real wood) outperforms full incongruence (wood-look laminate with metal thermal backing) by d ≈ 0.8–1.2 on pleasantness
- Haptic incongruence alone (wood-look laminate, correct temperature) produces a LARGER penalty than visual incongruence alone (laminate-look real wood, correct texture and temperature), because haptic confirmation is the higher-precision channel for material identification
- Skin conductance response at moment of incongruent touch is larger than at moment of congruent touch (the PE signal), with latency < 500ms
- The incongruence penalty is LARGER for materials with strong identity (wood, stone) than for materials with weak identity (generic plastic, painted drywall), because strong-identity materials generate more precise prior predictions

**Falsification**: If haptic incongruence produces a SMALLER penalty than visual incongruence, then the prior-precision model of cross-modal material evaluation is wrong — vision dominates material evaluation even for confirmatory channels.

### Prediction 3: Contact Thermal PE Follows McGlone's CT-Afferent Boundaries

**McGlone**: A direct test of whether architectural surface contact engages the CT-afferent pathway.

**Test design**: Participants touch surfaces of varying thermal effusivity (wood, plaster, stone, metal) at varying surface temperatures (18°C, 22°C, 26°C, 30°C, 34°C) while fMRI or EEG measures cortical activation patterns.

**Specific predictions**:
- Surfaces producing skin-contact temperatures in the 28–36°C range (CT-afferent optimum) activate the posterior insular cortex (affective touch pathway), while surfaces producing temperatures outside this range activate the somatosensory cortex (discriminative touch pathway)
- Wood at room temperature (20–22°C) produces contact temperatures in the warm-pleasant range (28–32°C after 3 seconds) due to low effusivity, and shows insular cortex activation
- Metal at the same room temperature produces contact temperatures below 28°C and shows somatosensory cortex activation WITHOUT insular cortex activation
- This dissociation is the neural signature of the affective vs. discriminative touch distinction and validates MAT2's two-pathway model

**Falsification**: If all materials produce the same cortical activation pattern regardless of thermal effusivity, then the CT-afferent pathway is not selectively engaged by architectural surfaces and MAT2's affective-touch mechanism needs revision.

### Prediction 4: Cultural Moderation of Channel Weights

**Majid**: A cross-cultural replication of Miyazaki's wood-channel studies.

**Test design**: The wood channel factorial (Prediction 1) replicated with participants from at minimum four cultural groups: Japanese, Scandinavian, Middle Eastern, and Indian. Same protocol, same measures.

**Specific predictions**:
- Visual channel weights are MOST consistent across cultures (d variation < 0.15 across groups) because fractal fluency is a low-level perceptual mechanism
- Olfactory channel weights are MODERATELY variable (d variation 0.15–0.30) because olfactory attention and categorization varies by culture
- Biophilic-cultural channel weights are MOST variable (d variation > 0.30) because cultural material associations differ fundamentally
- The haptic-thermal channel weight is culturally moderated by CLIMATE rather than CULTURE per se: participants from hot climates prefer cool-effusivity materials, participants from cold climates prefer warm-effusivity materials, independent of cultural background

**Falsification**: If all channel weights are culturally invariant (no significant group differences), then the cultural moderation model is wrong and material experience is primarily biological. If the visual channel varies as much as the cultural channel, then even "low-level" perception is culturally shaped.

## Panel MAT-III Prospectus: Priorities for a Follow-Up Materials Panel

**Spence**: MAT-II has accomplished calibration-level improvements for MAT1 (substantially calibrated), MAT2 (partially calibrated), and MAT4 (partially calibrated), while providing experimental protocols for MAT3 and MAT5 that await execution. A third materials panel would prioritize data collection on the experiments we have designed, plus extension to domains MAT-II did not reach.

#### DEEPENING — Calibrating and Parameterizing MAT1–MAT5

**Deepening Priority 1: Execute the MAT4 Channel Factorial**

The 2×2×2 factorial (visual × haptic × olfactory wood) is the single highest-priority experiment across the entire template system. It directly tests the convergence model that underpins MAT4, L3, and VIEW1. MAT-III should review results of this experiment — if conducted in the interim — or commission it as a collaborative study.

**What the panel would produce**: Empirically validated channel weights for MAT4 with confidence intervals; empirical super-additivity estimate; time-dependent olfactory contribution function.

**Panelists who would return**: Miyazaki (physiological measures), Spence (cross-modal design), Lindauer (material specification).

**Additional panelist needed**: Wastiels (Vrije Universiteit Brussel, material perception in architecture, ~200 citations) — has conducted some of the only factorial material-perception studies in architectural settings.

**Deepening Priority 2: Execute the MAT3 Congruence Experiment**

The 8-condition congruence experiment tests whether cross-modal material incongruence produces measurable PE with specific effect sizes and the predicted channel hierarchy (haptic > visual > thermal).

**What the panel would produce**: Empirical congruence penalty magnitudes; validated channel hierarchy for material evaluation; physiological PE signature (skin conductance latency and amplitude) for material incongruence.

**Panelists who would return**: Spence (experimental design), Lindauer (material sample fabrication), McGlone (affective touch measures).

**Additional panelist needed**: Tiest (VU Amsterdam, material perception through touch, ~1,500 citations) — has conducted systematic studies of haptic material perception that could inform the congruence experiment.

**Deepening Priority 3: Neuroimaging Validation of Architectural Surface Contact**

Prediction 3's fMRI/EEG study testing whether touching architectural materials at different temperatures selectively engages the insular cortex (affective pathway) vs. somatosensory cortex (discriminative pathway).

**What the panel would produce**: Neural validation of MAT2's two-pathway model for architectural surfaces; empirical boundary values for the affective-touch temperature range in architectural contact.

**Panelists who would return**: McGlone (CT-afferent neuroscience), Miyazaki (physiological measures in material studies).

**Additional panelist needed**: Morrison (CNRS Lyon, affective touch neuroimaging, ~3,000 citations) — leading neuroimager of the CT-afferent pathway.

**Deepening Priority 4: Non-Wood Material Profile Empirical Validation**

The stone, concrete, and metal channel profiles are currently expert judgment. Systematic preference and physiological studies with controlled single-channel exposure to these materials would replace expert judgment with empirical weights.

**What the panel would produce**: Empirically grounded channel profiles for stone, concrete, and metal; material-specific super-additivity estimates.

**Panelists who would return**: Zumthor (architectural calibration), Pallasmaa (phenomenological vocabulary), Brotas (light-material interaction).

**Additional panelist needed**: Karana (TU Delft, materials experience lab, ~2,000 citations) — has developed systematic methods for characterizing the experiential properties of materials.

#### BROADENING — Extending to New Mechanisms, Populations, and Domains

**Broadening Priority 1: Cross-Cultural Calibration (Prediction 4)**

Execute the cross-cultural replication of the wood channel factorial with participants from Japanese, Scandinavian, Middle Eastern, and Indian cultural contexts. This is critical for making the template system globally applicable rather than Western/Japanese-specific.

**What the panel would produce**: Culture-specific channel weight modifiers for MAT4; validation or revision of MAT5's multiplicative moderator model; climate-vs-culture dissociation for thermal channel.

**Panelists who would return**: Majid (cross-cultural perception), Miyazaki (protocol standardization), de Dear (climate-thermal interaction).

**Additional panelist needed**: Researchers from the target cultures — potentially Gupta or Indraganti (thermal comfort in Indian buildings, ~500 citations combined) for the Indian context; Haddad (thermal comfort in Middle Eastern architecture, ~200 citations) for the Middle Eastern context.

**Broadening Priority 2: Material Experience Across the Lifespan**

MAT1–MAT5 assume adult occupants with normative sensory function. But thermal sensitivity changes with age (reduced peripheral vasoconstriction shifts the comfort band), CT-afferent density declines in aging skin (potentially reducing the affective touch response), and cultural material associations accumulate over decades. A lifespan extension would add AGE as a formalized moderator across MAT1, MAT2, and MAT5.

**What the panel would produce**: Age-adjusted thermal comfort boundaries for MAT1; age-dependent CT-afferent response curves for MAT2; developmental trajectory of material-cultural associations for MAT5.

**Panelists who would return**: McGlone (CT-afferent aging data), de Dear (thermal comfort in elderly populations).

**Additional panelist needed**: Ackerley (University of Gothenburg, developmental changes in affective touch, ~1,000 citations) — has studied CT-afferent function across the lifespan.

**Broadening Priority 3: Sustainable Materials and Novel Bio-Materials**

The template system addresses traditional materials (wood, stone, concrete, metal) but emerging architectural materials — cross-laminated timber (CLT), rammed earth, mycelium composites, recycled plastics, 3D-printed concrete — have novel channel profiles that may not fit existing templates. A broadening effort would assess whether these materials require new templates or can be adequately described by existing channel profiles with modified parameters.

**What the panel would produce**: Channel profiles for 5–8 emerging material types; assessment of whether the existing five-channel framework is sufficient or needs extension.

**Panelists who would return**: Lindauer (material property measurement), Brotas (light-material interaction).

**Additional panelist needed**: Block (ETH Zurich, digital fabrication and novel construction materials, ~2,000 citations) — would bring the emerging-materials perspective.

MAT-II lifts A1 and A7 to ★★★★. If MAT-III accomplishes Deepening Priorities 1–2, the MAT4 convergence model and MAT3 congruence model would be empirically validated with effect sizes, moving both templates from "partially calibrated" to "substantially calibrated." Broadening Priority 1 would make the template system cross-culturally applicable. Together, these would consolidate A1 and A7 at solid ★★★★ and potentially approach ★★★★★ on the strongest mechanisms (MAT1, MAT2).

## Scope Exclusions — Work That Belongs Elsewhere

**Spence**: The following topics arose during the panel but belong in other panels:

**1. Light-material interaction optimization** — Brotas raised the critical point that material visual properties are light-dependent. But optimizing the LIGHTING of materials is an L-series question (L1 luminance contrast interacts with material reflectance), not a MAT-series question. The cross-reference should note the interaction, but the calibration work belongs in a joint L-III/MAT-III session or a dedicated cross-modal panel.

**2. Acoustic material specification** — The acoustic channel of materials (absorption, reflection, impact sound) is covered by the M-series (acoustic templates) and AX1 (cross-modal bridging). MAT-II addressed the acoustic channel's CONTRIBUTION to material convergence (the weight within MAT4) but did not attempt to calibrate acoustic material properties per se. That belongs in a future Acoustic Calibration panel (M-II).

**3. Material toxicity and indoor air quality** — VOC emissions from materials affect indoor air quality and occupant health. OLF1 addresses the PE dimension of olfactory experience, but the toxicological dimension (formaldehyde from composites, isocyanates from polyurethane) is a public health question that exceeds the PE framework's scope. Material health certification systems (Declare, HPD, Cradle-to-Cradle) address this.

**4. Material sustainability and environmental impact** — Life-cycle assessment, embodied carbon, recyclability, and circular economy principles are essential architectural considerations but not cognitive/perceptual ones. The template system evaluates how materials affect OCCUPANTS, not how they affect the PLANET. These are complementary but separate evaluation frameworks.

**5. Digital and smart materials** — Responsive materials (thermochromic coatings, phase-change materials, electrochromic glazing) that change properties dynamically belong partly in the TP-series (temporal dynamics) and partly in a future Smart Materials panel. MAT-II addresses static material properties.
