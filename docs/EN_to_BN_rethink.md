of background perturbations and fail outside that range. Pearl
represents all causal relationships identically (arrows + structural
equations), but Woodward insists invariance range is constitutive of the
relationship itself. The key move: the EWG\'s discount
factors *operationalize* Woodward\'s invariance for environmental
psychology. CONSTITUTIVE = maximally invariant (definitional, holds
everywhere). MECHANISM = invariant across mechanism-preserving contexts.
EMPIRICAL\_COVARIANCE = invariant across observed contexts but unknown
otherwise. ANALOGICAL = invariant to the extent analogy holds.
THEORETICAL\_DEFAULT = invariance entirely speculative. The ordering is
not arbitrary --- it tracks expected invariance range. And the clincher:
invariance assessment *requires provenance*. You can\'t assess
invariance range without knowing what sustains the relationship. Pearl
can\'t get there from structural equations alone.

Disentangled epistemic beliefs from causal probabilities across
theoretical frameworks

This is a genuinely important question and I want to work through it
carefully because you\'re putting your finger on a distinction that, if
muddled, would undermine the entire architecture. Let me take the pieces
in order.

**The naming question first.** \"Credence\" is the standard term in
formal epistemology (following de Finetti, Jeffrey, Joyce) for a
rational agent\'s degree of belief in a proposition. But I think you\'re
right to want distance from it, because in the EWG context it risks
confusion with the CPT values in the BN. My recommendation would
be **\"evidential confidence\"** or simply **\"confidence\"** --- the
symbol ω already suggests this. The number ω ∈ (0,1) attached to an edge
in the EWG represents: *how confident should we be, given the available
evidence, that this particular evidential bridge holds?* It is not a
probability in the frequentist sense. It is not a conditional
probability. It is a measure of how well-supported a specific claim is
by specific evidence of a specific type.

\"Believability\" has the right intuitive feel but sounds informal for a
formal system. \"Credibility\" is good but has a technical meaning in
Dempster-Shafer theory that could create crosstalk. \"Current degree of
belief\" is accurate but wordy. I\'d go with **\"evidential
confidence\"** (or just \"confidence weight\" ω) for the formal system
and use \"degree of belief\" in prose exposition. But you should pick
what resonates --- the key is that whatever we call it, it is
categorically different from a conditional probability.

**Now the hard question: what is the relationship between ω and the
CPTs?**

Here\'s how I\'d frame the distinction. There are two completely
different questions being answered:

The EWG edge from A to C with warrant type τ and confidence ω
answers: **\"How well-supported is the claim that A bears relation τ to
C?\"** This is a second-order, meta-level judgment. It is about
the *evidence for a relationship*, not about the relationship itself.
When we say ω = 0.85 on a MECHANISM edge from daylight to serotonin, we
mean: the evidence that this mechanistic pathway exists is strong
(Lambert et al. identified it, it\'s been replicated, the biochemistry
is understood). We are not saying anything yet about how *much* daylight
changes serotonin levels, or in what functional form, or with what
variance across individuals.

The CPT in the BN from A to C answers: **\"Given that A takes value a,
what is the probability distribution over C?\"** This is a first-order,
object-level claim. It is about *what happens in the world*. When the BN
says P(serotonin = elevated \| daylight = high) = 0.72, it means: in the
actual population, in the actual context, 72% of people exposed to high
daylight show elevated serotonin. This is an empirical claim about
frequencies or propensities, not a meta-level judgment about evidence
quality.

The projection function π is the bridge. It takes the meta-level
information (τ, ω, d) and uses it to *modulate* the object-level CPTs.
The formula --- working in log-odds, multiplying by the discount factor
d(τ) --- has a specific meaning: the discount factor determines how much
of the lab-derived CPT survives transfer to the target context. A
MECHANISM edge with d = 0.80 transmits 80% of the log-odds deviation
from ignorance (the prior of 0.50). An ANALOGICAL edge with d = 0.40
transmits only 40%. The confidence weight ω further modulates: if we\'re
only moderately confident in the evidence (ω = 0.65), that attenuation
compounds with the type discount.

So: **ω tells you how good the evidence is. The CPT tells you what the
evidence says. The discount d tells you how much of what the evidence
says survives transfer to a new context.** Three different numbers doing
three different jobs.

**Now your observation about the EWG having many more edges than the BN
--- this is exactly right and deeply important.**

The EWG is an *epistemic* graph. It represents everything we know or
believe about the relationships in a domain, at every level of
description. It can include:

Observable-to-observable edges (daylight exposure → reported mood)

Observable-to-latent edges (daylight → retinal ganglion activation)

Latent-to-latent edges (retinal ganglion activation → raphe nuclei
activity → tryptophan hydroxylase upregulation)

Theoretical/computational-level edges (stimulus complexity → predictive
coding prediction error → surprise signal → arousal)

Cross-level edges connecting Marr\'s (1982) computational, algorithmic,
and implementational descriptions

The BN is an *operational* graph. It represents only the variables that
the architect can measure, manipulate, or care about. It projects from
the EWG by collapsing intermediate states. The architect doesn\'t need
nodes for \"raphe nuclei activity\" or \"predictive coding prediction
error.\" The architect needs: if I set the window-to-wall ratio to X,
what is the probability distribution over patient mood? Over recovery
time? Over occupant satisfaction?

The projection π is therefore not just a numerical transformation --- it
is also a *structural* transformation. It collapses paths in the EWG
into single edges in the BN, computing the effective CPT for a direct BN
edge from the chain of EWG edges that it summarizes. A BN edge from
\"daylight\" to \"mood\" might summarize an EWG path that runs through
five intermediate neurochemical states, each with its own warrant type
and confidence. The minimum-discount composition principle tells you the
effective warrant quality of the collapsed path. The CPT computation
aggregates the functional relationships along the chain.

**Confounds and interactions --- this is where the distinction gets
really sharp.**

In the BN, a confound is a variable that is a common cause of two other
variables: if Z causes both A and C, then A and C will be correlated
even if A does not cause C. Pearl\'s framework handles confounds through
the back-door criterion, d-separation, and the do-calculus. But notice:
to handle a confound in the BN, *the confound must be a node in the BN*.
If the confound is unmeasured, Pearl\'s framework can sometimes still
identify the causal effect (using instrumental variables or the
front-door criterion), but only if the graph structure is known.

In the EWG, a confound shows up differently. It shows up as a *property
of the warrant type*. When an edge is typed EMPIRICAL\_COVARIANCE, the
type itself carries the information that the relationship *may be
confounded* --- that\'s part of what it means to have only correlational
evidence. When an edge is typed MECHANISM, the type carries the
information that confounds are less threatening --- because the
mechanism identifies the causal pathway, any confounder would need to
operate through that pathway or through a parallel pathway that produces
the same pattern. The EWG doesn\'t need to enumerate all possible
confounders as nodes; the warrant type is a summary statistic for
confounding risk.

An interaction (in the statistical sense: A\'s effect on C depends on
the level of B) is a feature of the functional form of the structural
equation: C = f(A, B, U) where f is not separable in A and B. In the BN,
this appears in the CPT: P(C \| A, B) ≠ P(C \| A) · P(C \| B). In the
EWG, an interaction might appear as a separate edge from B to the A→C
relationship, or as a modifier on the A→C edge\'s confidence weight (the
evidence for A→C was gathered at a specific level of B; if B differs in
the target context, the confidence should be lower). The EWG can
represent the *epistemic status* of an interaction --- do we know about
it? How well-established is it? --- in ways the BN cannot.

**Finally: the theory/mechanism distinction, which is perhaps the
deepest question you\'re raising.**

A *mechanism*, in the Machamer, Darden, and Craver (2000) sense, is a
specific organized system of entities and activities that produces a
specific phenomenon. The serotonergic pathway from retina to raphe
nuclei is a mechanism. It has parts (retinal ganglion cells, the
retinohypothalamic tract, the raphe nuclei, tryptophan hydroxylase). It
has activities (phototransduction, axonal transmission, enzymatic
catalysis). It produces a specific effect (serotonin synthesis).
Mechanisms are in the world --- they are spatiotemporally located, they
can be intervened on, they can break.

A *theory*, in the sense you\'re gesturing at, is a higher-level
explanatory framework that explains *why* the mechanism exists
and *why* it has the form it does. Predictive coding theory (Friston,
2010; Clark, 2013) doesn\'t specify a particular mechanism. It says:
brains minimize prediction error; therefore, any neural system that
processes sensory input should show certain computational properties
(prediction, error signals, precision weighting). Specific mechanisms
--- the serotonergic pathway, the dopaminergic reward system, the
cortical hierarchy --- are *instances* of the theory\'s predictions. The
theory doesn\'t cause the instances; it explains them.

This maps onto Marr\'s (1982) three levels. The theory operates at the
computational level: what problem is the system solving? The mechanism
operates at the implementational level: what neural hardware implements
the solution? There may also be an algorithmic level: what
representations and processes does the system use?

Why does this distinction matter for the EWG?

Because a theory-level claim and a mechanism-level claim have different
warrant types, different transfer properties, and different roles in the

**EN vs EWG.** I agree --- **Epistemic Network** (EN) is the right name.
EN vs BN is immediately intuitive: one is about what
we *know* (epistemic), the other is about what we *predict* (Bayesian).
The word \"warrant\" was doing useful philosophical work (connecting to
Toulmin), but it can live inside the EN as the name for the edge
annotations rather than the whole graph. So: the **Epistemic
Network** contains edges annotated with **warrant types** and **warrant
strengths**. Clean.

**Is the EN Bayesian?** No, and we should be upfront about this. The EN
is Bayesian in one narrow sense: the confidence weights ω can be
interpreted as subjective probabilities (degrees of belief), which is a
Bayesian concept going back to de Finetti (1937) and Ramsey (1926). But
the EN is *not* Bayesian in three important senses. First, it is not a
Bayesian Network in Pearl\'s technical sense --- it permits cycles,
parallel edges, and typed edges, none of which BNs allow. Second, it
does not satisfy the Markov condition --- the conditional independence
structure that defines a BN does not apply to the EN, because the EN\'s
edges represent evidential support, not causal or probabilistic
dependence. Third, the update rule for ω when new evidence arrives is
not necessarily Bayesian conditionalization --- it may involve
qualitative judgments about evidence quality that don\'t reduce to
likelihood ratios. The EN is better described as
a **coherentist** epistemic structure (following Quine\'s web of belief)
that *interfaces with* a Bayesian computational structure (the BN)
through the projection function π. The EN is epistemology; the BN is
probability theory. They are governed by different norms.

**EMPIRICAL\_COVARIANCE naming.** I\'d push back on \"Confound Risk\"
--- for the reason you almost caught yourself: it names
the *vulnerability* rather than the *evidence*. The warrant type should
describe what you *have*, not what you *lack*. What you have is a
replicated statistical association. What you lack is a mechanism
explaining it. The confounding vulnerability is a *consequence* of the
type, not the type itself. Similarly, \"Raw Correlation\" implies the
correlation hasn\'t been processed, but these are often carefully
controlled studies with sophisticated statistical analysis --- they\'re
just not mechanistic.

My recommendation: **EMPIRICAL\_ASSOCIATION**. It says exactly what the
evidence is (an empirically observed association), it doesn\'t prejudge
the analysis quality (not \"raw\"), and it naturally contrasts with
MECHANISM (we see the association but don\'t know the mechanism).
\"Covariance\" is technically more precise but less intuitive for
non-statisticians. \"Correlation\" works but has baggage (\"correlation
is not causation\" makes it sound dismissive). \"Association\" is the
epidemiological term of art --- it\'s what you report when you have
evidence of a relationship but aren\'t claiming causation. I think this
works better.

**Interactions: should we explicitly identify them?** This is a design
decision with real tradeoffs. If we add interaction edges (A×B → C,
meaning A\'s effect on C depends on B\'s level), then yes, the EN gets
much denser. For N variables, the number of possible pairwise
interactions is O(N²), and each interaction could have its own warrant
type and strength. The EN could easily triple in edge count.

My recommendation: interactions should be represented but not as a
separate edge type. Instead, they should be **annotations on existing
edges**. An edge A → C gets an optional interaction qualifier: \"this
evidence was gathered at level B = b₀; the relationship may not hold at
other levels of B.\" This keeps the graph manageable while preserving
the information. The interaction qualifier affects the projection: when
π encounters an edge with an interaction annotation, and the target
context has B ≠ b₀, it further attenuates the projected CPT.
Interactions are *modifiers on edges*, not edges themselves. This is
analogous to how Cartwright (1989) treats capacities --- the capacity of
A to produce C is context-dependent, and the context-dependence is a
property of the capacity, not a separate causal relationship.

**Transportability analysis, in plain English.**

Transportability is Pearl and Bareinboim\'s (2011, 2016) formal answer
to the question: when can you take a causal effect estimated in one
context (say, a psychology lab at Stanford) and validly apply it in a
different context (say, a hospital in Mumbai)?

The core idea is simple. You draw the causal graph for the source
context (the lab) and then mark every variable where the target context
(the building) might differ. These marks are called **S-nodes** ---
think of them as red flags saying \"this variable might work differently
here.\" For example: the lab used college students (S-node on
\"population\"), the lab had controlled lighting (S-node on \"lighting
variability\"), the lab measured over 30 minutes (S-node on \"exposure
duration\").

Pearl then proves formal conditions under which the causal effect is
still identifiable despite these differences. Sometimes the differences
don\'t matter --- if the S-nodes are on variables that don\'t lie on any
active causal path between your intervention and your outcome, the lab
result transports directly. Sometimes you need to adjust --- if an
S-node sits on a mediator, you may need to reweight the effect using
data from the target population. Sometimes transport is impossible ---
if an S-node sits directly on the mechanism and you have no
target-context data, you simply cannot make the transfer.

The critical limitation, and the reason the EN matters: **Pearl\'s
framework tells you what to do once the S-nodes are placed, but it
doesn\'t tell you where to place them.** S-node placement requires
knowing *which* variables might differ between contexts, and that
knowledge depends on the warrant type. If you have a MECHANISM warrant,
you know exactly which variables participate in the causal chain, so you
know exactly where to check for context differences. If you have only an
EMPIRICAL\_ASSOCIATION warrant, the mediating pathway is unknown, so
context-sensitivity could enter *anywhere* --- you\'d need S-nodes on
every variable, which typically makes transport formally unidentifiable.

The EN\'s warrant types are, in effect, a guide to S-node placement.
That\'s one of the core practical contributions.

**You\'re right. Explanation should increase confidence. And in the EN,
it does --- but only when the architecture is set up correctly.**

The confusion comes from conflating two very different graph
topologies: **serial chains** (where theory is the *only path*)
vs. **parallel support** (where theory *explains* an independently
established empirical finding).

**Case 1: Theory as the only bridge (serial).** There is no direct
empirical evidence that fractal facades improve wellbeing.
The *only* way to get from \"fractal dimension\" to \"wellbeing\" is
through the theoretical chain: fractals → cortical response →
\[THEORY\_DERIVED: prediction error\] → \[THEORY\_DERIVED: stress
reduction\] → wellbeing. Here, the theory is doing all the bridging
work. It\'s the only road between two disconnected empirical findings.
The minimum-discount rule correctly says: this chain is only as strong
as its weakest link, d = 0.25. You cannot claim more confidence than the
most speculative step warrants, because *if that step is wrong, the
whole chain collapses*.

**Case 2: Theory as explanation of an existing empirical finding
(parallel).** Your car mechanic example is this case. You already have a
direct EMPIRICAL\_ASSOCIATION edge: \"replacing the carburetor fixes the
stalling\" (d = 0.80, ω = 0.70). The mechanic has seen this work twenty
times. That edge stands on its own. Now the mechanic learns *why*: the
gas/oxygen ratio was wrong, and the new carburetor restores the correct
ratio, which produces proper combustion. This mechanistic understanding
creates a *second, parallel* line of support --- a MECHANISM edge
running through intermediate nodes (gas ratio → combustion efficiency →
engine performance), with its own warrant type and strength.

In the EN, these are **parallel edges** between the same pair of claims
(or parallel paths --- a direct edge and a multi-step path arriving at
the same destination). And the combination rule for parallel evidence
is *additive in log-odds space*, not minimum-discount. The log-odds
contributions *sum*:

Total = d₁ · ω₁ · logit(p₁) + d₂ · ω₂ · logit(p₂) + \...

The mechanism *adds* to the empirical association. The total confidence
goes *up*, not down. Exactly as your intuition demands.

**So the architecture already handles this correctly --- the key is
recognizing the graph topology.**

Here\'s the principle stated precisely. When someone discovers a
mechanism explaining an existing empirical finding, two things happen in
the EN:

**First, the original EMPIRICAL\_ASSOCIATION edge stays.** It doesn\'t
go away. The correlation has been observed and replicated. It stands as
its own line of evidence.

**Second, a new MECHANISM path is added** --- a parallel route through
intermediate nodes that explains *why* the association exists. This path
has its own warrant type (MECHANISM for identified steps,
THEORY\_DERIVED for interpretive steps) and its own warrant strength.

**Third, the original edge\'s warrant strength ω goes up.** And this is
the subtle point that I had not made explicit. When an empirical
association receives a mechanistic explanation, the association itself
becomes more trustworthy --- because we now have a reason to believe the
correlation is not confounded. Before the mechanism was known, the
carburetor-stalling correlation might have been confounded (maybe the
mechanic was also unknowingly adjusting the timing when replacing the
carburetor). After the mechanism is known (gas/oxygen ratio →
combustion), we understand why the replacement works, which reduces the
probability of confounding. So ω on the original EMPIRICAL\_ASSOCIATION
edge increases --- say, from 0.70 to 0.82. The explanation doesn\'t
change the *type* of the empirical evidence (it\'s still an observed
association), but it increases our *confidence* in that evidence.

**Fourth, the two lines of evidence combine additively** through the
projection. The BN edge \"carburetor replacement → stalling fixed\" now
gets contributions from both the empirical association and the
mechanistic path, producing a higher CPT value than either alone.

**This gives us four distinct effects of mechanistic explanation on
confidence:**

**Direct addition**: The mechanism provides an independent line of
support (parallel path in log-odds).

**Warrant strength boost**: The mechanism increases ω on the original
empirical edge by reducing confounding risk.

**Transfer reliability improvement**: A claim supported by both
MECHANISM and EMPIRICAL\_ASSOCIATION transfers better than one supported
by EMPIRICAL\_ASSOCIATION alone, because the mechanism tells you where
to check for context differences (S-node placement).

**Research targeting**: When the mechanism is partially known, the EN
identifies exactly which links need empirical confirmation.

All four effects increase confidence. None decrease it. Your intuition
is completely vindicated.

misleading.**

In the fractal case, there *is no direct empirical association* between
fractal facades and occupant wellbeing. Nobody has run a study showing
\"buildings with fractal facades produce happier occupants.\" The only
path from fractals to wellbeing runs through the theoretical middle

bridge. The minimum discount (d = 0.25) is correct because the theory is
doing all the connecting work.

If someone *did* run that study --- measuring wellbeing in buildings
with and without fractal facades --- and found a positive association,
the EN would immediately add a direct EMPIRICAL\_ASSOCIATION edge

And the theoretical explanation increases ω on the empirical edge by

**The deep principle:** Theory never decreases confidence relative to

empirical support, provides less confidence than direct empirical

To put it in your car mechanic terms: knowing why the carburetor fix

mechanic who *also* understands the theory outperforms both.

roughly 12% of the world\'s population, and that this population is
systematically unusual on many psychological dimensions

**Neurodiversity**: autism spectrum, ADHD, sensory processing

how people respond to environmental stimuli (fluorescent lighting that

**Age**: children, working-age adults, elderly --- with different
sensory acuities, different mobility constraints, different cognitive
resources, different needs from the built environment

**Sensory differences**: vision impairment, hearing differences, chronic

baseline stress levels, and what counts as \"normal\" or \"comfortable\"

There are three options, and I think the right answer is a combination.

**Option 1: Population factors as modifiers on warrant strength (ω).**

Under this approach, every EN edge gets an additional annotation:
a **population scope** descriptor that records *who was studied*. When
the target population differs from the studied population, ω is
attenuated. So an edge established with Swedish undergraduates gets ω =

the attenuation is ad hoc. How much should ω drop when going from
Swedish undergraduates to Gujarati elders? Without a principled basis,
the adjustment is just hand-waving.

function.**

Under this approach, π takes an additional input: a **population
distance** measure between the studied population and the target
population. The projection formula becomes:

logit(p\_target) = d(τ) · ω · δ(pop\_source, pop\_target) ·
logit(p\_lab)

where δ is a population transfer factor ∈ (0, 1) that attenuates the
projection based on how different the populations are. This is clean ---
it separates population transfer from evidence quality (ω) and from
evidence type (d). But it requires a principled way to compute δ, which
is itself a hard problem.

**Option 3: Population factors as explicit nodes in the EN.**

Under this approach, population characteristics become **moderator
nodes** in the EN. The edge \"daylight → mood\" gets an interaction
annotation: \"this relationship is moderated by culture, such that the
effect is stronger in populations with more indoor lifestyles.\" Or more
formally: there is an edge from \"cultural context\" to the \"daylight →
mood\" relationship, with its own warrant type (EMPIRICAL\_ASSOCIATION

strength.
