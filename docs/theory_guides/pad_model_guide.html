What Is Mehrabian-Russell PAD Model?

The PAD model proposes that all emotional responses to environments can be described using three independent dimensions: Pleasure (how pleasant or unpleasant the experience is), Arousal (how stimulated or calm you feel), and Dominance (how much control you feel you have). Any environmental experience — walking into a cathedral, sitting in a cramped airplane, or relaxing in a garden — can be located as a point in this three-dimensional emotional space.

For architects, the PAD model provides a systematic vocabulary for specifying the emotional goals of a design. Instead of vague terms like 'cozy' or 'impressive,' a designer can specify target regions in PAD space and then identify the environmental features (lighting, scale, color, acoustics) that produce those emotional coordinates.

Historical Origins

Albert Mehrabian and James Russell developed the model in their 1974 book 'An Approach to Environmental Psychology,' published by MIT Press. The work grew from Mehrabian's research on nonverbal communication and Russell's work on the structure of emotion (Russell later developed the influential Circumplex Model of Affect in 1980, which uses just Pleasure and Arousal). The PAD model was explicitly designed for environmental psychology — to describe how physical environments affect emotional states. It was one of the first attempts to apply dimensional emotion theory specifically to person-environment transactions.

How It Works

Pleasure captures the hedonic valence of the environment — driven by aesthetic quality, comfort, and match with preferences. Arousal captures the level of neural activation — driven by stimulus intensity, novelty, complexity, and unpredictability. Dominance captures the sense of control and agency — driven by spatial openness, freedom of movement, and ability to modify the environment. The three dimensions are theoretically orthogonal: a space can be pleasant but low-arousal (a quiet garden) or pleasant but high-arousal (a lively market), and either can feel controlling or liberating.

Applications in Design

The PAD model has been widely used in retail design (stores aim for moderate pleasure + moderate arousal + low dominance to encourage browsing), healthcare design (patient rooms aim for high pleasure + low arousal + high dominance), workplace design (offices balance arousal for productivity with dominance for autonomy), and hospitality design. The Semantic Differential Scale based on PAD is one of the most commonly used instruments in environmental assessment research.

Formal Model

Formally: E(s) = [P(s), A(s), D(s)]ᵀ, where E is the emotional response vector to stimulus/environment s, and P, A, D are continuous dimensions typically measured on bipolar scales (e.g., -4 to +4). Mehrabian proposed that approach-avoidance behavior is a function of all three dimensions: Approach = 0.69P + 0.32A - 0.17D (for novel environments), though the specific weights vary by context.

Rejection Rationale

Fully reduces to IC + NM + DP without residual. Pleasure maps to IC valence (interoceptive prediction error direction), Arousal maps to NM sympathetic activation, Dominance maps to DP perceived control. No organizing principle unique to PAD remains after this decomposition — it is a dimensionality claim, not a domain theory.
