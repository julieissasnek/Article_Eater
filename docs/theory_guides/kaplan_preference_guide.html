What Is Kaplan Preference Matrix?

The Kaplan Preference Matrix is a framework for understanding why people prefer certain environments over others. It identifies four information-based qualities that make a scene appealing: coherence (the scene makes sense), complexity (there's enough to look at), legibility (you can orient yourself), and mystery (there's a promise of more information if you go further). These four qualities arise from crossing two dimensions: understanding vs. exploration, and immediate vs. inferred.

For designers, this is one of the most practical frameworks in environmental psychology. It explains, for instance, why a well-maintained garden path curving out of sight (mystery + legibility) is more appealing than a dead-end alley (no mystery) or a featureless field (no complexity). It gives designers specific, actionable qualities to aim for.

Historical Origins

Rachel Kaplan and Stephen Kaplan developed this framework through decades of research at the University of Michigan, beginning in the 1970s. Their seminal 1989 book 'The Experience of Nature: A Psychological Perspective' formalized the matrix. The Kaplans' work grew from an evolutionary perspective: our preference for certain landscapes reflects adaptation to environments that supported survival — coherent, complex, legible, and mysterious landscapes were those that offered both safety and opportunity for exploration. Their earlier work on Attention Restoration Theory (1989, 1995) proposed that natural environments restore directed attention through 'soft fascination.'

How It Works

The four qualities map onto two fundamental cognitive needs. Understanding (coherence + legibility) reflects the need to make sense of the current environment — to orient, to predict, to feel safe. Exploration (complexity + mystery) reflects the need to acquire new information — to discover, to learn, to expand one's cognitive map. The immediate/inferred distinction captures whether the information is directly available in the current view or must be inferred (requiring mental effort or physical movement). The theory predicts that the most preferred environments score high on all four qualities simultaneously.

Applications in Design

The framework has been applied to landscape design, park planning, residential neighborhood evaluation, hospital garden design, and urban streetscape assessment. Mystery (perhaps the most architecturally actionable quality) can be created through curved paths, partial views, layered vegetation, and spatial sequences that reveal new information progressively. Legibility comes from clear landmarks, spatial hierarchy, and orientation cues. Coherence comes from consistent materials, organized spatial structure, and visual order.

Formal Model

Formally: P(x) = w₁·Coherence(x) + w₂·Complexity(x) + w₃·Legibility(x) + w₄·Mystery(x), where P is environmental preference, the w values are relative weights (empirically estimated), and each quality is measured on a continuous scale. Research suggests mystery and coherence are typically the strongest predictors. The matrix structure is: Understanding × Immediate = Coherence; Exploration × Immediate = Complexity; Understanding × Inferred = Legibility; Exploration × Inferred = Mystery.
