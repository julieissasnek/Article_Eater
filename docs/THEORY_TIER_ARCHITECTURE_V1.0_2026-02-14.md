## The Corrected Framework Hierarchy
## Version 1.0 — February 14, 2026

---

# 1. THE CORRECTION

The original Article Eater design placed domain-specific theories like Attention Restoration Theory (Kaplan, 1995) and Stress Recovery Theory (Ulrich, 1983) at Tier 1 — the foundational framework level. This was wrong. These are phenomenological descriptions of *what happens* (nature restores attention, nature reduces stress) but not mechanistic accounts of *why* at a level that connects to neural implementation. They belong in Tier 2 as domain-specific organizing schemas, not in Tier 1 as framework theories.

The distinction matters architecturally. A true framework theory constrains reasoning *across* domains through mechanistic depth — it tells you what must be happening at the neural level whenever any environmental effect occurs, regardless of the specific environment or outcome. A descriptive theory like ART only organizes phenomena within one domain (nature exposure → attentional restoration) and could in principle be entirely wrong about the mechanism while correctly describing the phenomenology.

**Three criteria for Tier 1 status:**
1. **Mechanistic specificity** — specifies mechanisms at a level connecting to neural implementation
2. **Cross-domain generativity** — generates predictions across domains, not within one domain
3. **Convergent multi-method support** — supported by evidence from multiple methodologies (single-cell recording, fMRI, lesion studies, computational modeling, behavioral experiments)

# 2. TIER 1: TRUE FRAMEWORK THEORIES

These are neurally grounded, cross-domain frameworks that any environmental effect must operate through. They provide the mechanistic vocabulary for explaining *why* built environments affect human cognition, affect, and behavior.

## 2.1 Predictive Processing / Active Inference / Free Energy Principle

**Core claim**: The brain maintains a hierarchical generative model of its environment and minimizes prediction error through perception (updating the model) and action (changing the environment to match predictions).

**Neural implementation**: Cortical hierarchies with ascending prediction error signals and descending prediction signals. Precision modulated by neuromodulatory systems (dopamine, acetylcholine, serotonin, norepinephrine). Superficial pyramidal neurons encode prediction errors; deep pyramidal neurons encode predictions (Bastos et al., 2012).

**Why it matters for neuroarchitecture**: Every encounter with a built environment involves generating predictions about spatial structure, sensory input, and affordances; experiencing prediction errors when the environment deviates from expectations; precision-weighting those errors based on context; and updating the generative model or acting to change the environment. This framework provides the mechanistic basis for understanding why familiarity modulates aesthetic response, why moderate complexity is preferred (manageable prediction errors that can be resolved — the Goldilocks "sweet spot"), why cultural background shapes preferences (different generative models produce different predictions), and why legibility matters for wellbeing (legible environments allow more precise predictions, reducing metabolic cost).

**Key references**: Friston (2010, cited ~8,000); Clark (2013, cited ~5,500; 2016, cited ~3,000); Hohwy (2013, cited ~2,500); Kanai, Komura, Shipp, & Friston (2015, cited ~500).

## 2.2 Spatial Navigation and Cognitive Mapping

**Core claim**: The hippocampal-entorhinal system constructs an allocentric cognitive map of spatial structure using place cells, grid cells, head direction cells, border cells, and speed cells — supporting navigation, planning, memory, and imagination.

**Neural implementation**: One of the most well-characterized neural systems in neuroscience (2014 Nobel Prize to O'Keefe and the Mosers). Place cells in hippocampus, grid cells in entorhinal cortex, with theta oscillations coordinating spatial coding. Recent evidence extends to domain-general relational mapping — concepts, social hierarchies, and abstract relationships may use the same grid-cell coding scheme (Behrens et al., 2018; Bellmund, Gärdenfors, Moser, & Doeller, 2018).

**Why it matters for neuroarchitecture**: The hippocampal-entorhinal system is the primary neural substrate for experiencing architectural space. When you enter a building, your brain constructs a spatial representation using these cell types. Architecture's legibility, complexity, and structure directly affect the quality and stability of this representation. Buildings hard to navigate produce unstable cognitive maps → measurable increases in stress → direct neural signatures in hippocampal theta and grid cell coherence.

**Specific testable predictions**: Buildings with clear spatial hierarchies should produce more coherent hippocampal representations and lower stress. Landmarks at decision points should stabilize cognitive maps. Environments affording efficient path integration should reduce allostatic load. Spatial disorientation should correlate with increased hippocampal theta and elevated cortisol.

**Key references**: O'Keefe & Dostrovsky (1971, cited ~5,000); O'Keefe & Nadel (1978, cited ~10,000); Hafting, Fyhn, Molden, Moser, & Moser (2005, cited ~5,000); Moser, Kropff, & Moser (2008, cited ~3,000); Behrens et al. (2018, cited ~1,200); Bellmund et al. (2018, cited ~800).

## 2.3 Dual-Process Theory / Implicit vs. Explicit Processing

**Core claim**: Fast, automatic, implicit processing (System 1 / Type 1) and slow, deliberate, explicit processing (System 2 / Type 2) operate through different neural systems and interact constantly.

**Neural implementation**: Implicit processing relies on subcortical circuits (amygdala, basal ganglia, superior colliculus) and sensory cortices. Explicit processing recruits prefrontal cortex (dorsolateral PFC, anterior cingulate), working memory networks, and attentional control systems.

**Why it matters for neuroarchitecture**: Most environmental experience is processed implicitly. Your first affective response to a room — comfort, unease, spaciousness, claustrophobia — is an implicit evaluation within hundreds of milliseconds, driven by low-spatial-frequency visual features, binocular depth cues, and learned associations. Explicit evaluation ("I notice the ceiling height is generous") comes later and may override or ratify the implicit assessment. This means: (a) architectural effects on wellbeing operate primarily through implicit channels, (b) measurement must capture both implicit (physiological, RT, ERP) and explicit (self-report) responses, and (c) the *discrepancy* between implicit and explicit responses is itself meaningful — when someone says they like a space but their cortisol is elevated, dual-process theory explains this as a conflict between implicit threat detection and explicit social desirability.

**Key references**: Kahneman (2011, cited ~60,000); Evans & Stanovich (2013, cited ~3,500).

## 2.4 Default Mode Network vs. Task-Positive Network Dynamics

**Core claim**: The brain has a default mode network (DMN) — midline and lateral parietal regions active during rest, mind-wandering, self-referential thought, and social cognition — that is anti-correlated with task-positive networks (TPN) involved in focused attention and executive control. The balance between them is critical for wellbeing, creativity, and cognitive restoration.

**Neural implementation**: DMN uses ~20% of brain glucose despite small cortical volume. Suppressed by noradrenergic arousal. Chronic suppression associated with increased cortisol and reduced immune function (Treadway et al., 2015).

**Why it matters for neuroarchitecture**: Built environments differentially engage DMN vs. TPN. Environments demanding sustained task-positive engagement (navigating complex unfamiliar buildings, processing dense signage, monitoring threats) suppress DMN and produce "directed attention fatigue" — what Kaplan (1995) described phenomenologically but this framework explains mechanistically. Environments allowing DMN engagement (restful, safe, familiar, moderately stimulating spaces) support mind-wandering, self-reflection, memory consolidation, and creative incubation.

**The deeper mechanism**: DMN can be understood as the brain's "generative model in maintenance mode" — running simulations, consolidating predictions, updating the model without pressing external demands (Pezzulo, Rigoli, & Friston, 2015). Suppressing DMN = suppressing model maintenance = increasingly inaccurate generative model = more prediction errors = more cortisol = vicious cycle of cognitive fatigue.

**Key references**: Raichle et al. (2001, cited ~15,000); Fox et al. (2005, cited ~10,000); Buckner, Andrews-Hanna, & Schacter (2008, cited ~7,500).

## 2.5 Neuromodulatory Systems and the Neurochemistry of Affect

**Core claim**: The brain's major neuromodulatory systems — dopamine, serotonin, norepinephrine, acetylcholine, cortisol/HPA axis, oxytocin, endogenous opioids — modulate cognition, affect, and behavior through well-characterized anatomy, pharmacology, and functional roles.

**Key systems and their architectural relevance**:

**Dopamine** (reward prediction error, motivation, approach): Mesolimbic system (VTA → nucleus accumbens) encodes difference between expected and received reward. Architectural novelty, beauty, and positive surprise modulate dopaminergic signaling. Distinction between "wanting" (dopaminergic incentive salience) and "liking" (opioidergic hedonic impact) explains why people may seek novel environments even without high enjoyment (Berridge & Robinson, 2016).

**Norepinephrine / Locus coeruleus** (arousal, explore-exploit tradeoff): LC-NE system modulates gain of neural processing — adjusts signal-to-noise ratio across cortex. In predictive processing terms, modulates precision of prediction errors. Alarming, novel, or uncertain environments drive LC-NE activation, shifting from exploitation (routine behavior in familiar spaces) to exploration (information-gathering in novel spaces). Connects directly to Goldilocks: moderate noradrenergic tone = optimal function; extremes produce drowsiness or anxiety (Aston-Jones & Cohen, 2005).

**HPA axis / Cortisol** (stress, allostatic load): Most directly measured pathway in environmental psychology. Chronic elevation produces allostatic load with downstream effects on immune function, hippocampal neurogenesis (linking back to spatial cognition), and cardiovascular health (McEwen, 2007).

**Acetylcholine** (attention, learning, precision modulation): Signals expected uncertainty — boosts precision of sensory prediction errors during learning. Novel environments requiring learning engage cholinergic systems (Yu & Dayan, 2005).

**Serotonin** (mood regulation, harm avoidance): Modulates precision of affective predictions. Low serotonergic tone = increased threat sensitivity, modulating architectural fear responses.

**Key references**: Schultz, Dayan, & Montague (1997, cited ~12,000); Aston-Jones & Cohen (2005, cited ~5,000); McEwen (2007, cited ~5,500); Yu & Dayan (2005, cited ~3,500).

## 2.6 Interoception and the Construction of Affect

**Core claim**: Affective experience is *constructed* through integration of interoceptive signals (from the body about physiological state), exteroceptive signals (from the environment), and prior beliefs (predictions about body state). Interoceptive prediction error — mismatch between predicted and actual body state — is a primary driver of affective experience (Barrett, 2017; Seth, 2013; Seth & Friston, 2016).

**Neural implementation**: Anterior insula as key hub for interoceptive awareness (Craig, 2009). Interoceptive predictions generated in same hierarchical Bayesian framework as exteroceptive predictions.

**Why it matters for neuroarchitecture**: Explains *how* environmental features get translated into subjective feelings. Architecture doesn't directly cause emotions — it modulates bodily physiological state (through visual processing, spatial cognition, autonomic regulation, postural adjustments), and the brain constructs an affective experience by integrating these bodily changes with environmental predictions. Same environment → different affective experiences in different people because different interoceptive sensitivities and different conceptual frameworks for categorizing bodily states → explains cultural and individual variation in architectural preferences (Goldilocks framework).

**Key references**: Barrett (2017, cited ~4,000); Barrett & Simmons (2015, cited ~1,800); Craig (2009, cited ~6,000); Seth (2013, cited ~2,500); Seth & Friston (2016, cited ~800).

## 2.7 Memory Systems: Hippocampal-Cortical Consolidation

**Core claim**: Rapid hippocampal encoding of specific episodes interacts with slow cortical learning of statistical regularities to produce episodic memory (what happened to me in this place) and semantic memory (what is generally true about places like this). Sleep-dependent consolidation replays hippocampal traces in cortex, gradually extracting generalizations (McClelland, McNaughton, & O'Reilly, 1995; Diekelmann & Born, 2010).

**Why it matters for neuroarchitecture**: Architectural experience involves both episodic memory (my experience of this specific building today) and semantic architectural knowledge (what hospitals/temples/homes are generally like). Semantic knowledge constitutes the generative model's priors — the predictions brought to any new architectural encounter. Cultural variation = different semantic architectural knowledge → different predictions. Explains why first impressions persist (rapid hippocampal encoding creates strong episodic traces) and why repeated exposure changes experience (consolidation extracts regularities, updates semantic priors).

**Key references**: McClelland, McNaughton, & O'Reilly (1995, cited ~5,000); Diekelmann & Born (2010, cited ~4,000).

## 2.8 Embodied Cognition and Sensorimotor Contingencies

**Core claim**: Cognition is constitutively shaped by the body's morphology, sensorimotor capacities, and interaction with the environment. Includes Gibson's ecological approach (affordances) but extends to the broader claim that spatial, social, and abstract cognition are grounded in sensorimotor experience (Varela, Thompson, & Rosch, 1991; O'Regan & Noë, 2001; Gibson, 1979).

**Neural implementation**: Canonical neurons in premotor cortex respond to graspable objects (Rizzolatti & Craighero, 2004). Dorsal visual stream computes spatial relationships for action (Goodale & Milner, 1992). Vestibular system contributes to spatial cognition and self-location.

**Why it matters for neuroarchitecture**: Architectural experience is inescapably embodied — you move through buildings, your body orients to surfaces and openings, posture responds to ceiling heights and enclosure levels, locomotion adapts to floor surfaces and gradients. Architectural affordances are perceived properties specifying possibilities for action (doorways afford passage, stairs afford climbing, railings afford grasping). Body's engagement with affordances is a primary channel through which buildings affect cognition and affect.

**Key references**: Gibson (1979, cited ~35,000); Varela, Thompson, & Rosch (1991, cited ~15,000); Goodale & Milner (1992, cited ~8,000); O'Regan & Noë (2001, cited ~3,000).

# 3. TIER 2: DOMAIN-SPECIFIC THEORIES (PROPERLY DEMOTED)

These theories organize phenomena within environmental psychology and cognitive neuroarchitecture but lack the mechanistic depth of Tier 1. They are valuable as organizing schemas and historical repositories of empirical findings, but they should be *explained by* Tier 1 frameworks rather than serving as foundational constraints. Each is now reinterpreted through the Tier 1 lens.

## 3.1 Attention Restoration Theory (Kaplan, 1995)

**Original claim**: Natural environments restore directed attention through "soft fascination."

**Tier 1 reinterpretation**: A phenomenological description of what happens when DMN engagement is permitted (Tier 1.4) after sustained task-positive suppression, in environments with low-demand sensory input that doesn't drive strong prediction errors (Tier 1.1). "Soft fascination" = moderate prediction error with high precision from natural scenes, engaging enough to prevent boredom but not demanding enough to suppress DMN.

**What ART gets right**: The phenomenology of restoration is accurately described.

**What ART lacks**: No mechanism connecting "soft fascination" to neural processes. No explanation of *why* nature produces soft fascination (predictive processing provides this: natural scenes have 1/f spatial frequency spectra and fractal structure well-matched to the visual generative model). No predictions about individual differences or boundary conditions.

**Cited by**: ~7,000

---

## 3.2 Stress Recovery Theory (Ulrich, 1983, 1984)

**Original claim**: Natural environments promote recovery from psychophysiological stress through affective responses.

**Tier 1 reinterpretation**: Natural scenes reduce noradrenergic arousal (Tier 1.5 — neuromodulatory systems) by reducing prediction error load (Tier 1.1 — predictive processing), which lowers HPA axis activation and cortisol, supports hippocampal function (Tier 1.7 — memory systems) and immune function. The affective "recovery" is constructed from improved interoceptive prediction (Tier 1.6 — lower cortisol and autonomic arousal = fewer interoceptive prediction errors = more positive affect).

**What SRT gets right**: The empirical observation is robust — nature views accelerate physiological recovery.

**What SRT lacks**: No mechanism beyond "affective response." The Tier 1 account generates predictions SRT cannot: benefits should be greater for patients with higher trait anxiety (more room for noradrenergic reduction), greater during high HPA activation (pre-surgery), and partially blocked by noradrenergic agonists.

**Cited by**: Ulrich (1984), ~6,000

---

## 3.3 Prospect-Refuge Theory (Appleton, 1975)

**Original claim**: Humans prefer landscapes offering both prospect (open views, informational access) and refuge (enclosed, protected spaces).

**Tier 1 reinterpretation**: Optimal environmental configurations for balancing exploration (prospect = informational access) and safety (refuge = threat protection), mediated by the noradrenergic explore-exploit tradeoff (Tier 1.5). Prospect satisfies the exploratory drive (moderate LC-NE activation); refuge satisfies the safety drive (reduced threat-related HPA activation). The balance maps to the Goldilocks zone.

**Cited by**: ~2,500

---

## 3.4 Biophilia Hypothesis (Wilson, 1984)

**Original claim**: Humans have an innate affiliation with living systems and natural environments.

**Tier 1 reinterpretation**: A claim about the generative model's evolutionary priors (Tier 1.1). Natural environments are deeply familiar to the human generative model (millions of years of evolutionary calibration), producing lower prediction errors and more efficient processing than novel human-made environments. "Innate affiliation" = strong Bayesian priors favoring natural scene statistics.

**Cited by**: ~2,000+

---

## 3.5 Environmental Preference / Complexity-Liking (Berlyne, 1971)

**Original claim**: Inverted-U relationship between stimulus complexity and hedonic value.

**Tier 1 reinterpretation**: The Goldilocks zone of prediction error (Tier 1.1 + Tier 1.5). Moderate prediction errors that are resolvable are rewarding (dopaminergic reward from successful model updating); prediction errors too large to resolve are aversive (noradrenergic hyperarousal); prediction errors too small are boring (insufficient dopaminergic activation).

**Cited by**: ~4,500

---

## 3.6 Wayfinding Theory (Lynch, 1960; Weisman, 1981)

**Original claim**: Spatial legibility — paths, edges, districts, nodes, landmarks — determines navigability and sense of place.

**Tier 1 reinterpretation**: Phenomenological descriptions of spatial legibility that the cognitive mapping framework (Tier 1.2) explains mechanistically through place cell stability and grid cell coherence. Lynch's "imageability" = the capacity of an environment to produce a stable, coherent hippocampal cognitive map.

**Cited by**: Lynch (1960), ~15,000

---

## 3.7 Psychophysics of Space

**Category**: Empirical regularities (visual cliff, affordance scaling, ceiling height effects) that embodied cognition and sensorimotor contingency theory (Tier 1.8) organize.

# 4. TIER 2b: METHODOLOGICAL FRAMEWORKS

These are not theories about environments but about *how to study* environments:

- Measurement theory for each instrument modality (EEG, fMRI, cortisol, GSR, eye tracking, behavioral measures, self-report)
- Experimental design principles (internal/external validity, confound control, power analysis)
- Statistical methodology (Bayesian vs. frequentist, effect size estimation, meta-analytic methods)

These feed the method registry (Sprint 4b) and the methodological validity framework.

---

# 5. TIER 3: EXTRACTED CLAIMS

Individual empirical findings from the primary literature. These are the nodes that populate the web of belief. Each claim inherits its evaluation from the Tier 1 frameworks it is consistent with (or inconsistent with) and the Tier 2b methodological assessment of its source study.

# 6. HOW THE CORRECTED TIERS CHANGE WEB REASONING

## 6.1 Before (incorrect tiers): Shallow explanation

**Finding**: Hospital patients with nature views have shorter recovery times (Ulrich, 1984).

**Explanation**: "ART says nature restores attention. Biophilia says we innately prefer nature." → No mechanism, no further predictions.

## 6.2 After (corrected tiers): Deep multi-framework explanation

**Tier 1.1 (Predictive processing)**: Natural scenes have statistical properties (1/f spectra, fractal structure, moderate complexity) well-matched to the visual generative model → lower prediction errors → less metabolic processing cost.

**Tier 1.4 (DMN/TPN)**: Lower processing demands → greater DMN engagement → self-regulation, memory consolidation, immune function during recovery.

**Tier 1.5 (Neuromodulatory)**: Reduced prediction error → decreased noradrenergic arousal and HPA activation → lower cortisol → supports hippocampal function (Tier 1.7) and immune function.

**Tier 1.6 (Interoception)**: Lower cortisol and autonomic arousal → fewer interoceptive prediction errors → more positive affective construction → patient categorizes as "feeling better."

**Tier 2 (ART)**: "Soft fascination" = the phenomenological description of moderate prediction errors from natural scenes.

**Tier 2 (Biophilia)**: "Innate affiliation" = evolutionary calibration of the generative model.

## 6.3 What this generates that the old structure couldn't

Predictions that follow only from the corrected Tier 1 accounts:

1. Artificial scenes with the same 1/f spectral structure as natural scenes should produce similar benefits (partially confirmed — Joye & van den Berg, 2011)
2. Benefits should be greater for patients with higher trait anxiety (more baseline noradrenergic activation to reduce)
3. Benefits should be greater during periods of highest HPA activation (pre-surgery)
4. Benefits should be partially blocked by noradrenergic agonists
5. Benefits should be absent or reduced in individuals with interoceptive insensitivity (alexithymia)
6. The benefits should scale with the match between the environmental statistics and the individual's generative model priors (cultural variation predicted)

None of these follow from ART or biophilia alone.

| Link Type | From → To | Function |
|-----------|-----------|----------|
| Explanatory Derivation | Framework → Template | How Tier 1 generates Tier 2 mechanisms |
| Template Instantiation | Template → Claim | How templates explain specific findings |
| Analogical Exemplar | Exemplar → Claim | Cross-domain pattern matching |

| Measurement Modulation | Methodological Validity → Evidence | Methods adjust evidential weight |
| Cross-Framework Integration | Framework ↔ Framework | Inter-framework coherence |

## 7.3 Computational operations

1. **Template Matching**: New claim → search for mechanistic templates that explain it
2. **Constraint Propagation**: Collect structural constraints from frameworks, weighted by credibility, use to constrain BN assembly
3. **Credibility Flow**: Bidirectional — top-down (theory supports claim) and bottom-up (confirmed predictions strengthen theory)
4. **Anomaly Detection**: Claims with strong evidence but poor theoretical fit → flagged as scientifically interesting
5. **Framework Health Monitoring**: Track each framework's prediction accuracy, constraint violation rate, anomaly accumulation → Kuhnian "crisis" detection
6. **Measurement Adequacy Assessment**: Check whether evidence base is methodologically diverse or narrow → triangulation as coherence
