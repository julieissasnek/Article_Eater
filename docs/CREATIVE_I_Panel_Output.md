# ROUND TABLE PHASE — OPENING STATEMENTS

## Statement 1: Roger Beaty (Penn State)

I shall present the core construct this panel must calibrate: the coupling between the default mode network and the executive control network during creative cognition. My research programme has established that creative ability is associated not with the activity of any single brain network but with the capacity to dynamically couple networks that typically operate in opposition (Beaty et al., 2016, 2018; Beaty, Benedek, Silvia, & Schacter, 2016).

The primary evidence: using task-based fMRI during divergent thinking (Alternative Uses Task), we identified functional connectivity between DMN hubs (medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate) and ECN hubs (dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, inferior parietal lobule) that was significantly stronger in highly creative individuals (Beaty et al., 2018, N = 163, cross-validated connectome-based predictive modelling, r = 0.27 between predicted and observed creativity scores). This DMN-ECN coupling was replicated across three independent datasets and was specific to creative tasks — it did not emerge during non-creative executive function tasks.

The mechanism I propose: creative ideation requires two concurrent processes — the generation of candidate ideas (DMN-driven associative retrieval from episodic and semantic memory) and the evaluation/selection of promising ideas (ECN-driven constraint satisfaction and goal monitoring). The architectural implication is that environments supporting creativity must facilitate BOTH processes simultaneously or in rapid alternation — not just relaxation (which would favour DMN alone) or focus (which would favour ECN alone).

For CREATIVE_NETWORK_DYNAMICS_001, the core parameter is the DMN-ECN coupling coefficient: the functional connectivity strength during creative tasks. In our data, this ranges from r = 0.15 (low creative ability) to r = 0.45 (high creative ability), with a population mean of approximately r = 0.25. The question for the panel is how architectural features modulate this coupling — and I must be candid that we have no direct measurement of DMN-ECN coupling in architectural contexts. All our data is from MRI scanners.

## Statement 2: Kalina Christoff (UBC)

I must offer a theoretical challenge to Beaty's coupling account. My framework treats creative cognition not as a stable coupling state but as a dynamic trajectory through a state space defined by the interaction of multiple networks (Christoff, Irving, Fox, Spreng, & Andrews-Hanna, 2016). The DMN is not a single network but a family of interacting subsystems — the core DMN (medial PFC, posterior cingulate), the medial temporal subsystem (hippocampus, parahippocampal cortex), and the dorsomedial subsystem (temporoparietal junction, lateral temporal cortex). Creative cognition recruits different subsystems at different phases of the creative process.

The critical distinction: spontaneous thought (mind-wandering, daydreaming) recruits core DMN with minimal ECN involvement. Deliberate creative ideation (the Alternative Uses Task, Beaty's primary paradigm) recruits DMN-ECN coupling because the task imposes explicit goals and constraints. But the most creative breakthroughs — insight moments, aha experiences — may involve a qualitatively different dynamic: a phase transition from DMN-dominated spontaneous exploration to sudden ECN engagement when a promising configuration is detected (Kounios & Beeman, 2014; the anterior superior temporal gyrus gamma burst at the moment of insight).

This matters for architecture because the three phases (spontaneous exploration, deliberate ideation, insight) have different environmental optima. Spontaneous exploration thrives in low-demand, nature-rich, unstructured environments. Deliberate ideation thrives in moderate-stimulation, semi-private settings. Insight is unpredictable but may be facilitated by environmental transitions — the famous "shower insight" or "walking epiphany" — where a change in sensory context disrupts the current processing mode and allows a new configuration to emerge.

For the panel, I propose that CREATIVE_NETWORK_DYNAMICS_001 must include an insight/aha mechanism step (per clearance Issue 2). The Kounios and Beeman (2014, EEG, N = 78) gamma burst at 300 ms post-solution represents the DMN→ECN handoff moment. This is mechanistically distinct from the sustained DMN-ECN coupling that Beaty describes — it is a rapid, threshold-crossing event rather than a graded correlation.

## Statement 3: Jonathan Schooler (UCSB)

My contribution addresses incubation — the observation that creative problems are sometimes solved more effectively after a period of setting them aside. The meta-analytic evidence (Sio & Ormerod, 2009, k = 117, N > 3,000) confirms a reliable incubation effect for divergent thinking tasks (d = 0.32, 95% CI [0.19, 0.45]) and a smaller but significant effect for insight problems (d = 0.21, 95% CI [0.08, 0.34]). The effect is moderated by the type of interpolated activity: incubation is most effective when the intervening period involves low-demand tasks that permit mind-wandering (Baird et al., 2012, N = 145, d = 0.39 for undemanding vs. demanding interpolated task).

The mechanism I propose for INCUBATION_ARCHITECTURE_001 is attention restoration combined with unconscious associative processing. During incubation, the individual disengages from deliberate problem-solving (ECN withdrawal), enters a state of diffuse attention (DMN re-engagement), and allows spontaneous recombination of problem elements without executive constraint. The architectural conditions that support this are precisely those identified in Kaplan's (1995) Attention Restoration Theory: soft fascination (gentle, non-demanding sensory engagement, as from nature views, water features, or kinetic art), extent (a sense of spaciousness or being in a different world), compatibility (the environment does not require effortful processing), and being-away (physical or psychological distance from the work context).

The constraint per C-09 is critical: incubation requires that the body budget be in surplus. Per the Barrett-Craig two-stage model (CMR working model), the relevant mechanism is Stage 2 (anterior insula, evaluative): the brain must appraise the current bodily state as safe, rested, and metabolically sufficient before releasing the DMN from homeostatic monitoring into unconstrained associative processing. An occupant who is thermally uncomfortable, hungry, or stressed will not incubate effectively because the anterior insula is occupied with allostatic regulation rather than available for spontaneous thought.

## Statement 4: Marily Oppezzo (Santa Clara University)

I provide the evidence for the movement component of incubation. Our study (Oppezzo & Schwartz, 2014, N = 176, 4 experiments) demonstrated that walking increases divergent thinking by approximately 60% (d ≈ 0.60 for Guilford Alternative Uses Task) relative to sitting. The effect occurs both indoors (treadmill) and outdoors, suggesting that the motor activity itself — not the change of scenery — is the primary driver.

However, I must be transparent about the constraints per C-03. The 60% increase and d ≈ 0.60 come from our study, which is the primary evidence. Keinänen (2016) partially replicated with a smaller effect (d ≈ 0.35 for outdoor walking). The limited replication history means the confidence should not exceed 0.50. Additionally, the walking effect is specific to divergent thinking; it does not improve convergent thinking (in our Experiment 3, walking participants performed equivalently on the Remote Associates Test, a convergent task). This divergent-specific pattern is consistent with the IE-DPT framing (C-08): walking facilitates implicit, associative processing (divergent) but not explicit, constraint-based processing (convergent).

The architectural implication: buildings designed for creative work should include walking circuits — corridors, galleries, garden paths, internal staircases — that permit ambulatory incubation. The CREA3 motor pathway (walking → proprioceptive stimulation → divergent thinking) is genuinely independent of the CREA2B affect-broadening pathway (ceiling height → spatial liberation → affect broadening → divergent thinking; VF3, d = 0.40). These pathways are additive per C-01.

## Statement 5: Rex Jung (UNM)

I provide the neuroanatomical perspective on creative capacity — what distinguishes creative brains structurally, and how restorative environments might support creative function. My research programme has examined white matter integrity, cortical thickness, and grey matter volume in relation to creative achievement and divergent thinking ability (Jung et al., 2013; Jung, Mead, Carrasco, & Flores, 2013).

The key finding for HC_CREATIVE_DIVERGENCE_001: frontal lobe white matter integrity (specifically the inferior frontal occipitofrontal fasciculus) correlates with composite creativity scores (r = 0.32, N = 65, DTI study; Jung et al., 2010). This suggests that creative ability depends on efficient long-range cortical connectivity — the ability to rapidly propagate associative activation across distant brain regions. Critically, this connectivity is degraded by fatigue, stress, and sustained cognitive load (Lim & Dinges, 2010; the prefrontal cortex is disproportionately vulnerable to sleep deprivation and chronic stress).

The restoration-creativity link is therefore mechanistic, not merely correlational: restorative environments restore prefrontal function (Berman, Jonides, & Kaplan, 2008, N = 38, d = 0.46 for nature walk vs. urban walk on directed attention), which restores the long-range connectivity substrate for creative cognition. The HC_CREATIVE_DIVERGENCE_001 template must specify this restorative pathway while inheriting MEMORY-I's DMN re-engagement conditions per C-02. The creative template owns the DIVERGENT THINKING output; MEMORY-I owns the CONSOLIDATION output. Same environmental trigger, different downstream consequences — and crucially, different timescales: creative restoration operates on a 15-30 minute timescale (a short break restores divergent capacity), while memory consolidation operates on hours to days.

## Statement 6: Keith Sawyer (UNC)

I address collaborative creativity — the process by which groups produce creative outputs that exceed what any individual member could produce alone. My research (Sawyer, 2007, 2012; Sawyer & DeZutter, 2009) has established the construct of "group flow" — a state of collective creative emergence that occurs when group interaction reaches a critical threshold of mutual responsiveness, shared purpose, and balanced participation.

The empirical evidence for spatial effects on group creativity is modest but suggestive. Osborn's (1953) brainstorming paradigm showed that nominal groups (individuals working alone then pooling) outperform interactive groups — the "production loss" finding (Diehl & Stroebe, 1987, meta-analysis, d = -0.25 for interactive vs. nominal groups). However, this production loss is context-dependent: it is reduced in groups with established relationships (Paulus & Nijstad, 2003), in groups using structured ideation procedures (electronic brainstorming eliminates it), and — critically for architecture — in spatial configurations that allow simultaneous visual access and acoustic privacy (Sailer, 2010; Allen & Henn, 2007).

For COLLABORATIVE_CREATIVITY_ARCHITECTURE_001, I propose that the spatial parameters include: visual connectivity (the proportion of the workspace visible from any given point, per space syntax methods), acoustic zoning (the degree to which group conversation is contained within a defined zone without spilling to neighbouring groups), and flexible reconfigurability (the ease with which the space can shift between open-collaboration and focused-individual modes). These are architectural parameters that Sailer can anchor with empirical data.

The IE-DPT framing (C-08) is particularly illuminating for collaborative creativity: group brainstorming is primarily an IMPLICIT process (spontaneous association, building on others' ideas) but group evaluation and selection is an EXPLICIT process (deliberate critique, convergent filtering). The spatial requirements differ: implicit group creativity benefits from open, informal, semi-circular seating arrangements; explicit group evaluation benefits from structured, forward-facing, presentation-style arrangements. A creative workspace must support transition between these modes.

## Statement 7: Lucina Uddin (UCLA)

I provide the triple-network model perspective. My research programme has established that cognitive control and creative cognition depend on the dynamic interaction of three large-scale brain networks: the default mode network (DMN), the executive control network (ECN), and the salience network (SN, anchored in the anterior insula and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex) (Uddin, 2015; Uddin, Yeo, & Spreng, 2019).

The salience network serves as the switch — it detects salient internal or external events and coordinates the transition between DMN-dominant and ECN-dominant processing states (Menon & Uddin, 2010, over 5,000 citations). For creative cognition, the SN's role is critical: it detects when a DMN-generated candidate idea is "promising" enough to warrant ECN evaluation. The gamma burst that Kounios and Beeman (2014) identified at the moment of insight may be a salience network signal — the detection of a match between the unconsciously generated solution and the problem constraints held in working memory.

For CROSS_CREATIVE_NETWORK_DYNAMICS_001, I propose that the mechanism chain include the SN as a mediating variable: environmental features → SN activation → network switching dynamics → creative output. This is mechanistically distinct from the base template (which models DMN-ECN coupling directly) because it includes the switching mechanism itself — what triggers the transition from one network mode to another. Environmental features that modulate SN activation include stimulus novelty (unexpected sensory events), contrast (transitions between different spatial qualities), and social signals (the presence and behaviour of other people).

The environmental modulation of network switching is the key architectural lever. Buildings that provide uniform, unchanging sensory environments suppress SN activation and reduce network switching frequency — this may support sustained focus (ECN-dominant) but at the cost of creative flexibility. Buildings that provide varied, transitional, and mildly surprising sensory environments activate SN and increase switching frequency — potentially supporting creative cognition but at the cost of sustained focus. The Goldilocks zone for creative workplaces is the architectural design challenge.

## Statement 8: Kerstin Sailer (UCL Bartlett)

I provide the architectural evidence bridge. My research uses space syntax methods to quantify spatial configuration and relate it to occupant behaviour patterns (Sailer, 2010; Sailer & McCulloh, 2012). The most directly relevant finding: in creative workplaces (advertising agencies, design studios, technology companies), the spatial integration of the workspace (a graph-theoretic measure of how well-connected each space is to all other spaces) predicts the frequency of unplanned face-to-face interactions (Sailer, 2010, N = 3 organisations, r = 0.45 between spatial integration and interaction frequency). These unplanned interactions are a prerequisite for serendipitous creative exchange — the "water cooler" effect that innovation-space designers seek.

However, I must be candid per C-05: the evidence base for innovation-space design is primarily observational and case-study. No randomised controlled trial has assigned creative workers to different spatial configurations and measured creative output. The studies I cite (Allen & Henn, 2007; Sailer, 2010) measure interaction frequency and self-reported satisfaction, not creative productivity directly. The inference from "more interaction" to "more creativity" is plausible but carries an additional bridge (ANALOGICAL warrant at best). Furthermore, the effect of spatial integration is confounded by organisational culture — companies that invest in open, well-connected workspaces also tend to invest in other creativity-supporting practices.

For the architectural parameters, I propose: spatial integration coefficient (from space syntax analysis) as the primary spatial predictor of interaction frequency, with a bridge warrant of EMPIRICAL_COVARIANCE and confidence ≤ 0.50 per C-05. The second-order bridge from interaction frequency to creative output must be flagged as ANALOGICAL with THEORETICAL_DEFAULT.

# CRUCIBLE DEBATES

## Crucible 1: DMN-ECN Coupling — Graded Correlation vs. Phase Transition

**Beaty**: The evidence from connectome-based predictive modelling (Beaty et al., 2018, N = 163) shows a continuous distribution of DMN-ECN coupling strength across individuals. There is no bimodal distribution, no threshold — the coupling is graded. This implies that environmental interventions modulate creativity along a continuous dose-response function: slightly more DMN-ECN coupling produces slightly more creativity. For architectural design, this means incremental improvements in environmental conditions (slightly better nature views, slightly more comfortable rest areas) produce proportional returns.

**Christoff**: Your data describes individual differences in a static, task-averaged measure. But the temporal dynamics within a single creative episode tell a different story. Using dynamic functional connectivity analysis (sliding-window correlation), Beaty, Thakral, Madore, Benedek, and Schacter (2018) showed that DMN-ECN coupling fluctuates rapidly during creative tasks — it is not a stable state but a series of transient coupling events. And the moment of creative insight, as Kounios and Beeman (2014) demonstrated, involves a sudden, high-amplitude gamma burst in the right anterior temporal lobe that coincides with a rapid shift in network configuration. This is a phase transition — a qualitative shift in network dynamics — not a gradual increase in coupling strength.

The architectural implication is fundamentally different: if creativity involves phase transitions, then the environmental design goal is to facilitate state TRANSITIONS rather than maintain optimal states. This favours architectural VARIETY — sequential spaces with different qualities (quiet → stimulating → quiet) that provoke network reconfigurations — over architectural OPTIMALITY — a single "ideal creative space."

**Beaty**: I can accept that transient coupling events are the functional unit, not sustained coupling. But "phase transition" is a strong mathematical claim. In dynamical systems theory, a phase transition implies criticality — the system poised at a boundary between two attractors. There is no evidence that the DMN-ECN system operates near criticality during creative cognition. The fluctuations I observe could equally be described as stochastic switching between metastable states (Deco, Jirsa, & McIntosh, 2011) without invoking criticality.

**Uddin**: The salience network may resolve this debate. If the SN functions as a switch (Menon & Uddin, 2010), then DMN-ECN transitions ARE discrete switching events triggered by SN activation — but the RATE of switching is a continuous variable modulated by SN excitability. Environmental novelty and contrast increase SN excitability, increasing the switching rate. This gives you both: discrete transitions at the mechanistic level, but a continuous dose-response at the architectural level (more environmental variety → more SN activation → higher switching rate → more creative state transitions).

**Panel consensus**: Adopt the SN-mediated switching model as the working framework. DMN-ECN coupling during creativity consists of discrete switching events mediated by the salience network, but the rate of switching is a continuous variable modulated by environmental features. Architectural implications: both incremental improvements (continuous dose-response via SN excitability) AND sequential spatial variety (discrete transitions via network reconfigurations) are relevant design strategies. The base template (CREATIVE_NETWORK_DYNAMICS_001) owns the DMN-ECN mechanism and the insight/aha step; the CROSS_ template owns the SN-mediated switching mechanism and environmental modulation.

## Crucible 2: Walking-Creativity Pathway — Motor Route vs. Cognitive Route

**Oppezzo**: Our data (Oppezzo & Schwartz, 2014) provides direct evidence for the motor route. The key experiment: walking on a treadmill facing a blank wall (no nature, no visual variety, no change of scenery) produced the same divergent thinking boost as walking outdoors. If the cognitive route (attentional release → mind-wandering) were primary, the treadmill-blank-wall condition should show a reduced effect because it provides no soft fascination or nature content. The equivalent effect across conditions suggests that motor activity itself — proprioceptive and vestibular stimulation from bipedal locomotion — is the active ingredient.

**Schooler**: I must push back. Your treadmill condition eliminates nature views but does NOT eliminate attentional release. Walking on a treadmill is a low-demand, rhythmic activity that permits mind-wandering — just like walking outdoors. The attentional release mechanism predicts equivalent effects across both conditions. To dissociate the two routes, you would need a condition where motor activity is present but attentional demand is HIGH (walking on a treadmill while performing a concurrent demanding task). If the motor route is primary, creativity should still be boosted; if the cognitive route is primary, the concurrent task should eliminate the benefit.

**Oppezzo**: That experiment has not been done — I acknowledge the gap. But there is converging evidence for motor contributions: Slepian and Ambady (2012, N = 46, d = 0.42) showed that fluid arm movements (not walking, just smooth motor activity) increased creative fluency relative to rigid movements. This dissociates motor from attentional effects because both conditions involve the same attentional demand.

**Schooler**: Slepian and Ambady's paradigm is quite different from naturalistic walking. The fluid-movement effect could reflect embodied cognition (metaphorical mapping from "fluid" to "creative flexibility") rather than a direct motor-to-creativity pathway. The mechanism may be conceptual metaphor activation, not proprioceptive stimulation.

**Panel consensus**: Both routes are retained as contributing mechanisms. The motor route (CREA3: proprioceptive/vestibular stimulation → sensorimotor cortex activation → associative network priming) is supported by Oppezzo & Schwartz (2014) and Slepian & Ambady (2012) but with unresolved confounds — confidence ≤ 0.50 per C-03. The cognitive route (attentional release → DMN re-engagement → mind-wandering → divergent thinking) is supported by Baird et al. (2012) and Schooler et al. (2011) — confidence 0.50. The two routes are modelled as additive (per C-01) but their relative contributions remain uncalibrated. Architectural implication: walking circuits serve both routes simultaneously; nature corridors serve both routes plus soft fascination; treadmill rooms serve the motor route but not soft fascination.

## Crucible 3: Environmental Stimulation — Inverted-U vs. Differential-Mode

**Sawyer**: The classical position is the Yerkes-Dodson inverted-U: moderate arousal (moderate environmental stimulation) optimises performance, including creative performance. Mehta, Zhu, and Cheema (2012, N = 65, 5 experiments) showed that moderate ambient noise (70 dB, coffee-shop level) enhanced creative performance relative to both low noise (50 dB) and high noise (85 dB) on the Remote Associates Test. The inverted-U predicts a single Goldilocks zone for creative environments — moderate stimulation for all creative tasks.

**Jung**: But the Mehta et al. finding applies to convergent creativity (Remote Associates Test is a convergent measure — single correct answer). The evidence for divergent creativity suggests a DIFFERENT optimum. Oppezzo's walking data shows that LOW-demand environments boost divergent thinking. Baird et al. (2012) show that UNDEMANDING interpolated tasks (mind-wandering-permissive) produce better incubation than DEMANDING tasks. The pattern is: low stimulation for divergent, moderate stimulation for convergent.

**Uddin**: This maps onto the network model. DMN-dominant processing (divergent, implicit) is suppressed by environmental demand — high stimulation activates the SN, which recruits the ECN and suppresses the DMN. So low-stimulation environments favour DMN-dominant (divergent) processing, while moderate-to-high stimulation environments favour ECN-dominant (convergent) processing. The inverted-U is an artefact of averaging across divergent and convergent tasks.

**Christoff**: This is the differential-mode model, and it has a specific architectural prediction: creative workplaces should provide BOTH low-stimulation zones (for divergent incubation — quiet rooms, nature views, soft fascination) AND moderate-stimulation zones (for convergent evaluation — shared workspaces, whiteboards, structured meeting rooms), with easy transitions between them. A single "creative space" optimised for one mode will underserve the other.

**Panel consensus**: The differential-mode model is adopted as the primary framework for PROCESSING_STYLE_MODULATION_001 and CROSS_ENVIRONMENTAL_PROCESSING_STYLE_001. Low stimulation → DMN-dominant → divergent/implicit processing (IE-DPT: implicit). Moderate-to-high stimulation → ECN-dominant → convergent/explicit processing (IE-DPT: explicit). The inverted-U is retained as a secondary model applicable when divergent and convergent demands are averaged, but it is not the primary design tool. The architectural implication is spatial variety and modal zoning rather than a single optimum.

## IE-DPT Interaction (C-08 compliance)

The creativity domain provides the clearest IE-DPT instantiation in the CMR system:

**IMPLICIT processing** (DMN-dominant): Divergent thinking, associative spreading, mind-wandering, incubation, unconscious recombination. Optimal environmental conditions: low stimulation (50 dB ambient noise, dim lighting 150-300 lux, low social density), nature views, walking circuits, soft fascination.

**EXPLICIT processing** (ECN-dominant): Convergent thinking, constraint satisfaction, evaluative selection, deliberate problem-solving. Optimal environmental conditions: moderate stimulation (70 dB, standard lighting 300-500 lux, semi-structured social interaction), whiteboards, structured meeting rooms.

**TRANSITION** (SN-mediated): The salience network detects promising implicit-mode outputs and triggers the switch to explicit evaluation. Environmental transitions (changing spaces, encountering novel stimuli) facilitate SN-mediated mode switching. The insight/aha moment (Kounios & Beeman, 2014, gamma burst) is the prototypical implicit→explicit transition.

**Architectural design implication**: Creative workplaces require BOTH implicit-optimal and explicit-optimal zones with easy, low-friction transitions between them. A single "creative space" optimised for one mode underserves the other. The differential-mode model (Crucible 3 consensus) replaces the single-optimum inverted-U as the primary design framework.
