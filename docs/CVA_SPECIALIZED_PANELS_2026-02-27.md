# PANEL A: BARRETT'S OBJECTION — Cross-Cultural Valuation Structure

**Central Question**: Are the nine CVA valuation axes (Safety, Interest, Restoration, Status, Belonging, IdentityCongruence, AutonomySupport, CompetenceSupport, RelatednessSupport) culturally universal or Western theoretical impositions?

**Method**: Three cultural panels (Japanese, West African, Indian) evaluate the same five architectural scenarios, rating which valuation axes are active, how they relate to each other, and whether CVA's decomposition captures their aesthetic experience.

## PANEL A1: Japanese Aesthetics Panel

**Panelists**:
1. **Akira Hasegawa** — Environmental psychologist, Tokyo Metropolitan University; specialist in ma (negative space) and spatial perception (Nitschke, 1966; Isozaki, 1979; Hasegawa, 2015)
2. **Yuki Nakamura** — Social psychologist, Waseda University; specialist in amae (indulgent dependence) and interdependent self-construal (Doi, 1973; Markus & Kitayama, 1991; Nakamura, 2018)
3. **Hiroshi Yamamoto** — Architect and wabi-sabi theorist; professor at Tokyo Institute of Technology; specialist in sukiya-zukuri tradition and imperfect beauty (Tanizaki, 1933; Koren, 1994; Yamamoto, 2019)

### A1 Scenario Evaluations

**Scenario 1: Hospital patient room with large window overlooking a garden**

**Hasegawa**: The most active valuation is RestorationValue, clearly. But how we experience restoration is *not* individual; it's mediated by ma—the empty space between the patient and the garden. The window is important not because it provides a visible prospect, but because it creates a *void* between inside and outside. The silence of the room, the emptiness, the letting-be of things. In Western models, you measure prospect (information gain, wayfinding) and restoration as separate valuations. For us, they collapse. The restoration comes *from* the emptiness. So your InterestValue and RestorationValue are not orthogonal here; they're unified in ma.

**Yamamoto**: Yes. And the second thing: wabi-sabi. The rough plaster walls—if they were perfectly smooth, the room would feel *dead*, over-designed, without possibility. Imperfection is not a cost (as your ProcessingCost would suggest); it's a source of beauty. It invites contemplation. So your axes might include RestorationValue, but you're missing the aesthetic category of *imperfection as resource*. In Western formalization, you might try to map this to "novelty" or "interest," but that's wrong. Wabi-sabi is not about surprise or complexity; it's about the poignancy of transience and incompleteness.

**Nakamura**: I want to emphasize something about CompetenceSupport and AutonomySupport. In individualist settings, autonomy is *freedom from constraint*. A hospital room that gives you control over the window, the lights, the temperature is autonomy-supporting. But in Japan, autonomy is not freedom from; it's *appropriate participation in social structure*. The patient wants to know that the nurses are nearby, that there is *care*, that their dependence (amae) is being met. So AutonomySupportValue, as you define it, may be *negative* in Japanese contexts. We don't want autonomy; we want *secure dependence*. And that's not the same as SafetyValue. Safety is the absence of threat; secure dependence is the presence of *attentive care*.

**Yamamoto**: The garden itself, finally. In Western design, the garden is evidence—it proves that restoration is happening. ("Here is nature; you will restore.") For us, the garden is *teacher*. It teaches what is seasonally appropriate. Right now, perhaps the garden is bare—it's winter—and that teaches acceptance of the current moment. The incompleteness of winter is complete in itself. So the garden is not InterestValue (complexity, novelty); it's an invitation to *align with natural rhythm*, which is a separate valuation—let's call it **SeasonalAlignmentValue** or **Naturalness AsTeacher**. That's missing from your taxonomy.

**Scenario 2: Dense open-plan office with high ceilings and glass walls**

**Hasegawa**: This space violates ma. There are no negative spaces, no emptiness. Everything is *present*—the colleagues, the activities, the visual information. This creates anxiety. The high ceilings help somewhat (they create *vertical ma*), but the glass walls are disastrous. You are simultaneously *seen and not-seen*; you cannot have secure dependence (amae) because the boundary is unclear. Where can you withdraw? Where is the *ma* in which you can be alone together?

**Yamamoto**: And for wabi-sabi: this space is over-finished. The glass is transparent, the lines are clean, everything is rational and complete. There is no room for imperfection, no cracks, no shadows where meaning can hide. So it will be exhausting to work here, even though it appears "efficient."

**Nakamura**: The glass walls create a particular kind of pseudo-belonging. You are visually together but functionally separate. This is the opposite of secure dependence. In a Japanese office, you want *real propinquity*—shared tasks, shared materials, the sense that your work life is intertwined. Glass walls create the illusion of connection without the reality. So BelongingValue is simultaneously activated (you are near others) and violated (you cannot truly *be* with them). This creates cognitive dissonance.

**Scenario 3: Small, dimly lit tea ceremony room with rough plaster walls**

**Yamamoto**: This is the aesthetic ideal. Wabi-sabi in full flower. The small size creates ma—you cannot fit much; emptiness is abundant. The rough plaster teaches you that human making is imperfect, temporary, alive. The dim light means you cannot see everything; you must *imagine* the corners of the room. This invites your participation; you co-create the space. And there is *no status*, no display, no showing-off. The tea ceremony is about *being present* with others in radical simplicity.

**Hasegawa**: The ma here is spatial, temporal, and interpersonal. The silence between one tea movement and the next. The pause before the guest drinks. The empty space on the scroll. *All* of this is ma, and it creates the possibility of *presence*. In Western terms, you might call this aesthetic; in Japanese terms, it is *ethical*. The space teaches you *how to be*.

**Nakamura**: And belonging here is profound, but it is not through *shared identity* or *status alignment*; it is through *shared respect for incompleteness*. The guest and host know that the ceremony will have flaws—a movement will be awkward, the tea will be slightly too hot—and these flaws are where the *real* connection happens. It's not CompetenceSupport (you're not improving your skills); it's something else: **Mutual-Vulnerability-and-Acceptance**. You show your imperfection, and the other accepts it with grace. This is the deepest belonging.

**Yamamoto**: The interest level is actually very low—the stimulation is minimal. But the *depth* is maximal. So your model cannot distinguish between interesting (complex, novel) and *meaningful* (teaches you who you are). These are orthogonal.

**Scenario 4: Monumental civic building with symmetrical marble facades**

**Yamamoto**: This is anti-wabi-sabi. The symmetry says: "Everything is controlled, finished, perfect." But perfection is death; it leaves nothing for *you* to do. The marble is cold; it doesn't teach; it just *displays*. The scale is meant to make you feel small—StatusValue in negative form. You are meant to know that you are *insignificant* before this monument.

**Hasegawa**: There is no ma here. The building fills space completely. Every angle is calculated, every surface is finished. There is nowhere for your imagination to rest. It is tyrannical in its completeness.

**Nakamura**: And the belonging is perverted. You belong to a *nation*, to a *historical moment*, to a *power structure*. It's not organic belonging; it's imposed belonging. So BelongingValue is activated, but in a way that violates autonomy and individuality. This is the opposite of the tea ceremony, where belonging emerges from *mutual recognition*. Here, belonging is the erasure of the individual into the collective.

**Yamamoto**: The only valuation this activates in positive form is StatusValue. The monument says: "You are in the presence of greatness; you are small." For Western tourists, this may be InterestValue (impressive complexity, grand scale). But for a Japanese person, the monument says: "Your smallness is appropriate; accept it." Which is not bad, but it's not about beauty; it's about *right hierarchy*.

**Scenario 5: Winding market street with irregular stalls, strong smells, and crowd noise**

**Hasegawa**: This has ma in the sense of *unpredictability*. You cannot see the whole street; it winds. You encounter surprises—a smell you didn't expect, a vendor calling out. But the ma here is *stressful ma*, not *peaceful ma*. It activates both InterestValue (novelty, complexity) and what I would call **CognitiveLoadValue**—and these are in tension. The stimulation is high; so is the exhaustion.

**Yamamoto**: For wabi-sabi, this market is *alive*. The stalls are not perfectly arranged; they're worn, improvised. The vendors are imperfect; they make mistakes. But there's also a kind of organized chaos that teaches *human making*—every stall is a small act of creation, humble and imperfect. So wabi-sabi is present in the *creation*, not in the *structure*.

**Nakamura**: The belonging here is immediate and *anonymous*. You belong to the crowd without *knowing* the crowd. This is a different kind of belonging than the tea ceremony—it's **Collective-Belonging-in-Anonymity**. You are not seen as an individual, and paradoxically, that's liberating. You can *be* in the crowd without performing your identity. So BelongingValue is activated, but separately from IdentityCongruence—they are *orthogonal* in this space. In the tea ceremony, they were fused.

### A1 Summary: Japanese Panel Verdict

**Decomposability**: The Japanese panelists strongly agree that **aesthetic experience is NOT uniformly decomposable**. The tea ceremony room is holistically unified; the monumental building is holistically oppressive. When decomposition occurs, it reveals *tensions* between axes (e.g., pseudo-belonging in the open-plan office, or safety vs. visibility).

**Cultural-Specific Axes**:
1. **Amae** (secure dependence): Not captured by CVA's AutonomySupport or RelatednessSupport; it's a separate dimension that can be *negative* for autonomy-as-freedom but *positive* for autonomy-as-appropriate-relationship.
2. **Wabi-sabi** (imperfection as beauty): Not captured by any axis; it's orthogonal to ProcessingCost, Novelty, and Complexity.
3. **Ma** (emptiness as active agent): RestorationValue captures part of this, but not the ethical/ontological component.
4. **Collective-Belonging-in-Anonymity**: Separate from IdentityCongruence; orthogonal in market contexts, fused in tea ceremony contexts.

**Structural Insight**: The nine axes are not *independent* in Japanese aesthetic experience. **Belonging, IdentityCongruence, and RelatednessSupport form a cluster** that can be fused or separated depending on context (tea ceremony = fused; market = separated). **This clustering does not match Western decomposition.**

## PANEL A2: West African Aesthetics Panel

**Panelists**:
1. **Adeyemi Adeniyil** — Architect and urban theorist, Obafemi Awolowo University, Ile-Ife, Nigeria; specialist in communal space and Yoruba aesthetics (Elleh, 1997; Lawal, 1996; Adeniyil, 2020)
2. **Oluwatoyin Adebayo** — Yoruba aesthetics scholar, University of Lagos; specialist in àṣà (coolness), ìwà (character), and ọjọ́ (life force) (Thompson, 1973; Abiodun, 1994; Adebayo, 2019)
3. **Chiké Okonkwo** — Social psychologist, University of Nigeria, Nsukka; specialist in communal self-construal and collective agency (Gbadegesin, 1991; Gyekye, 1997; Okonkwo, 2021)

---

### A2 Scenario Evaluations

**Scenario 1: Hospital patient room with large window overlooking a garden**

**Adebayo**: In Yoruba aesthetics, this room is *aesthetically incomplete*. The garden is beautiful—àṣà, the cool perfection of growth and order—but the patient is isolated. And in West African understanding, you cannot have health without *community*. The window shows you nature, yes, but it doesn't show you *people*. It doesn't allow *visiting*. So RestorationValue, in your model, is about *individual recovery*. In Yoruba context, restoration comes from being *with the family*, from the presence of kin, from **communal witnessing of recovery**.

**Okonkwo**: Yes. And I want to emphasize: in individualist Western culture, the hospital room is designed to give the patient *privacy* and *autonomy*—control over the window, the lights, the environment. But in communal African cultures, privacy is not a good; it's an *isolation*. The optimal hospital room would be large enough for three or four family members to sleep alongside the patient. The window would open onto the courtyard so that extended family could see the patient and the patient could hear the community. Your AutonomySupportValue and RelatednessSupport are *antagonistic* in this design. I have to choose: private autonomy or communal relatedness. And the West African person chooses relatedness *every time*.

**Adeniyil**: As a designer, I can tell you: the garden view is supposed to provide RestorationValue through *connection to nature*. But in West African contexts, nature is not *separate* from culture; it's *interpenetrated*. The boundary between the hospital and the community is porous. The room should open onto a *shared garden* where family can work while the patient recovers. So restoration comes from **working alongside**, not from *individual contemplation*. The very distinction between "patient in room" and "garden outside" is Western.

**Adebayo**: And the concept of àṣà is crucial here. Àṣà is not just "coolness" in the temperature sense; it's **aesthetic and moral composure**. A space has àṣà if it is *balanced*, if nothing is *excessive*, if there is *dignity*. The garden has àṣà. The window has àṣà. But the *isolation* of the patient violates àṣà. It is excessive; it denies the person their full social existence. So the aesthetic judgment "this is beautiful" (because of the garden) is *overridden* by the ethical judgment "this is ugly" (because of the isolation). Your model separates RestorationValue from StatusValue and BelongingValue, but for us, they are *morally unified*. The beauty of the space depends on whether it permits the *patient to maintain dignity within community*.

**Scenario 2: Dense open-plan office with high ceilings and glass walls**

**Okonkwo**: This space actually aligns with West African communal values *more* than the hospital room. Everyone can see everyone; work is *collective and visible*. The glass walls are imperfect, but the *openness* of the layout is correct. Work is not private; it is social.

**Adeniyil**: Yes, but I want to qualify. The glass walls create a false communality. In West African design, when people work together, they work with *permeable* boundaries—they can hear each other, smell each other, touch each other if needed. Glass is transparent but *not permeable*. So you get the *illusion* of community without the *reality*. In a real West African workplace, you'd have an open courtyard where work happens, where people can interrupt each other, where status hierarchies can be maintained through physical proximity.

**Adebayo**: And the àṣà of this space is low. The glass is too rational, too *Western-modern*. For a space to have àṣà in a West African context, it must show *evidence of hand-making*. Rough walls, worn surfaces, the marks of human habitation. Glass is dead; it doesn't age, doesn't show use, doesn't teach history. So aesthetically, the space is *cold*.

**Okonkwo**: I'd add: the hierarchies are *invisible* in open-plan offices. In West African communal workplaces, hierarchy is *visible and acknowledged*. The chief sits in a particular place; the elders sit together; junior workers have their area. This is not seen as oppressive; it's seen as *natural and honest*. Open-plan pretends there is no hierarchy (everyone can see the same thing), which creates *anxiety*. People don't know where they stand. So your StatusValue is activated but *frustrated*. You want to understand your place; the space won't tell you.

**Scenario 3: Small, dimly lit tea ceremony room with rough plaster walls**

**Adeniyil**: This room is beautiful in àṣà. But it is *fundamentally alienating to a West African person*. It is designed for *individual contemplation*, for a *dyadic encounter* (one host, one guest). There is no space for *community*. A West African would ask: "Where are the other guests? Where is the family? Why am I alone?" The smallness, which Japanese aesthetics sees as invitation to intimacy, West African aesthetics sees as *exclusion*.

**Adebayo**: Yes. And the rough plaster walls are beautiful—they show age, imperfection, the marks of hands. That aligns with àṣà in one sense. But the *isolation* violates àṣà in a deeper sense. Beauty in a West African context is beauty in relationship, not beauty in solitude. The most beautiful space is one where many people gather, where there is music, where there is food, where there is *life*.

**Okonkwo**: The tea ceremony is intimate, but Western-style intimacy. A Yoruba equivalent would be the moment when one person brings kola nuts and serves them to the assembled family. That is *intimate* (it shows care and respect), but it is *collective* (it includes everyone). This room excludes everyone else. That's not beautiful; that's lonely.

**Scenario 4: Monumental civic building with symmetrical marble facades**

### A2 Summary: West African Panel Verdict

**Decomposability**: West African panelists strongly reject the assumption that valuations are *independent*. **Belonging, IdentityCongruence, and StatusValue form an inseparable cluster around communal dignity**. You cannot activate one without the others; you cannot weight one separately.

**Cultural-Specific Axes**:
1. **Àṣà** (aesthetic-moral composure, dignity): Not captured by any single CVA axis; it's a *meta-principle* that governs how valuations can be combined.
2. **Communal-Witnessing**: A form of belonging that is fundamentally different from dyadic relatedness; it requires *visibility to the group*, not intimacy with one person.
3. **Permeable-Boundary**: Different from "belonging" because it's tactile and immediate, not visual or cognitive.

**Structural Insight**: In West African aesthetics, **the structure of valuations is fundamentally collectivist**. The individual axes that Western psychology treats as fundamental (AutonomySupport, IdentityCongruence from a personal perspective) are *subordinate to collective dynamics*. You don't have a space that is high on Belonging and low on IdentityCongruence; if you're belonging, your identity is automatically congruent with the group.

**Critical Disagreement with CVA**: The nine axes assume that valuations can be independently varied. West African aesthetics shows that in collectivist contexts, **valuations are rigidly coupled**. You cannot design for high belonging and low status alignment; these co-vary.

### A3 Summary: Indian Panel Verdict

**Rasa Theory as Alternative Decomposition**: The panelists point out that Indian aesthetics has its own *nine-fold decomposition*—the nine rasas (śṛṅgāra, hāsya, karuṇā, raudra, vīra, bhayānaka, bībhatsa, adbhuta, śānta). The parallel is striking: CVA has 9 valuations; rasa theory has 9 emotions. **But the rasas are not independent dimensions; they are holistic aesthetic states that may combine or conflict**.

**Decomposability Question**: Indian aesthetics says that experiences *can* be described as rasas, but the rasas are not *causes*; they are *phenomenological descriptions*. The space evokes *śānta*; you experience śānta. You don't experience the *components* of śānta and then synthesize them. This is direct evidence that **CVA's decomposition may be post-hoc description, not real-time cause**, as Friston suggested in the main panel.

**Holism vs. Decomposition**: The Indian panelists unanimously argue that aesthetic experience is **irreducibly holistic**. You cannot separate Belonging from IdentityCongruence from RelatednessSupport in the tea ceremony room; they are unified in *śānta*. In the market, they are unified in *śṛṅgāra*. This directly supports **Barrett's objection: beauty might be a holistic gestalt, not a compressed vector**.

**Missing Cultural Axes**:
1. **Cosmic-Alignment / Dharmic-Congruence**: Alignment with universal order, not just local goals.
2. **Mandala-Coherence**: Sacred spatial geometry, not just layout efficiency.
3. **Sacred-Maturation**: Some experiences teach spiritual development (śānta), others activate ego-inflation (adbhuta in the wrong context).

### Structural Insights: Where the Nine Axes RECONFIGURE

1. **Japanese**: Belonging + IdentityCongruence + RelatednessSupport form a **dependence cluster** that can be fused or separated. Restoration comes through emptiness (ma), not individual recovery. Wabi-sabi introduces a separate aesthetic principle orthogonal to complexity/novelty.

2. **West African**: Status + Belonging + IdentityCongruence are **inseparably fused** around communal dignity (àṣà). You cannot have high belonging and low status; they co-vary. Individual autonomy is either irrelevant or negative.

3. **Indian**: Belonging, Identity, and Relatedness dissolve into **cosmic alignment**. The relevant decomposition is into rasas (emotional-aesthetic states), not valuations. Mandala-structure is a separate spatial principle orthogonal to CVA's constraints.

### Panelist Consensus on CVA

All three cultural panels agree on three critical points:

1. **The nine axes are not culturally universal**. They reflect Western individualist assumptions about autonomy, personal identity, and independence. Collectivist cultures require different axial structures.

2. **Valuations are not independent**. They co-vary in ways that CVA's linear model cannot capture. Belonging and Identity are coupled in ways that vary by culture.

3. **Aesthetic experience may be irreducibly holistic**. The attempt to decompose beauty into nine valuations may be post-hoc description rather than real-time causation. The experience is *rasa*, not *nine-dimensional vector compressed into words*.

**Barrett's Objection: PARTIALLY SUPPORTED**. The nine axes are indeed culturally constructed. However, the objection is incomplete—it's not just that *weights* differ by culture, but that **the structure itself transforms**. A revised CVA would need culture-specific valuation spaces, not just culture-specific weights.

## Panel B Design Problems

### Problem 1: A Meditation Center in Downtown Manhattan

**Brief**: Design a space for deep restoration in the middle of urban chaos. The site is a 2000 sq ft converted warehouse loft in Tribeca. Surrounding constraints: street noise (85+ dB), visual density, crowds, 24-hour activity. The user population is busy professionals seeking 30-minute respite breaks.

**Panelists**:
1. **Juhani Pallasmaa** voice (phenomenological architecture, sensory experience)
2. **Kengo Kuma** voice (material dissolution, anti-object architecture)
3. **Diébédo Francis Kéré** voice (community-participatory design, climate-responsive)
4. **Elizabeth Diller** voice (conceptual architecture, experiential design)

---

### Problem 1: Intuitive Designs (Before CVA)

**Pallasmaa's intuition**:

I would start with *sound*. The street noise is the problem, so isolation is obvious—but wrong. You don't restore by *removing* the city; you restore by *recontextualizing* it. I would use the exterior walls as a *acoustic labyrinth*. The building envelope has *depth*—not a simple wall, but a 3-foot-thick layered wall (fiberglass, air gap, recycled wool, air gap). As the sound travels through this wall, it *degrades*. But you can still *feel* the city's vibration through the floor.

Inside, I would use *water*—a shallow reflecting pool that covers half the floor. The water absorbs high frequencies and creates a *continuity* between the person and the city-sound. You're not isolated; you're in *dialogue* with the noise. The meditation is not escape but *acceptance*. The ceiling should be very high (20 feet) and dark, so you lose sense of the room's boundaries. You exist in *infinite darkness* while remaining aware of the city's pulse.

Materials matter: rough concrete (unfinished warehouse surfaces), water, maybe *wood* (but weathered, showing age). Not beautiful in a designer sense; beautiful in the sense of *honest materials speaking*.

**Kuma's intuition**:

I'm opposed to what I call "fortress architecture"—thick walls that *block the world*. Instead, I would make the boundary *permeable*. The space should feel like *you are not in a separate room, but in a pocket of the street*. The walls would be translucent, not opaque—frosted glass, semi-transparent materials. You can see the *shape* of the city but not the detail. The sound is still audible but *filtered*.

The key is *materiality*. The materials should be "weak"—they don't *announce* themselves. Natural wood that is allowed to weather and stain. No finished surfaces. The floor would be *loose* boards, maybe *sand* in some areas, so your footsteps are muffled and become *part of the meditation*. Sitting down, you are sitting on something *natural*, not *designed*.

I would create *negative space* through the arrangement of things—a few large boulders, a single tree (if possible), or *absence*. The emptiness is the design.

**Kéré's intuition**:

I would start by asking: *who* is meditating? If they are busy professionals from Manhattan, they are coming from a *context of constant social demand*. So the meditation needs to restore their *sense of agency*, their *connection to something non-corporate*.

I would build this space *with* the community. I would hire local workers from the neighborhood to construct it, using *salvaged materials* from the area. The space becomes a *gift* from the neighborhood to the professionals. The walls would be made of *reclaimed brick* from demolished buildings (there's history in every brick). The floor would be *wooden planks* from salvaged warehouses. The light would come through *simple windows*, maybe with adjustable shutters that the occupant can control.

The restoration comes from the *story of the materials*—knowing that these bricks have sheltered people before, that these boards have carried weight. The space teaches *continuity*, *stewardship*, *participation in the life of the city*.

**Diller's intuition**:

I would focus on *concept*. What does "meditation" mean in a downtown Manhattan context? It's not escape; it's *pause*. The space should enact pause.

I would create a *threshold*—a moment where you transition from the street to the interior. This might be as simple as a *black box entry* (you pass through a small dark space before arriving at the bright interior) or as elaborate as a *rotating screen* that you pass through, which slowly rotates to close after you enter, sealing the space but not *locking* it. The concept is: *the city is still happening outside, but I have momentarily stepped out of it*.

The interior would be almost *empty*—a large room with a single element of focus. Perhaps a *vertical light shaft* (natural light from a skylight), or a *water feature* (a narrow stream running across the floor). The emptiness is the concept. You bring your *own mind* to the space; the architecture doesn't *fill* it for you.

The walls might be white, but *very finely textured*—so they reflect light in complex ways depending on the time of day and your position. The space is *simple in form, complex in perception*.

### Problem 1: CVA Decomposition Applied

**Moderator shows CVA matrix**:

```
Design Problem: Meditation Center, Downtown Manhattan

Constraints (Environmental):
- Sound: 85+ dB street noise; high-frequency dominance
- Visual: Dense urban environment; visual complexity
- Temporal: Brief use (30-minute sessions); immediate need for restoration
- Spatial: 2000 sq ft; limited ceiling height

Valuation Target (Primary): RestorationValue (4-5)
Secondary Valuations:
- SafetyValue: 3 (need to feel secure, not trapped)
- InterestValue: 1-2 (minimal; you want boredom, not stimulation)
- AutonomySupportValue: 2 (ability to control light, sound, duration)
- IdentityCongruenceValue: 2 (the space should not demand a particular identity)

CVA Principle: To maximize RestorationValue while the environment maximizes *constraints*
(noise, complexity), you need to *invert* the constraint signal.

High RestorationValue = f(Low-ProcessingCost, High-Prospect, High-Refuge, Low-Threat)
High street noise = High-AcousticProcessingCost
=> Invert by creating intermediate acoustic layer (decay the signal)

High-Refuge is achieved by *permeability of boundary*, not *closure of boundary*.
Paradox: The boundary should be *psychologically opaque* (you feel private)
but *acoustically permeable* (you sense the city's presence).

Strategy: Use acoustic *absorption* (materials that decay sound)
+ *sympathetic resonance* (materials that resonate with certain frequencies,
turning threat-noise into presence-noise).
```

### Problem 1: Architect Responses to CVA

**Pallasmaa**:

This is excellent. CVA names what I was intuiting. The "acoustic inversion" insight—turning high noise into *sympathetic resonance*—is precisely what the layered wall does. It *absorbs* the threat-frequencies while *preserving* the low-frequency presence of the city. What CVA adds is *systematicity*. Without the framework, I might design *one* acoustic wall. With CVA, I see that I need to layer multiple inversions: acoustic (walls), visual (high ceiling, darkness), temporal (short sessions require *immediate* restoration, not gradual acclimation).

The insight about "psychological opacity + acoustic permeability" is new to me. I was thinking of restoration as *isolation*, and CVA reframes it as *recontextualization*. That changes the design. Now I would add a *visual window* (maybe small, high up) so you can see the *light* of the city without seeing the detail. You are simultaneously *inside and outside*. This is more sophisticated than my original intuition.

One thing CVA doesn't help with: *material honesty*. The framework doesn't explain why *rough concrete* is more restorative than *polished concrete*, even if both have the same acoustic and visual properties. That's still tacit knowledge.

**Kuma**:

CVA is helpful, but I want to push back. CVA focuses on *constraints* and their *inversion*. But my design philosophy is the opposite: *dissolve constraints into relationships*. The noise is not a *constraint to invert*; it's a *material to work with*.

The acoustic layer that Pallasmaa proposes is still *oppositional*—it's trying to *fight* the noise. I would instead *embrace* the noise. What if the meditation center has *no walls*? Just an *elevated platform* in the middle of the street, where you sit while the city moves around you. The noise is *full-spectrum*, unfiltered. Your meditation is not *escape* but *acceptance*.

That said, CVA helps me understand why this is *radical*. You're taking a constraint (noise) and *not inverting it*—you're accepting it at maximum intensity. This violates the RestorationValue target. But it achieves something else: **authentic presence**. You're not in a designed space; you're in the city. The space doesn't *restore* you; it *accepts* you.

So CVA is useful as *constraint*, not as *goal*. It helps me understand what I'm *rejecting* by not optimizing RestorationValue.

**Kéré**:

CVA is missing the *community dimension*. The meditation center for busy professionals is not just about individual restoration; it's about *reconnecting to shared place*. A space that is designed *with* the neighborhood and *from* the neighborhood's materials is restorative not because it has low ProcessingCost, but because it teaches *belonging*.

If I use CVA, I would add a *social valuation*: the space should support community participation in its creation and maintenance. This might increase *short-term ProcessingCost* (the space is less finished, more rough), but it increases *long-term RestorationValue* because it teaches *agency*. You restore yourself by *knowing that you shaped this space*.

CVA helps me articulate this: I'm trading off InterestValue (finish, polish) for BelongingValue (community participation). The framework makes that trade *visible*.

**Diller**:

CVA is about *solving problems*. But meditation is not a problem to solve; it's a *concept to enact*. The CVA framework tries to optimize RestorationValue, but what if the *concept* is "emptiness," and emptiness sometimes requires *boredom*, *difficulty*, *aesthetic roughness*?

That said, CVA helps me understand what I'm *conceptually opposing*. The framework says: "High RestorationValue = Low-ProcessingCost." My concept says: "True emptiness might require *confronting ProcessingCost*." This is a genuine disagreement, and CVA makes it explicit.

One thing CVA could add: a *concept layer*. Not just "what constraints are active," but "what narrative or idea is this space enacting?" The meditation center enacts the concept: "The city is always outside; I am briefly inside." That concept drives all the design decisions. CVA doesn't have language for concepts.

### Problem 1: Final Verdict

**Question**: Did CVA change your design? Did it reveal blind spots or constrain creativity?

**Consensus**:

1. **CVA helps with *articulation***. Architects intuitively understand trade-offs (ProcessingCost vs. RestorationValue), but CVA names them explicitly. This allows for *communication* and *rigor*.

2. **CVA constrains by *optimizing*. Kuma and Diller both emphasize that the best architectural ideas often come from *rejecting* optimization. If CVA says "maximize RestorationValue," the most interesting designs might come from *not maximizing RestorationValue*.

3. **CVA is missing *social and conceptual dimensions***. Kéré points out that restoration involves community; Diller points out that spaces enact concepts, not just optimize valuations.

4. **CVA is useful as a *tool for making trade-offs visible*, but not as a *prescriptive model* for design**.

### Problem 2: A Children's Hospital (Ages 5-12)

**Brief**: Design a hospital where the patients experience simultaneous support for SafetyValue (need for security and absence of threat), CompetenceSupportValue (need for mastery and agency), and InterestValue (need for stimulation and play). These may be in tension. The site is 50,000 sq ft across five floors. Patients are hospitalized for 3-14 days on average.

---

### Problem 2: Intuitive Designs (Before CVA)

**Pallasmaa**:

A children's hospital is fundamentally about *trust*. The child must feel that this place is *caring*, not threatening. The sensory experience is primary. The smell of the space—I would avoid sterile hospital smell (which says "sick people live here"). Instead, I would use *subtle natural smells*—maybe a *green plant-filled courtyard* with a gentle water feature. The sound should be *quiet but alive*—the sound of water, maybe gentle music, but never *silence* (which feels haunted in a hospital).

The color palette would be *warm*—earth tones, soft greens, warm whites (not cold surgical white). The lighting should have *variation* (not fluorescent flatness)—natural light from windows where possible, and warm artificial light elsewhere.

For CompetenceSupport, each patient should have a *small area of control*—a whiteboard where they can draw, a window that opens, buttons they can press. These small acts of agency are *restorative*.

But here's the paradox: children also need *play*, which is *chaos*. So you need to create *zones*: some zones are *safe and controlled* (the patient rooms), and some zones are *playful and chaotic* (the play areas). The architecture should make this *transition clear*.

**Kuma**:

Children are naturally *exploratory*. A hospital constrains exploration (you're ill, you can't leave your room). So I would design the building itself as a *playground*. The hallways would have *interesting materials*—walls you can touch (rough, smooth, warm, cold), floors that change texture, ceilings at different heights. Even as you're moving to a *threatening place* (the OR), you're moving through *interesting architecture*.

Each room would have a *view*—not a beautiful view (that's for adults), but an *active view*. You can see into other rooms, into the courtyard, into the kitchen where food is being prepared. You can see *life happening*, which is reassuring. The feeling is: "I'm sick, but the world is still going on; I'm part of it."

For CompetenceSupport, the room itself would be *partially unfinished*. There might be building materials visible (bricks, exposed wires), which teach the child *how the building is made*. That understanding is empowering.

**Kéré**:

I would prioritize *community* in the hospital. Children are separated from their families by illness; the space should minimize that separation. I would design *family rooms* where parents and siblings can sleep in the hospital. I would create *shared cooking spaces* where families can prepare food from home. The hospital becomes a *village*, not an institution.

For CompetenceSupport, I would ask the children *themselves* to decorate their rooms—with drawings, with objects from home, with meaning. The room becomes theirs, not the hospital's. The sense of ownership is *protective*.

For InterestValue, I would create *workshop spaces* where children can *make things*—art, music, simple woodwork. The activity itself is restorative, and it gives them agency.

**Diller**:

I would think about the *journey* of a child through the hospital. Arriving is *scary*. The first experience should be *wonder*, not *clinical*. I would create an *entry atrium* with a *dramatic light installation*—maybe a *moving light feature* that children can control. They press a button, and a light dances across a wall. This first *interaction* is an act of *agency*. "I can make things happen here."

Then, as you move deeper into the hospital, the spaces would get more *intimate*. The big atrium is public; your room is private. There's a *gradient* from public to private.

For CompetenceSupport, I would design the medical equipment to be *visible and intelligible*. The IV stand would have an interesting shape; the monitor would have *readable* displays. The child understands *what is happening to their body*. This understanding is *empowering*.

### Problem 2: CVA Decomposition Applied

```
Design Problem: Children's Hospital

Valuations (Simultaneous, Often in Tension):
- SafetyValue: 4-5 (child must feel protected, not abandoned)
- CompetenceSupportValue: 4 (child must feel agency and mastery)
- InterestValue: 3-4 (child must feel stimulated, not bored)

Constraint Interaction:
- Hospital is inherently threatening (medical procedures, strangers, loss of autonomy)
- Hospital is inherently constraining (movement limited, procedures scheduled)
- Hospital is inherently boring (long recovery periods, isolation from peers)

CVA Insight: The three valuations are *negatively coupled*.
- High SafetyValue requires *predictability*, *familiar adults*, *comfortable spaces*
- High CompetenceSupportValue requires *novelty*, *agency*, *challenge*
- High InterestValue requires *stimulation*, *surprise*, *complexity*

=> Cannot simply optimize all three simultaneously.

Strategy: *Segregate the valuations by space and time*.
- Patient room: High SafetyValue (familiar, calm, controlled by patient)
- Play areas: High InterestValue (diverse, complex, surprising)
- Procedural areas: High CompetenceSupportValue (transparent, intelligible, child-controlled)

Architecture must make these *transitions visible* so the child understands
which valuation is active in which zone.

Additional insight: *Separation is key*. Mixing SafetyValue and InterestValue
in the same space creates *cognitive conflict*. A room that is both
"safe and comfortable" and "exciting and stimulating" is neither.
```

---

### Problem 2: Architect Responses to CVA

**Pallasmaa**:

CVA makes explicit what I was intuiting: the three valuations are *in tension*. My solution was to *separate spaces*—safe rooms for sleep, play areas for stimulation. CVA validates this and goes further: it says *why this works*. The child's brain is not confused because the child understands the *transition*. When you walk from the patient room to the play area, you're entering a *different valuation zone*.

This suggests a design detail: *thresholds*. The transition between zones should be *marked*. A change in floor material, a color change, a doorway. The child's mind is ready: "Now I'm in the play zone; I can be stimulated." This is more sophisticated than my original intuition.

But CVA also misses something: *surprise within zones*. A safe room can have *subtle surprises*—a hidden shelf, a small window at kid-height. These don't undermine SafetyValue; they *enrich* it. So the segregation is not absolute; there's *layering*.

**Kuma**:

CVA says the three valuations are *in tension*. I want to reject that. I think they can be *woven together*. The hallway (which is a *transition zone*) can have high InterestValue (interesting materials, complex geometry) while maintaining SafetyValue (you're inside the building, staff is nearby). The play area can have high InterestValue (stimulation) and high SafetyValue (soft materials, no hazards).

But CVA helps me articulate this: the *key is making the tension explicit*. If the child understands that "interesting hallway = still safe," then both valuations are active. The danger is when InterestValue creates *genuine hazard* (sharp objects, unrestricted access), which violates SafetyValue. CVA helps me identify where the tension is *real* vs. *apparent*.

**Kéré**:

CVA is missing BelongingValue. The hospital is a place where children are *separated from family*. The restoration is not just about safe rooms or interesting play; it's about *maintaining connection to community*. I would add: the space should be designed so that family is *present and visible* throughout. The play area has space for parents to watch and participate. The patient room is sized for a parent to sleep alongside.

This might seem to violate the "segregation of valuations" principle, but it actually *integrates* them. A play area where parents are present is both *stimulating* (InterestValue) and *safe* (SafetyValue, because family is there) and *belonging* (BelongingValue, because family is integrated).

CVA would be stronger if it had *relational valuations*—valuations that are defined by the presence of *another person*, not just the space.

**Diller**:

CVA is helpful but incomplete. The problem is not just managing three valuations; it's *narrating* the child's experience. The hospital visit is a *journey*, and the architecture should enact a narrative.

Act One (Arrival): Wonder and agency. The child is introduced to the hospital through an experience of *power* (I can make things happen here).

Act Two (Treatment): Safety and transparency. The child understands the *why* of procedures; the medical equipment is demystified.

Act Three (Recovery): Interest and agency. The child is gradually given more *freedom* to explore and create.

The architecture doesn't just *optimize valuations*; it *stages a narrative of recovery*. CVA would be stronger if it could model *temporal dynamics* of valuations—how they change over the course of the hospital stay.

---

### Problem 2: Final Verdict

**Question**: Can CVA help you design this?

**Consensus**:

1. **CVA is useful for identifying *conflicts* between valuations**. The framework makes it clear that SafetyValue, CompetenceSupportValue, and InterestValue cannot be equally maximized in the same space. This directs architects toward *spatial segregation* and *clear transitions*.

2. **CVA underestimates the possibility of *integrated* valuations**. Kéré points out that family presence can simultaneously support Safety, Competence, and Belonging. The framework is too pessimistic about *coupling*.

3. **CVA is missing *temporal and narrative* dimensions**. Diller emphasizes that the hospital is not just a *space* but a *journey*. The valuations should *evolve* over time. CVA's static model cannot capture this.

4. **CVA is stronger as a *constraint-identification* tool than as a *design prescriptive***. Rather than "design to maximize these valuations," it's more useful to ask "which valuations will be in conflict here? How can we segregate or integrate them?"

**Zumthor's Concern: PARTIALLY ADDRESSED**. CVA does help architects identify design opportunities (spatial segregation, threshold design, transparent medical equipment). But it does not *generate* the phenomenological richness of the design. Architects still rely on *intuition* about materials, sensory experience, and narrative.

### Problem 3: A Multicultural Community Center (12 different immigrant groups)

**Brief**: Design a space that serves recent immigrants from 12 different countries, with different religious practices, social structures, and aesthetic preferences. The space must support **BelongingValue** and **IdentityCongruenceValue** simultaneously, even though these groups have different definitions of "home." The site is 30,000 sq ft.

---

### Problem 3: Intuitive Designs (Before CVA)

**Pallasmaa**:

I would start by rejecting the idea that *one space can feel like home to everyone*. Instead, I would create *multiple micro-homes*. Each cultural group would have a *small room* designed with materials and colors from their tradition. The Afghan group would have a room with *carpets and warm colors*. The Japanese group would have a room with *simplicity and wood*. The Somali group would have a room that is *open and light*.

But here's the key: these micro-homes are *not isolated*. They open onto a *common courtyard* where all groups gather. The architecture says: "You have your home here (your cultural room), and you also belong to this larger community (the courtyard)."

The sensory experience of moving from *specific cultural space* to *universal common space* is the design gesture.

**Kuma**:

I would reject the idea that *cultural rooms can be designed by outsiders*. Instead, I would ask: "What is *already* a home to these groups?" Maybe it's the *street markets* they come from. Maybe it's *public squares*. I would design the community center to *be a street market*.

The building would be very *open*—mostly covered markets, not enclosed rooms. There would be *multiple vendors* from different cultures selling food, crafts, items from home. The space *emerges* from the community, not from my design. My role is to create *infrastructure*—the roof structure, the basic layout—but the *life of the space* comes from the community using it.

This means the space is never *finished*. It's constantly *remade* by the community. That's the opposite of my usual design philosophy, but for a *multicultural space*, I think it's necessary.

**Kéré**:

I would involve the communities in the *design process itself*. Before designing, I would hold *workshops* with each group. "What does home feel like? What is important in your gathering spaces? What are your fears and hopes?" From these conversations, design principles would emerge.

Then, I would involve them in *building*. Young people from each community would learn *construction skills* by helping build the center. The building becomes a *gift from the community to itself*.

For the final space: I would create *shared cooking areas*. Food is the *universal home language*. In these kitchens, the Afghan grandmother teaches her granddaughter to make ashak, the Somali woman teaches her daughter to make sambusas, the Japanese couple teaches their friends to make miso soup. The space is *alive* with the *making of home*.

Each cultural group also has *private spaces*—for prayer, for celebration, for gathering. But the *connective tissue* is *shared making*.

**Diller**:

I would focus on *translation* rather than *assimilation*. The space doesn't try to *blend* all cultures into one; it celebrates *difference*. I would create multiple *galleries*—not museums, but living spaces where each group can *display and celebrate their culture*.

But I would also create *encounter spaces*—spaces where different groups *meet and learn*. A Japanese garden adjacent to an Afghan courtyard. They are different, but they are *in relationship*. The architecture enacts *dialogue*.

Each encounter space would have an *interpretive element*—maybe a *panel* explaining "why this Japanese garden uses rocks this way," "why Afghan courtyards have water features." The architecture teaches *cultural literacy*, not just *tolerance*.

### Problem 3: CVA Decomposition Applied

```
Design Problem: Multicultural Community Center

Valuations (Simultaneous, CONFLICTING):
- BelongingValue: High (each group wants to feel part of a community)
- IdentityCongruenceValue: High (each group wants their identity recognized)

Challenge: Different groups have DIFFERENT conceptions of belonging and identity.
- Afghan: Belonging = large family gathered; Identity = Muslim, honor-based

- Norwegian: Belonging = egalitarian; Identity = independent

CVA Question: Can you design a space where these *different* definitions
of BelongingValue are simultaneously satisfied?

Traditional CVA answer: Weight the valuations differently for different groups.
But this requires *segregated spaces*—each group has a high-BelongingValue
space designed for their specific conception.

Problem: Segregation violates the *whole purpose* of the community center
(to bring groups together).

Deeper Problem: Belonging and IdentityCongruence may not be orthogonal.
- For the Afghan group: Identity (Muslim) + Belonging (family) are *inseparable*.
- For the Japanese group: Identity (humble) + Belonging (subtle connection) are *fused*.

So the nine-axis model assumes Belonging and IdentityCongruence can be
independently varied. But in this space, they are *structurally coupled*.
```

### Problem 3: Architect Responses to CVA

**Pallasmaa**:

CVA shows the problem clearly: you cannot have *one space* that simultaneously supports *different cultural definitions* of BelongingValue and IdentityCongruence. My solution is: create *multiple spaces*, but connect them. This way, each group has high Belonging and IdentityCongruence in their *cultural room*, and the community has *belonging via the shared courtyard*.

But CVA also raises a deeper question: Is this solution *genuine* community building, or is it *tolerant segregation*? The groups are near each other but not *integrated*. CVA doesn't help me answer this ethical question.

**Kuma**:

CVA is too *spatial*. The real question is not "what space supports BelongingValue," but "what *activity* brings people together?" The answer is not architecture; it's *use*. If the Afghan family and the Japanese family *cook together*, they belong together. If the space is just *adjacent*, they don't.

So I would design for *programmatic integration*—spaces where *activities mix*. Maybe a shared *garden* where the Afghan family plants vegetables and the Japanese family maintains a zen garden. They're doing different things, but they're doing them *together*.

**Kéré**:

I agree with Kuma. The real solution is *participation*. You don't design a multicultural space; you *facilitate* a community designing itself. The space emerges from the *shared work* of building it. That shared work is what creates belonging, not the architecture.

CVA tries to solve this through *spatial design*, but the real solution is *social design*.

**Diller**:

CVA helps me articulate the problem: BelongingValue and IdentityCongruence are *decoupled in Western individualism* but *coupled in many cultures*. My architecture should reflect this. I would create spaces where cultural identity is *visible and celebrated*, and these spaces are *integrated* (not segregated).

So the Japanese garden is not "the space for Japanese people"; it's "a space that enacts Japanese aesthetic principles, and everyone can experience it." The Afghan courtyard is not "for Afghan families"; it's "a space that enacts Afghan social principles."

The architecture teaches *cultural literacy*. You experience the Japanese garden and you understand: "Ah, emptiness is beautiful." You experience the Afghan courtyard and you understand: "Ah, gathering around water is sacred."

This way, *everyone* has high IdentityCongruenceValue (because everyone can encounter their own culture) and high BelongingValue (because everyone learns and celebrates each other's cultures).

**Consensus**:

1. **CVA exposes the problem: BelongingValue and IdentityCongruenceValue are culturally contingent.** The framework is most useful for identifying where valuations *conflict* across cultures.

2. **CVA is insufficient for the solution. All four architects point to social and programmatic factors (shared making, participation, cultural literacy) as more important than spatial design.**

3. **CVA would be stronger with a *social dimension***—how do spaces *facilitate activity and encounter*? The current framework focuses on *perceptual and emotional* valuations, not *social and relational* ones.

### D1: Friston's Precision-Weighting Question

**Original Objection**: "You say ActivityFrame = precision allocation in the hierarchical generative model. Does this *fully* resolve active inference integration, or are there mathematical gaps?"

**Friston's Assessment**:

The mathematical formalization shows that ActivityFrame can be interpreted as **precision-weighted hierarchical inference**. This is sound. Here's the critical path:

(1) The predictive processing hierarchy has multiple timescales. Fast (sensory), medium (constraint inference), slow (valuation).

(2) At each level, uncertainty is encoded as *precision* (inverse variance). The hierarchical system allocates precision to the levels that are most *informative* for the current goal.

(3) When the goal frame *shifts*, the precision allocation shifts. What was "noise" (low-precision sensory signal) becomes "signal" (high-precision, goal-relevant).

(4) This precision allocation is *exactly what CVA's ActivityFrame models*. It says: "These constraints are active (high precision) given this goal."

**Does this fully resolve active inference integration?** Mostly yes, but there is **one remaining gap**: *How does the system *learn* which constraints are relevant to which goals?* The formalization assumes you already know which constraints to allocate precision to. But learning this coupling is non-trivial.

**Mathematical Status**: RESOLVED FOR INFERENCE, NEEDS WORK FOR LEARNING.

If you want a complete model, you need explicit learning dynamics—how does the system update its understanding of which constraints support which goals? The formalization touches on this (Eq. 4.2) but doesn't fully specify it.

**Friston's Recommendation**: Add a **meta-learning layer** that tracks the history of goal-constraint couplings. The system learns: "When I want RestorativeValue, I should pay attention to low-frequency sounds and visual emptiness." This learning could be modeled using **Bayesian model comparison** (comparing alternative constraint-goal couplings) or **meta-reinforcement learning**.

---

### D2: The Compatibility Guarantee

**Original Objection**: "CVA reduces to current ATLAS when w_{ji}(A) = identity. Is this sufficient to guarantee backward compatibility?"

**Jordan's Assessment**:

Yes, this is **mathematically sufficient**, but let me be precise about what "backward compatibility" means.

The reduction is: **When w_{ji}(A) maps the valuation vector v directly to policy π (no intermediate constraint layer), CVA collapses to ATLAS.**

This is correct. It means:
- Old ATLAS models that directly map features → policy can be re-interpreted as **CVA with w_{ji} = identity**.
- The mathematical structure of CVA is a *proper generalization* of ATLAS.

**Is this sufficient for practical compatibility?** Almost, but there's one caveat. Old ATLAS models have *learned weights* that were optimized for the feature→policy mapping. When you re-interpret them as CVA's w_{ji}, those weights may not be *optimal* for the new interpretation.

Example: An old ATLAS model might have learned that "high-prospect" → "prefer this space." When you re-interpret this as a CVA model, you're saying: "high-prospect" activates "RestorativeValue," and "RestorativeValue" then activates preference. The learned weight from "prospect" to "RestorativeValue" might be different from the learned weight from "prospect" to "preference."

**Mathematical Status**: TECHNICALLY COMPATIBLE, with CAVEAT for optimization.

**Jordan's Recommendation**: When migrating old ATLAS models to CVA, **retrain the weights** (w_{ji}) using the same data that trained the original ATLAS model. This ensures that the new CVA model makes the same predictions as the old ATLAS model, while gaining the interpretability of the constraint-valuation decomposition.

### D3: Serial vs. Parallel Composition

**Original Objection**: "Current ATLAS has serial chains: d_eff = min(d_i), ω_eff = ∏ω_i. How do these work when the projection passes through a valuation layer?"

**Strogatz's Assessment**:

This is a **subtle but solvable problem**. The issue is:

In ATLAS, a *serial chain of constraints* multiplies their effects: if you have two constraints (both at 50% effectiveness), the combined effect is 25%. This makes sense for *independent constraints* (two barriers to entering a space).

But in CVA, when constraints feed into *valuations*, which then feed into *policy*, the composition is **different**. You can't just multiply; you need to account for *how the valuation layer transforms the signal*.

Here's the key insight: **The valuation layer acts as a *nonlinear filter***. A constraint that is (empirically) 50% effective might be (in valuation space) 10% effective or 80% effective, depending on the goal frame.

Example:
- Constraint: "Space is very dense (100 people/1000 sq ft)"
- Effect in ATLAS: Decreases *openness preference* (direct inhibition)
- Effect in CVA: Activates *BelongingValue* (if goal is social connection) OR activates *Threat* (if goal is personal space)

So the compositional rule **must account for the goal context**.

**Mathematical Formulation**:

For serial constraints passing through valuation:

```
d_eff = min(d_i)  [unchanged — spatial constraint]
ω_eff(A) = ∏[ω_i * v(w_i, A)]  [NEW: weighted by activated valuations]
```

Where v(w_i, A) is the valuation activation for constraint i given goal frame A.

**Mathematical Status**: SOLVABLE, with MODIFIED COMPOSITION RULE.

**Strogatz's Recommendation**: The modified rule is:

```
ω_eff(A) = ∏[ω_i * exp(-λ * (1 - v_activated(i, A)))]
```

Where:
- ω_i is the original ATLAS weight
- v_activated(i, A) is the proportion of constraint i's signal that activates valuations given goal A
- λ is a scaling parameter (controls how much the valuation layer modulates the constraint effect)

This ensures that constraints have **baseline effects** (the ω_i term) but are **modulated by the goal frame** (the valuation activation term).

### D4: Beauty as Sufficient Statistic

**Original Question**: "If B ≈ L(v) is a sufficient statistic for the valuation vector v, what information is lost? Can we bound the information loss?"

**Friston, Jordan, Strogatz (Joint Assessment)**:

This is the **deepest remaining mathematical question**. It goes to the heart of Barrett's objection.

**What it means**: A sufficient statistic *preserves* the information needed for some task (predicting behavior, understanding preference), while *discarding* information not needed for that task.

If B (beauty) is a sufficient statistic for v (valuations), then:
- Predicting policy from B is as good as predicting policy from v
- But describing the *phenomenology* (what the experience feels like) from B might lose information

**Information-Theoretic Bound**:

The information lost is bounded by the **mutual information** between different valuation vectors that map to the same beauty score:

```
I_lost = I(v1; v2 | B)  [Mutual information between two valuation vectors, given beauty]
```

Example: Two spaces might both feel "beautiful" (same B) but for different reasons:
- Space A: high RestorativeValue + low InterestValue
- Space B: low RestorativeValue + high InterestValue + high StatusValue

If both map to B = "beautiful," then the information about *why* it's beautiful is lost.

**Can we bound this?** Yes, using the **data processing inequality**:

```
I_lost ≤ min(H(v1), H(v2))  [Limited by entropy of valuation vectors]
```

Where H(v) is the Shannon entropy of the valuation distribution.

**Practical Bound**: In realistic scenarios with human perception, the information loss is probably **10-30%** of the total information in the valuation vector. This is *substantial but not catastrophic*.

**Mathematical Status**: MATHEMATICALLY FORMULATED, BOUNDS ARE EMPIRICALLY TESTABLE.

**Jordan's Recommendation**: To validate whether L(v) → B is a good sufficient statistic, **run empirical studies** where you:

1. Measure the full valuation vector v for a diverse set of spaces (using pairwise comparisons, rating scales, etc.)
2. Collect beauty ratings (B) for the same spaces
3. Train a model that predicts B from v
4. Measure the **residual information** (how much variance in v is not explained by B)
5. Check whether the unexplained variance is *task-irrelevant* (doesn't affect policy) or *task-relevant* (does affect policy)

If unexplained variance is task-irrelevant, then B is a good sufficient statistic. If task-relevant, then you need to include additional factors beyond B.
