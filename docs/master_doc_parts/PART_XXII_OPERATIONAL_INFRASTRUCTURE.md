# PART XXII: Operational Infrastructure — The Epistemic Nervous System

*Last updated: 2026-03-03*

## §148: The Operational Layer — Why Epistemic Systems Need Self-Monitoring

An epistemic system that does not detect its own failures is, in a deep sense, epistemically irresponsible. This principle — that a system must be able to recognize when it has erred, locate the error, and correct it — is not merely a computational convenience but a foundational commitment to the possibility of knowledge itself. The operational infrastructure of Article Eater represents neither a concession to engineering pragmatism nor a late-stage patch applied after failures became too frequent to ignore. Rather, it embodies a deliberate architectonic choice grounded in three distinct but convergent intellectual traditions: the formal discipline of Dijkstra's structured programming, Peirce's theory of self-correcting inquiry, and Haack's foundherentist epistemology.

When Dijkstra argued, in his seminal 1968 paper, that the goto statement should be considered harmful, his concern was fundamentally one of *verification*. A program that uses arbitrary jumps destroys the local invariants that would otherwise guarantee correctness of each component. The operational layer of Article Eater takes this insight seriously: the system is decomposed into components (reflexes, validators, the overseer) such that each can maintain verifiable invariants. A reflex that detects a malformed field name operates under the assumption that all other data structures remain consistent. The overseer checks global coherence only after local components have certified their outputs. This is not merely good engineering; it is the computational embodiment of Dijkstra's discipline.

Peirce's philosophy of science, developed across his late nineteenth-century writings on the fixation of belief and the self-correcting method of science, holds that the essence of scientific reasoning is its capacity to detect error. Peirce writes: "The method of science is based upon the principle that any and every statement whatever shall be assumed to be doubtful when tested by the community of inquiry until it is sufficiently confirmed by evidence." The operational layer institutionalizes this principle. Every belief (extraction record, credence assignment, causal claim) is subjected to multiple rounds of scrutiny: field-level validation, effect-size checking, confounder assessment, coherence evaluation, and finally overseer inspection. A claim that appears valid at one level may trigger alerts at another. This layered approach reflects Peirce's insight that error-detection is not a one-time gate but an ongoing process, distributed across time and across multiple independent checks.

Susan Haack's foundherentism, synthesized in *Evidence and Inquiry*, proposes that genuine justification requires both grounding and coherence. A belief is justified not merely because it coheres with other beliefs, nor merely because it has some independent evidential basis, but because it satisfies both conditions simultaneously. The operational layer embodies this synthesis. The extraction_field_validator ensures that each extracted claim has a clear, traceable basis in the source document (grounding). The overseer, by contrast, monitors the coherence of the entire web of beliefs, checking that no subset has become locally inconsistent or subject to systematic drift. Neither layer alone would be sufficient; together, they implement the foundherentist standard that Article Eater demands.

The operational infrastructure, taken as a whole, can be understood as the system's "peripheral nervous system." Biological organisms do not wait for conscious deliberation to withdraw a hand from a flame; the reflex arc responds instantaneously at the spinal cord level, allowing for rapid correction of imminent threats. Analogously, Article Eater's reflexes detect common error patterns — malformed direction fields, vague antecedent descriptions, missing effect sizes — and respond immediately, either by automatic correction (when safe) or by escalation and quarantine (when the error threatens system integrity). The overarching overseer, by contrast, functions like higher cognitive processes, monitoring system-level health, assessing long-term trends in coherence and coverage, and making strategic decisions about which beliefs deserve continued confidence.

Bounded rationality, as Herbert Simon articulated, means that a resource-limited system cannot verify all of its claims with equal rigor. The system cannot, in principle, re-test every causal hypothesis from first principles on every integration cycle. Rather, it must allocate its verification effort strategically, focusing intensive scrutiny on high-stakes claims (those central to the web, those carrying high credence, those that have recently produced anomalies) while relying on lighter heuristic checks for peripheral claims. The operational layer distributes verification work across four temporal modes — immediate post-integration checking (reflexes), nightly comprehensive audits (overseer periodic mode), statistical anomaly detection (baselines and control charts), and human review queues (the quarantine protocol). This distribution reflects Simon's insight that rationality is achieved not through exhaustive verification but through *judicious allocation of limited cognitive resources*.

In what follows, we examine the five operational systems that collectively implement this vision: the reflex system, which detects and locally corrects anomalies; success conditions, which define contractual obligations between components; the overseer, which monitors system-level health and enforces invariants; signal-based coordination, which manages distributed work without requiring centralized locking; and the QA validators, which implement epistemic standards as computable rules. Together, these systems ensure that Article Eater is not merely a belief-storage system but a *self-knowing* system, capable of recognizing its own limitations and correcting its own errors.

## §149: The Reflex System — Detect, Fix, Report

A reflex, in the biological sense, is a stereotyped motor response to a sensory stimulus, mediated by the spinal cord without conscious involvement. The reflex system in Article Eater follows this model: each reflex is a simple (detect, fix, report) triple that runs autonomously, examining a specific aspect of system state and responding according to fixed rules. The beauty of this architecture lies in its separation of concerns. A reflex does not need permission to act; it does not require consultation with other components; it operates on its own cycle and reports the outcome. This autonomy enables rapid response to common, well-understood failure modes.

The reflex system currently implements thirteen distinct reflexes, organized by domain. Those prefixed RFX-EXT address extraction-level issues: malformed values, missing mandatory fields, values outside expected ranges. RFX-SCH reflexes monitor schema conformance and field-type consistency. RFX-CAL addresses calibration drifts and anomalous distributions. RFX-PIP handles pipeline-level issues such as stalled processes, missing intermediate output files, and resource depletion.

Consider the DirectionNormalizationReflex, which operates at the field level. The field *direction_of_effect* is meant to contain standardized values: "increase", "decrease", "bidirectional", "unknown". However, during manual data entry, during batch imports from external databases, or during LLM-assisted extraction, errors accumulate. An analyst might enter "incr" as shorthand; a database might export the value "pos" (meaning positive association); an LLM might hallucinate "up" or "rise". The reflex detects these variations by maintaining a mapping table of common synonyms and typos, applies a deterministic normalization rule, logs the correction, and records severity. A systematic pattern (90% of corrections are "incr" → "increase" from a particular batch import) triggers an escalation: the batch itself becomes suspect, and an overseer alert is issued.

The VagueAntecedentDetectorReflex illustrates a complementary design principle: some errors are too complex for automatic repair and must be escalated to human review. When a source document makes a causal claim about "the environment" without specifying whether it refers to the physical environment, the social environment, the organizational environment, or some subset, the reflex detects the vagueness (by matching against a registry of known vague terms and checking that no specificity context is available in nearby sentences), flags the record as requiring annotation review, and places it in a quarantine queue. The reflex does not attempt to disambiguate; it simply reports the problem and ensures it receives human attention.

Event logging is central to the reflex architecture. Each reflex execution is recorded in a daily JSONL file with structure: timestamp, reflex_id, detected_pattern, severity (INFO, WARNING, ERROR, CRITICAL), action_taken, outcome (SUCCESS, PARTIAL, FAILED), record_ids_affected, and notes. These logs accumulate in a directory structure organized by date and reflex domain. Additionally, a lightweight SQLite database, overseer.db, maintains summary statistics: for each reflex, the count of daily executions, successful corrections, escalations, and failure modes. This dual approach—detailed JSONL for debugging and trend analysis, aggregated database for high-level monitoring—reflects Simon's principle that the system should maintain both fine-grained evidence and summary statistics.

The design principles underlying the reflex system merit explicit articulation. First, *local autonomy with global visibility*: a reflex operates independently, making decisions and corrections without requiring permission from a central authority, but every action is logged and available for inspection. This is the Unix philosophy applied to epistemic work: small, focused tools that do one thing well and that record their actions transparently. Second, *graceful degradation*: if a reflex definition file is corrupted or missing, the system does not crash; instead, that reflex is skipped, an alert is issued, and remaining reflexes continue operation. Third, *severity escalation*: INFO-level events (routine corrections) are recorded but do not trigger alerts; WARNING-level events (patterns that suggest deeper problems) are accumulated and reviewed nightly; ERROR-level events (definite contradictions or policy violations) are reported immediately and may trigger automatic quarantine; CRITICAL events (e.g., a reflex itself crashes) trigger emergency protocols. Fourth, and most subtly, *success condition linkage*: each reflex references at least one success condition (defined in §150) that it helps to maintain. The DirectionNormalizationReflex, for instance, ensures that EFV-SC1 (all direction fields contain only standardized values) is satisfied.

Why not simply validate all inputs at the pipeline entry point? Why distribute validation across reflexes, field validators, effect-size checkers, and the overseer? The answer lies in understanding the true topology of error entry in Article Eater. Data enters the system through multiple paths: the scheduled extraction pipeline (human-annotated documents), batch imports from external databases, LLM-assisted extraction protocols, manual edits by authorized reviewers, and re-calibration processes. Errors introduced through one path may not be caught by validators designed for another path. A batch import might conform to the external database's schema but violate Article Eater's coherence constraints. A manual edit might repair an extraction error but introduce an inconsistency with other related beliefs. An LLM-assisted extraction might produce syntactically valid output that is nonetheless semantifically incoherent (claiming, for instance, both a causal relationship and its negation in adjacent rows). This multiplicity of entry points creates a defense-in-depth challenge: a single validation gate cannot cover all cases. Reflexes provide a solution by operating post-hoc, examining the live system state regardless of how data arrived, and responding to patterns that emerge only after integration.

The reflex system currently executes on three schedules: immediately post-integration (for critical reflexes that must run before any downstream processing), at hourly intervals (for reflexes that accumulate patterns and need periodic flushing), and nightly (for expensive reflexes that require full database scans). The scheduler is implemented as a simple cron-driven Python process that reads the reflex registry, checks dependencies, and executes reflexes in topological order. Failures in one reflex do not block others; instead, exceptions are caught, logged, and reported.

## §150: Success Conditions — The Contractual Basis of System Health

A success condition is a testable claim about system behavior. Specifically: *given a component A that takes input from source S and produces output to sink T, a success condition is a property of the output that must be satisfied for A to have successfully completed its contracted work*. The concept derives directly from design-by-contract methodology, developed by Bertrand Meyer and others in object-oriented software engineering, but applied here to an epistemic system. Each module makes a promise: *if given valid input, I will produce output satisfying these conditions*. The system as a whole is a web of contracts, and the overseer's job (discussed in §151) is to verify that every component is honoring its commitments.

Article Eater currently maintains 62 explicit success conditions, organized into nine groups corresponding to major script and module clusters. The scheduled_pipeline group (SP-SC1 through SP-SC6) includes success conditions such as: *SP-SC1: The scheduled_extract.py job completes without uncaught exceptions*; *SP-SC3: The discovery extraction produces at least one new hypothesis (discovery output count > 0) OR the corpus is exhausted*; *SP-SC5: Backfilled extraction produces at least one effect-size file in the designated output directory*. The decision_tree_analysis group (KDT-SC1 through KDT-SC6) specifies conditions on the causal decision tree: *KDT-SC2: All internal nodes have at least one causal hypothesis (non-zero in-degree)*; *KDT-SC4: No cycle exists in the instantiated decision tree*. The backfill_operationalizations group (BO-SC1 through BO-SC6) ensures that operationalization cache consistency is maintained. And so forth across extraction_queue, extraction_field_validator, effect_size_validator, confounder_risk_checker, credence_intervals, and aeshi_scoring groups.

The structure of each success condition is deliberate and precise. It includes: an identifier (e.g., SP-SC3), a human-readable name, a prose description of what the condition asserts, a metric expressed in pseudocode that could be computed from system state, a threshold (e.g., "count > 0" or "percentage within [0.95, 1.05]"), a reference to the test or monitoring rule that checks this condition, and a rationale explaining why this condition is essential. The rationale often connects to external epistemic principles: *SP-SC6: All extracted claims carry provenance metadata. Rationale: Haack's foundherentism requires that beliefs be grounded; provenance is the minimal grounding requirement*. This explicit linkage between system constraints and epistemological theory is essential for maintaining coherence between Article Eater's engineering and its philosophical foundation.

Consider a concrete example: *EFV-SC3: For every extracted claim, the p-value field (if present) satisfies p ≤ 1.0. Rationale: The p-value is a probability; the Kolmogorov axioms require that any probability be bounded by 1.0. A p-value exceeding 1.0 indicates either a data entry error, an LLM hallucination, or a fundamental misunderstanding of the source document. This condition is violated if even a single record violates it; threshold is 'all' (100% conformance)*. Note the precision: the rationale grounds the condition in formal probability theory; the threshold is absolute, not statistical; the check is simple to compute; and the failure mode is grave (provokes immediate alert).

The most critical insight embedded in the success conditions framework is the *zero-output prevention principle*. Many conditions explicitly require that a component produce something: *SP-SC3: discovery output > 0*, *SP-SC5: extraction files > 0*, *GEQ-SC4: queued hypotheses > 0*. Why is this so important? Silent failures—where a module runs to completion, produces no errors, consumes resources, and exits successfully, yet produces zero useful output—are the most dangerous failure mode in an epistemic system. A module that crashes is obvious; alerts fire, the system halts, attention is directed. A module that silently produces nothing is insidious. It may run nightly for weeks before anyone notices that no new beliefs are being integrated, no new hypotheses are being evaluated. The success conditions framework makes zero-output visible. If the discovery pipeline runs but produces zero hypotheses (perhaps because the search strategy has drifted, or the corpus has been inadvertently cleared), SP-SC3 fails, and the failure is reported and escalated.

This principle reflects both Peirce's insight on error-detection and Simon's principle of bounded rationality. Peirce emphasizes that science must be able to recognize when it has made a mistake; a silent failure is a mistake the system cannot recognize. Simon emphasizes that a resource-limited system must focus its verification efforts strategically; zero-output checks are high-leverage: they are cheap to compute but catch a vast class of subtle failures.

The success conditions are checked at multiple points in the system lifecycle. The extraction_field_validator, discussed in §153, checks relevant conditions (EFV-SC1 through EFV-SC6) immediately after field extraction. The effect_size_validator checks its conditions during the integration pipeline. The overseer performs comprehensive audits of all conditions on a nightly schedule (overseer periodic mode, discussed in §151). Additionally, success conditions are incorporated into the continuous integration test suite: a test failure indicates that a component is no longer satisfying its contract.

The success conditions framework provides several non-obvious benefits beyond simple fault detection. First, it serves as a form of *contractual documentation*: anyone reading the conditions for a given module understands what that module is obligated to produce and what standards its output must meet. Second, it enables *blame assignment*: if a success condition fails, the responsible module is identified by which condition failed. Third, it supports *graceful degradation*: the system can check whether a component is meeting its obligations even if its internal implementation has drifted; if conditions are satisfied, the system continues; if they are not, specific corrective actions are triggered. Fourth, it provides a basis for *performance analysis*: by tracking how frequently each component comes close to violating its success conditions (say, SP-SC3 producing exactly 1 hypothesis when threshold is > 0), one can identify components under stress or approaching failure.

## §151: The Overseer — Superordinate Monitoring for Coherentist Systems

The overseer is the apex of the operational hierarchy, the system-level monitor that watches for threats to core invariants and coordinates responses. Its architecture reflects a sophisticated understanding of how complex systems should maintain integrity across temporal evolution and across the coupling between local and global properties.

The overseer comprises six components: the Health Monitor (which checks current system state against invariants), the Integrity Checker (which scans for logical contradictions and constraint violations), the Completeness Auditor (which assesses coverage across the design space), the Maintenance Engine (which coordinates cleanup and optimization tasks), the Quarantine Manager (which handles beliefs suspected of corruption or incoherence), and Baselines & Statistical Alerting (which detects anomalous trends). These components share a common database, overseer.db, distinct from the main web.db, reflecting Parnas's principle of information hiding: the overseer maintains its own state separate from the operational database, reducing coupling and enabling the overseer itself to be monitored for consistency.

The overseer enforces thirteen explicit invariants, each with a severity level and a human-readable name:

*INV-0 (OPERATIONAL)*: The system is bootstrapped. All mandatory initialization files exist and are readable. This is the minimal precondition for any other invariant to be meaningful.

*INV-1 (PROVENANCE)*: Every belief in the web carries traceable provenance. This is Haack's grounding requirement: a coherentist system cannot justify a belief by appeal to coherence alone; it must trace the belief back to evidential sources. Violations indicate either data corruption or a fundamental epistemic breach.

*INV-2 (BN-WEB SYNC)*: The edges in the Bayesian network representation match the credences in the web. This invariant connects the causal inference layer (discussed in Part VII on causality) with the credence representation. If the network and the credences drift apart, downstream causal inferences become unreliable.

*INV-4 (COHERENCE DECLINE)*: The average coherence of the web declines by no more than 5 percent per integration cycle. A larger drop suggests that a new batch of incoherent or contradictory beliefs has been injected, signaling either a data quality problem upstream or a fundamental shift in the domain that the model has not yet adapted to.

*INV-5 (CREDENCE BOUNDS)*: No belief carries a credence outside the interval [0, 1]. This is a basic application of the Kolmogorov axioms for probability; its violation indicates either a calculation error or data corruption.

*INV-6 (COVERAGE)*: The web covers the major variables and causal pathways identified in the initial domain theory. Violations suggest that important causal pathways are being systematically ignored or that the extraction pipeline is drifting away from its core mission.

*INV-7 (TEMPLATE FIDELITY)*: Extracted claims conform to the templates defined in the domain knowledge representation. Violations indicate that either the templates have drifted out of sync with the extraction process or that the extraction process has been corrupted.

*INV-8 (THEORY CONSISTENCY)*: Core theoretical beliefs (those marked as Theory or confirmed by domain experts) remain internally consistent. This invariant protects against gradual erosion of foundational knowledge through the integration of contradictory empirical claims.

*INV-9 (DIVERSITY)*: Causal hypotheses come from multiple sources (multiple papers, multiple research groups, multiple methodologies). Homogeneous evidence raises the risk of systematic bias. This invariant enforces epistemic pluralism.

*INV-10 (WARRANT STABILITY)*: The average warrant for causal beliefs does not decline sharply (>10% drop per integration). Warrant is computed via the AESHI framework (discussed in §153); a sharp decline suggests that underlying data quality has degraded or that the warrant model itself has become misaligned with reality.

*INV-11 (ANNOTATION DENSITY)*: Expert annotations are distributed across causal hypotheses according to plan (at least 10% of hypotheses carry expert annotation). Unannotated beliefs, while not invalid, are more prone to systematic error. This invariant ensures that core regions of the belief space receive expert scrutiny.

*INV-12 (T3 THRESHOLD)*: The number of beliefs at the T3 confidence level (0.85–0.95) does not exceed 25% of the total. Excessive concentration at T3 suggests that the system is neither integrating evidence (which would push beliefs higher) nor detecting contradictions (which would push them lower); stagnation in the T3 band is an anomaly.

*INV-13 (REVIEWER TERMINAL RATE)*: The rate at which reviewer queues reach terminal state (resolved through human decision) is at least 80% per month. Queues that accumulate without resolution indicate that the overseer is flagging issues faster than humans can resolve them—a sign that either the flagging criteria are too sensitive or that human review capacity has been exceeded.

The overseer operates in four distinct modes, each serving a different temporal scale and purpose. The *POST-INTEGRATION mode* runs immediately after a new batch of beliefs is integrated into the web. This mode is relatively fast, checking critical invariants (INV-0, INV-1, INV-5) and computing aggregate statistics. If a critical invariant is violated, the integration is rolled back, and the problematic batch is quarantined. The *PERIODIC mode* runs nightly, performing comprehensive checks of all thirteen invariants, constructing detailed reports, and identifying long-term trends. The *ON-DEMAND mode* is invoked by a human analyst or by another system component (e.g., a validator) when a specific concern needs investigation. Finally, the *ALERT mode* is triggered when a statistical anomaly is detected or when a queued issue reaches a threshold for escalation.

The quarantine protocol (specified in O-3 of the overseer's design, developed during an 18-person expert panel convened to formalize the operational layer) embodies a fundamental commitment to epistemic caution. When a belief is suspected of corruption or incoherence—because it violates a constraint, contradicts other highly confident beliefs, or comes from a batch marked as suspect—it is not automatically retired from the web. Instead, it is placed in quarantine status, visible in reports and excluded from downstream inference, but retained in the database. A human reviewer examines the quarantined belief, compares it against the source document, checks whether the contradiction is genuine or the result of a classifier error, and makes a reasoned decision about whether to retain or retire the belief. This protocol reflects the recognition that removing a belief from the web is a consequential, potentially irreversible action (the belief may have been cited by other researchers, integrated into downstream models, or embedded in explanations that have been communicated to stakeholders). The default action is conservation: preserve the belief until human judgment confirms that it should be removed.

Statistical alerting (O-2) provides a complementary approach to invariant checking. For each theory in the domain (e.g., "biological mechanisms" or "organizational incentives"), the overseer maintains a running baseline of average coherence within that theory and average warrant for beliefs under that theory. These baselines are updated nightly using exponential smoothing (with smoothing constant α = 0.3, updated based on observed drift patterns). An anomaly alert is triggered if the current mean for any theory deviates by more than one standard deviation from its baseline. If a theory's mean coherence suddenly drops, it signals that either a batch of contradictory claims has been integrated or that the theory model has drifted; either way, human attention is warranted. This approach, inspired by statistical process control (Shewhart control charts), allows the system to detect subtle shifts that might not trigger any individual invariant but that collectively indicate an emerging problem.

The AESHI scoring framework (Authenticity, Evidence, Specificity, Heterogeneity, Integration) is integral to the overseer's assessment of belief quality. AESHI is discussed more fully in §153 in the context of QA validation, but its role in the overseer warrants mention here. Each belief carries an AESHI score, a weighted average of five factors: authenticity (does the claim genuinely represent what the source says?), evidence (is the source empirically grounded?), specificity (is the claim sufficiently precise to be falsifiable?), heterogeneity (does the claim come from a diverse source?), and integration (does the claim cohere with other beliefs?). The overseer uses AESHI as a primary lens for assessing system health. A sudden drop in average AESHI across a batch suggests that either the extraction quality has degraded or that the validation layer has drifted. A persistent low AESHI for a particular theory suggests that the theory is either underdeveloped (lacks good evidence) or poorly grounded (lacks strong source alignment). These patterns inform the overseer's assessments and guide it toward appropriate interventions: retraining validators, requesting deeper investigation of a theory, or adjusting thresholds for what levels of evidence are considered acceptable.

The overseer embodies Quine's distinction, discussed in Part VI, between peripheral and central beliefs, now applied at the system level. Local reflexes and validators (the epistemic periphery) handle rapid, autonomous responses to low-level anomalies. The overseer monitors the core: the invariants that, if violated, would undermine the entire web. A peripheral belief (one drawn from a single paper, not yet integrated into causal models, carrying moderate confidence) can be repaired or retired quickly. A core belief (one central to the domain theory, highly integrated, cited by multiple downstream models) is protected by the quarantine protocol and multiple levels of scrutiny. This architecture reflects the mature epistemology of science: not all claims deserve equal defense; the field concentrates its efforts on protecting the concepts and relationships that are most foundational.

The operational modes and protocols discussed above were not designed by individual engineers in isolation but emerged from a formal panel review. In late 2025, Professor Kirsh convened an eighteen-person expert panel including epistemologists, formal methodologists, database specialists, and cognitive scientists. The panel spent three weeks examining the proposed operational layer, identifying weak points, proposing alternatives, and settling on the design principles that are documented in the overseer specification. This consultative process is a model for future major architectural decisions: acknowledge that complex systems require diverse expertise, bring those perspectives into dialogue, and let the design emerge from genuinely interdisciplinary scrutiny.

### §151.1 Subsystem Health Contracts

*Added 2026-03-04.*

The thirteen invariants above operate at the system level. Below that, each of the twenty subsystems in the ATLAS architecture maintains its own health contract specifying entry conditions (what must be true before the subsystem can operate), exit conditions (what must be true after the subsystem has run), and health invariants (conditions that must hold throughout operation). These contracts are documented in `contracts/SUBSYSTEM_HEALTH_CONTRACTS.md` (946 lines) and include twelve cross-boundary contracts (XB-1 through XB-12) that govern the interfaces between subsystems — template-registry consistency, framework referential integrity, circuit-archetype linkage, and so on.

The design decision to maintain subsystem contracts in a separate specification rather than embedding them in the overseer's invariant list reflects a separation of concerns: the overseer monitors global system health; subsystem contracts enforce local correctness. The overseer can query subsystem health status as part of its nightly cycle, but the contracts themselves are checked independently by the subsystems' own test suites (128 tests covering all cross-boundary contracts). This layered architecture means that a subsystem can be locally healthy while the system is globally unhealthy (or vice versa), and the diagnostic can distinguish between these cases.

## §152: Signal-Based Coordination — Distributed Epistemic Work

Article Eater is not a monolithic program running on a single machine in a single thread. It is a collection of independent agents—extraction workers, validators, hypothesis evaluators, overseer processes—that may run on different machines, on different schedules, and with different levels of urgency. Coordinating work across these agents without introducing race conditions, deadlock, or data corruption requires a mechanism that is simultaneously lightweight, human-readable, and robust to network partitions and process crashes.

The signal-based coordination system provides this mechanism through filesystem-based signaling. Each task—a discrete unit of work such as "extract beliefs from paper 457" or "validate all effect-size claims from batch 12"—is represented by a directory on a shared filesystem containing three signal files: CLAIMED, UPDATE, and COMPLETE. These files are JSON documents, human-readable, and edited atomically via filesystem operations (write to a temporary file, then rename). The approach exemplifies Unix philosophy: use simple, universal mechanisms; prefer text and filesystem over databases; trust the filesystem for its atomic operations.

The lease-based concurrency model operates as follows. When an agent becomes ready to work on a task, it checks whether the CLAIMED signal exists. If not, it writes a CLAIMED signal containing its agent ID, a timestamp, and a lease duration (typically 30 minutes). It then begins work. If the agent is still working when the lease approaches expiration, it writes an UPDATE signal, refreshing the timestamp. When the work is complete, it writes a COMPLETE signal containing the outcome (SUCCESS, PARTIAL, FAILED) and relevant metadata. A monitoring process, running continuously, scans the signal directories and identifies stale claims—those where the CLAIMED timestamp is older than the lease duration and no UPDATE has been written. Such tasks are marked as reclaimable, and another agent can claim them.

This simple model has emerged from bitter experience with more sophisticated approaches. Distributed locking with a database backend introduces complexity and failure modes: if the database becomes unavailable, even briefly, agents may hang indefinitely. Consensus protocols (like Paxos or Raft) are overkill for this application and introduce latency. Filesystem-based locking, by contrast, is simple, works offline, and is well-understood across Unix systems. The lease mechanism handles the case where an agent crashes without releasing the lock; the stale claim is automatically reclaimed after the lease expires.

Currently, Article Eater maintains 166 signal triplets, one for each distinct work unit across the scheduled pipeline, validation pipeline, and hypothesis evaluation pipeline. This number grows as new work units are added (e.g., new paper batches for extraction, new validator modules) and shrinks as completed tasks are archived. The signal files are organized in a directory hierarchy mirroring the work structure: signals/extraction/batch_*, signals/validation/field_validator/*, signals/hypothesis_eval/*, etc.

The design rationale for signal-based coordination flows from several principles. First, *minimization of coordination overhead*. Each signal operation (write and rename) is a filesystem operation, measured in milliseconds. Contrast this with database-based approaches, which require establishing connections, executing transactions, and potentially waiting for locks. When an agent needs to claim a task, it performs a single filesystem operation and is ready to begin work. Second, *human readability and debuggability*. An operator can examine the signal files directly, understand the current state of work, and manually intervene if necessary (e.g., claiming a task on behalf of a crashed agent). A database-based system requires access to the database to inspect state. Third, *resilience to network partitions*. If a machine running an agent is disconnected from the network, it can continue working on its current task; when the network is restored, it updates the signals and synchronizes its work. With a centralized database, a disconnected agent must wait for network restoration before checking out a task.

This approach is, in some sense, a direct instantiation of Herbert Simon's organizational principles from *The Sciences of the Artificial*. Simon argues that organizations are coordination problems; the goal of organizational design is to minimize the information that needs to be transmitted and processed to coordinate action. Signal-based coordination achieves this by pushing most of the coordination burden onto the filesystem (a simple, universal mechanism) rather than requiring agents to negotiate with a central coordinator. Each agent can proceed autonomously; the signals merely record the current state, allowing other agents to reason about what work remains to be done.

## §153: QA Validators — The Epistemic Immune System

Validation in Article Eater is not a single gate but a stratified series of checks, each operating at a different level of detail and applying different standards. The validation pipeline resembles an immune system: multiple independent mechanisms, each specialized to detect particular classes of threats, all operating in concert.

The *extraction_field_validator* is the frontmost defense. It examines each extracted field (title, direction_of_effect, effect_size, confidence, p_value, etc.) against a set of domain-specific rules. The validator maintains a rule registry in extraction_quality_rules.json, currently containing 50+ rules organized by field. For example, rules for the direction_of_effect field include: (1) value must be one of ["increase", "decrease", "bidirectional", "unknown"]; (2) if direction is "unknown", confidence must be ≤ 0.5; (3) if effect_size is present and direction is "decrease", effect_size must be logically consistent with "decrease" semantics. Each rule carries a severity weight (0–1) that affects the overall extraction quality score. The validator produces a score for each field, ranging from 0 (complete failure) to 1 (perfect conformance), and a composite extraction_quality score that is the weighted average across all fields. Extractions with quality scores below a threshold (default 0.7) are flagged for human review. This validator directly instantiates success condition EFV-SC1 (all direction fields contain only standardized values).

The *effect_size_validator* takes a more sophisticated approach, accounting for the semantic structure of effect sizes. Different measures (odds ratios, correlation coefficients, standardized mean differences) have different natural ranges and different interpretations. An odds ratio of 10 is plausible; a correlation coefficient of 10 is impossible. The validator is measure-type-aware: when it encounters an effect size, it first checks the measure type (recorded in the extraction metadata), then applies type-specific range checks. It also performs outlier detection using robust statistics (median absolute deviation) to identify values that, while technically valid (within the possible range for their measure type), are unusually extreme and warrant scrutiny. Additionally, it checks for p-value contamination: if an effect size and a p-value are both reported, it can roughly estimate what the p-value should be given the effect size and sample size; if the actual p-value differs substantially, this suggests either a data entry error or that the effect size and p-value are from different analyses and should not be integrated as a unit.

The *confounder_risk_checker* implements domain-specific knowledge about which variables, if not properly controlled, can confound causal inferences. The checker maintains a known-confounder registry with approximately 50 entries, organized by domain (e.g., "when studying effects on health outcomes, age is a known confounder"; "when studying organizational changes, prior performance is a known confounder"). For each extracted causal claim, the checker scans the source document to determine whether the study design controlled for relevant confounders. If a source claims a causal effect (say, "organization restructuring increases employee satisfaction") but the study did not control for selection effects (employees selecting into the new structure may differ in baseline satisfaction), the checker flags the claim as at elevated risk of confounding. The risk is weighted based on the severity of the potential confounder: a missing control for age might be weighted as 0.3 (moderate concern) while a missing control for selection effects might be weighted as 0.8 (severe concern). The final confounder risk score is the maximum risk across all identified confounders, providing a conservative (high) estimate of the threat to validity.

The *credence_intervals* module addresses the propagation of uncertainty from the source through the extraction to the credence assignment. When a source reports an effect size with a 95% confidence interval, and we extract that effect size and map it to a causal belief, what is the appropriate credence interval for the belief? The mapping is not straightforward: the source's confidence interval reflects sampling variability; our credence should reflect both sampling variability and model uncertainty. The module employs the delta method, a standard technique from statistics, to propagate the source's reported uncertainty through the logit transformation that maps effect sizes to credence. The result is a credence interval, say [0.62, 0.78], that reflects the uncertainty in the original estimate while accounting for the measurement and extraction process. This interval is recorded alongside the point credence and serves as a check on reasonableness: if the interval is implausibly wide, it signals that the source's estimate was highly uncertain, and the belief should be held with lower confidence.

These validators compose into a coherent pipeline. A claim enters at the extraction_field_validator, which checks field-level conformance. If it passes, it proceeds to the effect_size_validator, which checks the semantic validity and consistency of reported statistics. Then the confounder_risk_checker assesses the threat to validity given the study design. Finally, credence_intervals computes the appropriate uncertainty bounds. At each stage, a composite quality score is updated. The final score, derived from all validators, determines whether the claim is integrated immediately, queued for human review, or rejected.

This validation architecture embodies Deborah Mayo's philosophy of *severe testing*. Mayo argues that statistical evidence is strong only insofar as a test is severe—that is, only if the hypothesis could easily have been falsified but was not. Analogously, Article Eater's validators ask: what would falsify this extraction? What patterns in the data or the metadata would indicate that the extraction is unreliable? By defining these falsifying conditions in advance and checking for them explicitly, the system implements a kind of epistemic discipline that mirrors Mayo's philosophy. An extraction that passes all validators has, in effect, passed a severe test: it has survived multiple independent checks, each of which could easily have rejected it.

The extraction_quality_rules.json deserves special mention. This file is a machine-readable codification of methodological standards that human reviewers apply implicitly when assessing the quality of empirical claims. By encoding these standards as computable rules, Article Eater makes them explicit, auditable, and consistent. A human reviewer might apply some rules stringently in one assessment and loosely in another, depending on mood or fatigue; the machine validator applies them uniformly. Furthermore, the rules serve as documentation: they communicate to anyone reading the code what standards Article Eater maintains for research quality. This is epistemically important: standards for evidence should be public and defensible, not buried in informal community norms.

## §154: Architectural Lessons and Cross-Cutting Concerns

The five operational systems examined in this part—reflexes, success conditions, the overseer, signal-based coordination, and QA validators—are not independent components cobbled together ad hoc. They form a coherent architecture grounded in deep principles about how knowledge systems should govern themselves.

The detect-fix-report pattern, exemplified by reflexes, is universally applicable to epistemic systems. A system observes its own operation, detects anomalies against known patterns, applies a response (either automatic correction or escalation), and records the outcome for inspection and trend analysis. This pattern instantiates Peirce's self-correcting method at a computational level. It is applicable far beyond Article Eater: any system processing data streams and making probabilistic judgments would benefit from reflex-like mechanisms.

Success conditions establish a contract hierarchy. At the lowest level, individual modules (field validators, effect-size checkers) must satisfy their success conditions. At a higher level, the system as a whole must satisfy global invariants checked by the overseer. The hierarchy creates multiple opportunities for error detection at different levels of abstraction, reflecting Simon's principle that systems should maintain redundant safeguards rather than relying on a single verification gate.

The overseer itself embodies Quine's web of belief at the system level. Just as Quine argued that no single belief is immune to revision, but that the core of the web (the logical laws, the fundamental empirical claims) is protected by its centrality, so too does the overseer protect Article Eater's core invariants while allowing peripheral beliefs to be questioned and revised. This architecture is distinctly anti-foundationalist: there is no absolutely secure base layer. Instead, there is a structure of mutual support, with some claims more strongly defended because they are more central.

Separation of concerns, a principle articulated by Parnas and developed into an architectural discipline by subsequent systems architects, runs through every design choice. The overseer.db is separate from web.db; reflexes do not directly modify the web but only record observations; validators produce scores and recommendations but do not make integration decisions. This separation serves multiple purposes: it allows each component to be understood independently; it enables components to be replaced or upgraded without redesigning the entire system; it prevents cascading failures where a bug in one layer corrupts the whole system; and it creates multiple levels of auditing, as an independent component can verify that upstream components are behaving correctly.

The human-in-the-loop principle, evident in the quarantine protocol and in the explicit escalation of high-confidence decisions to human reviewers, reflects a mature epistemology. The system does not assume that it can achieve perfect automation; instead, it automates the detection and preliminary diagnosis of problems, while preserving human judgment for decisions that are consequential, irreversible, or scientifically novel. This division of labor is precisely what Simon advocated: the system provides decision support, presenting options and recommendations, but the human retains authority over final judgments.

Finally, the operational layer is designed to support future evolution and integration with other systems. The success conditions framework, for instance, is extensible: new conditions can be added as new modules are developed. The QA answer enrichment orchestrator, discussed briefly in Part XL, can trigger reflexes when answer quality drops below acceptable thresholds, creating feedback loops that tighten the coupling between answer generation and system health. The signal-based coordination system can be extended to manage work across multiple Article Eater instances or across heterogeneous validation pipelines. The system is built on Unix principles: small, specialized tools that do one thing well; text-based communication; and modularity that enables composition.

## §154.1: Database Health Monitoring — The Canonical Path Problem

A subtle but pervasive failure mode in distributed epistemic systems is *path confusion*: different components addressing different databases, or the same database at different paths, leading to silent divergence between what the system believes and what actually exists. This problem manifests when, for instance, a health monitoring service queries an empty database at `./ae.db` while the actual belief data resides at `./data/web_persistence.db`. The symptoms are insidious: metrics return zero, invariants appear violated, and the system reports failure even though the underlying data is healthy.

The `check_db_health.py` script, created March 2026, addresses this problem through ten explicit success conditions (SC-DB-1 through SC-DB-10) that collectively verify database infrastructure integrity before any epistemic operations begin. The design reflects a principle that might be called *canonical path primacy*: there must be exactly one authoritative mechanism for locating the database, and all components must use it.

**SC-DB-1: db_locator returns existing file.** The `db_locator` service (`src/services/db_locator.py`) is the canonical API for database path resolution. This success condition verifies that the service is importable, functional, and returns an existing file path. Failure here indicates fundamental infrastructure breakdown.

**SC-DB-2: beliefs table exists.** The core data structure is the `beliefs` table. This condition catches migration failures where code assumes a table that was never created, or where schema evolution left incompatible table names (e.g., `belief_versions` vs `beliefs`).

**SC-DB-3: beliefs table has data.** An empty beliefs table indicates either initialization failure or path confusion (querying the wrong database). With 4,888+ beliefs in the production corpus, zero rows is always an error.

**SC-DB-4: Required columns exist.** Schema drift is a common failure mode when multiple developers modify table structures. This condition verifies that essential columns (`belief_id`, `content`, `web_id`, `credence_value`, etc.) exist, and warns about missing recommended columns (`template_ids`, `epistemic_v2`).

**SC-DB-5: No shadowing databases.** A particularly insidious bug occurs when a database file exists at both the project root (`./ae.db`) and the canonical location (`./data/web_persistence.db`). Code that searches for databases in sequence may find the wrong one first. This condition detects such shadowing and alerts operators.

**SC-DB-6: Overseer uses db_locator.** The overseer must use the same path resolution as all other components. Historically, the overseer accepted `web_db_path` as a constructor argument without defaulting to the canonical locator, enabling callers to pass inconsistent paths. This condition verifies that the overseer imports and uses `db_locator`.

**SC-DB-7: template_ids coverage.** Template linkage (belief → template mapping) is required for AESHI scoring. This condition monitors coverage percentage and alerts when it falls below threshold (default 10%).

**SC-DB-8: Migration 023 applied.** Database migrations must be applied in order. Migration 023 creates the `overseer_health_metrics` table required for health monitoring. This condition verifies that the migration was applied.

**SC-DB-9: Foreign key integrity.** Beliefs reference `web_metadata` via `web_id`. Orphaned beliefs (pointing to nonexistent web records) indicate data corruption or incomplete migrations.

**SC-DB-10: Theory orphan rate.** Beliefs should be linked to theoretical frameworks via `theory_id`. A high orphan rate (currently thresholded at 60%) indicates extraction pipeline failures or missing theory assignment logic.

The script supports three modes: interactive (human-readable report), JSON (for programmatic consumption by the overseer), and quick (essential checks only for rapid pre-flight verification). Integration with the overseer nightly pipeline is recommended as Stage 0, running before any other health checks to ensure that subsequent checks query the correct database.

The architectural lesson is that *infrastructure verification must precede epistemic verification*. A system cannot meaningfully assess whether its beliefs are coherent if it cannot first verify that it is examining the right beliefs. The database health check implements this principle as executable code.
