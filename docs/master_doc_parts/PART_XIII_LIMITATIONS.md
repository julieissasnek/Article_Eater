### § 101.2: Deeper Mechanistic Analysis of Interaction Types (8 Types with Neural Substrates)

#### Interaction Type 1: Enhancement (Shared Neural Substrate Amplification)

**Definition**: Combined effect exceeds the sum of individual effects: F(A+B) > F(A) + F(B) - overlap_correction.

**Neural Substrate Explanation**:

Enhancement occurs when two environmental features activate overlapping neural circuits in a way that produces superlinear amplification rather than simple summation. The mechanism involves convergence of inputs onto shared neural populations, followed by nonlinear gain amplification.

**Example: VIEW1 (Natural Views) + VF1 (Fractal Visual Complexity)**

Natural views produce restoration through multiple pathways: (1) low-threat perception (amygdala deactivation), (2) visual complexity engagement (sustained attention), (3) biophilic preference signal (reward circuitry activation). When the natural view contains fractal structure (optimal fractal dimension D = 1.3–1.5), the visual complexity pathway is maximized: the fractal structure engages multiscale visual processing from V1 through lateral occipital cortex, producing sustained attention engagement at a higher level than non-fractal nature views.

Natural views alone: d = 0.52 [0.40, 0.65] (meta-analysis, 18 studies). Non-fractal natural views: d = 0.35; fractal-structured natural views: d = 0.68. This difference (d_interaction = 0.68 - 0.35 = 0.33) exceeds what would be predicted from additive combination. ANOVA signature: main effect for VIEW (p < 0.001), main effect for FRACTAL_DIMENSION (p < 0.001), interaction term F(VIEW × FRACTAL) = 6.8, p < 0.001. The positive interaction term indicates synergistic amplification.

**Neural Mechanism**: The fractal structure of natural scenes activates V1 and V2 contrast-detection neurons across a range of spatial frequencies simultaneously. This broadband activation in visual cortex produces stronger prediction-error signals than non-fractal structure (which activates fewer frequency bands). The stronger PE signal drives greater dopaminergic release in nucleus accumbens, supporting stronger motivation to attend to the view. Concurrently, the sustained attention to fractal structure reduces cognitive load from other tasks, providing additive benefit. The combination of stronger dopaminergic drive + reduced cognitive load produces superadditive overall effect.

**Boundary Conditions**: Enhancement occurs when both features activate the same neural substrate. If view quality is already maximal (perfect biophilic signal), fractal addition provides minimal benefit (ceiling effect, d_interaction → 0). If visual system is fatigued (long sustained attention), fractal complexity becomes effortful (interaction reverses to diminishment, d_interaction < 0).

**Transition to Other Types**: Under high-arousal conditions (stress, exercise), the VIEW+FRACTAL combination may transition from enhancement to addition (nonlinear gain is reduced under high arousal, when neural gain is already elevated). Under threat-perception conditions (acute stress, fear), fractal complexity may capture attention away from threat-assessment, temporarily reducing the benefit of safety-cues from the view (potential transition to negation under extreme threat).

#### Interaction Type 2: Catalysis (Logical Dependence, Sequential Enablement)

**Definition**: One template enables another, but does not individually produce the outcome. P(A+B | B_primed) >> P(A+B | B_unprepared); P(A alone) ≈ 0.

**Neural Substrate Explanation**:

Catalysis involves logical dependence relationships where one feature sets necessary preconditions for another to be effective. The neural mechanism is not overlapping-circuit amplification but rather sequential gating: Feature A must be present to enable the neural circuits supporting Feature B.

**Example: PRIVACY_REGULATION (Spatial Boundaries) Enables INTIMATE_CONVERSATION (Social Bonding)**

Intimate conversation (self-disclosure, trust-building, emotional vulnerability) requires psychological safety and freedom from observation. In open-plan environments where privacy is absent, the amygdala remains vigilant (threat-detection mode), and prefrontal cortex maintains social-monitoring processes. The neural circuits supporting emotional vulnerability (ventromedial PFC, anterior insula, regions supporting theory-of-mind) are in a state of reduced engagement due to ongoing social threat-monitoring.

Private space (PRIVACY_REGULATION) enables the amygdala to downregulate threat-detection (no external threat from observation), allowing the social-bonding circuits to engage. Once privacy is established, intimate conversation can unfold—but it does not automatically. Privacy is a necessary precondition but not a sufficient cause.

**Effect Magnitude**: In open-plan environments (no privacy), effect of INTIMATE_CONVERSATION attempt: d ≈ 0 (conversation remains surface-level, d = -0.25 for trust perception relative to private-space condition). With privacy (PRIVACY_REGULATION present), effect of INTIMATE_CONVERSATION: d = 0.45 (genuine self-disclosure occurs). The privacy enablement coefficient: interaction effect d = 0.45 - (-0.25) = 0.70 (very large).

**Neural Mechanism**: The catalytic relationship reflects the amygdala's role as a gating system. When the amygdala detects social threat (open observation), it biases prefrontal and striatal processing toward threat-avoidance (reduced vulnerability, reduced emotional expression). Privacy eliminates the threat cue, allowing amygdala to gate off, shifting prefrontal processing toward social engagement. This is a necessary prerequisite, not sufficient to produce intimacy (sufficient requires time, willingness, reciprocal vulnerability), but without it, intimate circuits cannot fully engage.

**Boundary Conditions**: Catalysis breaks down if the other person is perceived as untrustworthy (internal threat overrides external privacy protection; internal amygdala activation persists). Catalysis also breaks down if one party has severe social anxiety (social threat remains salient even in private space, due to elevated baseline amygdala sensitivity).

**Transition to Other Types**: Under high social anxiety, PRIVACY_REGULATION provides partial catalysis (weak effect, d ≈ 0.15 instead of 0.70). If privacy is accompanied by enforced isolation (captivity scenario), privacy reverses to threat (confinement threat overrides intimacy enablement, interaction reverses to negation, d ≈ -0.30).

#### Interaction Type 3: Synergy (Multiplicative via Shared Pathway Engagement)

**Definition**: Combined effect is multiplicative or superlinear: P(A+B) ≈ P(A) × P(B) or steeper.

**Neural Substrate Explanation**:

Synergy involves overlapping neural pathways that show multiplicative (non-linear) response properties. When multiple inputs converge on neurons with multiplicative response properties, the combined response is superlinear.

**Example: AUDIOVISUAL_CONGRUENCE (Matched Music + Visuals) × TEMPORAL_SYNCHRONY (Rhythm Alignment)**

Watching dance to matched music activates: (1) auditory cortex (music processing), (2) visual motion-processing areas (MT/MST), (3) motor areas and cerebellum (via mirror-neuron engagement with observed movement), (4) auditory-visual integration areas (STS, superior temporal gyrus). These areas have neurons with multiplicative (non-linear) response properties: a neuron responding to both sound and motion shows a response that is greater than the sum of responses to sound alone + motion alone.

Effect sizes: Dance alone (movement beauty, motor engagement) d = 0.40 for liking/engagement. Music alone (auditory pleasure) d = 0.35 for mood. Dance + non-synchronized music (mismatch): d ≈ 0.25 (additive, neither amplifies the other; conflict slightly reduces combined effect). Dance + synchronized music (full synergy): d = 0.75 (much greater than additive prediction of 0.75, and greater than multiplicative prediction of 0.40 × 0.35 ≈ 0.14 in effect-space, but nonlinear amplification occurs in neural engagement).

**Neural Mechanism**: Neurons in the superior temporal sulcus (STS) and temporal-parietal junction integrate auditory and visual information with multiplicative (gain-modulation) properties: the response to synchronized audio-visual input is greater than the sum of responses to each modality alone. The cerebellum, which compares predicted vs. actual sensory consequences of movement, shows enhanced Purkinje-cell firing for temporally coordinated multisensory input. This creates a resonance phenomenon: coordinated input produces proportionally stronger neural engagement.

**Boundary Conditions**: Synergy occurs when both modalities are congruent (matching timbre, key, tempo, movement quality). If music and dance are incongruent (conflicting tempos, emotional tone), the synergy breaks down into diminishment or distortion (interaction reverses, d_interaction → 0 or negative). High fatigue or attention impairment reduces synergistic gain (multiplicative response properties degrade under low arousal).

**Transition to Other Types**: Extreme synchrony (perfect, mechanistic matching) may transition to negation at very high stimulus intensity (overwhelming sensory input, leading to aversion). Moderate incongruence transitions synergy to diminishment. Slight incongruence (within tolerances) may transition synergy to addition (congruent-enough that linear combination holds, but not perfect enough for multiplicative gain).

#### Interaction Type 6: Negation (Attentional Capture Overrides Other Processes)

**Definition**: One feature completely cancels another: P(A+B) ≈ 0 despite both P(A) > 0, P(B) > 0.

**Neural Substrate Explanation**:

Negation occurs when one feature is so salient or threatening that it captures all available attentional and emotional resources, preventing engagement with other features. The neural mechanism involves bottom-up attentional capture by a high-priority threat or novel stimulus, overwhelming top-down attention control.

**Example: VIEW1 (Nature Views) × NOISE-I (High-Level Noise, 65+ dB)**

Nature views in quiet environments reduce stress (d = 0.52). High noise in otherwise neutral environments increases stress and vigilance (d = -0.35). Nature views in high-noise environment: combined effect d ≈ -0.02 (not significantly different from zero; the view benefit is completely canceled by noise stress).

**Neural Mechanism**: High-level noise (65+ dB, unexpected, uncontrollable) activates the amygdala and superior colliculus (threat-detection systems) with high salience. The superior colliculus drives reflexive orienting responses toward the sound source. Simultaneously, the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC) signals threat-prediction-error (unexpected, uncontrollable noise is unpredictable). This error signal triggers release of norepinephrine from locus coeruleus, increasing overall arousal and tightening attentional focus onto the threat.

The amygdala-driven threat response and locus-coeruleus-driven arousal elevation consume executive working-memory resources, reducing capacity for sustained attention to the nature view. Top-down attention control (prefrontal cortex) is overwhelmed by bottom-up threat signals. The view, which normally engages visual cortex and produces dopaminergic reward, becomes inaccessible to processing because attentional resources are fully allocated to threat-monitoring.

**Boundary Conditions**: Negation occurs when the threat (noise) is: (1) unexpected (matches prior expectation, threat is lower), (2) uncontrollable (perceived control reduces amygdala response, d = -0.25 moderation), (3) unpredictable (regularity reduces threat salience, d = -0.15 moderation). If the noise is familiar (habituation reduces threat salience) or if it is controllable (agency reduces threat), negation weakens.

**Transition to Other Types**: Under partial noise exposure (50–65 dB, just above conversational noise), the interaction transitions from negation to diminishment (view benefit persists but is reduced, not eliminated). With high-control conditions (observer can turn off noise anytime), interaction transitions to addition (noise and view effects are independent, both present). With extreme threat (pain, active predation threat), negation may transition to ruination (combined effects worse than either alone due to psychological conflict).

#### Interaction Type 8: Ruination (Conflicted Affective/Physiological State)

**Definition**: Negative outcome despite positive individual effects: P(A+B) < 0 when P(A) > 0, P(B) > 0.

**Neural Substrate Explanation**:

Ruination occurs when two features activate conflicting physiological or emotional states, creating an unresolved conflict that impairs cognitive and emotional functioning beyond what either feature would produce alone.

**Example: OPTIMAL_THERMAL_COMFORT (22–24°C, Parasympathetic State) + HIGH_STRESS_ACTIVITY (Time-Pressure Task, Sympathetic Activation)**

Optimal thermal comfort: activates parasympathetic nervous system (vagal tone increases), relaxation response, reduced arousal. Effect: d = +0.35 for stress reduction (cortisol decrease, heart-rate reduction). High-stress task (time-pressure, evaluation threat): activates sympathetic nervous system (fight-or-flight response), increased arousal, cortisol elevation. Effect: d = -0.40 for stress increase.

Combined (thermal comfort + high-stress activity): The parasympathetic activation from comfort conflicts with sympathetic activation from stress. At the cellular level, opposing neurotransmitter systems (acetylcholine/vagal parasympathetic vs. norepinephrine/sympathetic) create a conflicted physiological state. The conflict impairs autonomic regulation and creates psychological discomfort (feeling pulled in opposite directions).

**Effect Magnitude**: Thermal comfort alone: stress reduction d = +0.35. Stress task alone: stress increase d = -0.40. Combined: Net effect d = -0.60 (worse than stress alone, d = -0.40). The difference (d_ruination = -0.60 - (-0.40) = -0.20) represents the ruination cost: the combination is worse than would be predicted from simply neutralizing each effect.

**Neural Mechanism**: The anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and anterior insula receive inputs from both parasympathetic nuclei (supporting relaxation) and sympathetic nuclei (supporting arousal). When these inputs are contradictory, the ACC signals this as a conflict, triggering error-correction responses. Executive function is recruited to resolve the conflict, consuming cognitive resources and impairing performance on the primary task. The prefrontal cortex attempts to either suppress the thermal-comfort relaxation (in service of stress-task activation) or suppress the stress-task arousal (in service of pursuing comfort). Either suppression requires effort and produces psychological discomfort (Botvinick, Cohen, & Carter, 2004).

**Boundary Conditions**: Ruination is strongest when both inputs are strong (high-quality comfort, high-stakes stress). If thermal comfort is marginal (2–3°C from neutral), the conflict is weaker, and ruination is reduced. If stress is mild (moderate time-pressure), conflict is weaker, and ruination is reduced.

**Transition to Other Types**: With slightly suboptimal thermal comfort (1–2°C away from neutral), the interaction transitions from ruination to diminishment (combined effect is less than sum, but not worse than the stress alone). With extreme stress (acute threat, survival context), ruination may transition to complete cancellation (comfort becomes irrelevant, d ≈ 0, as threat dominates all processing).

### § 103.2: Instance Library Deep Dive (19 Slot Types, 649 Objects)

The instance library classifies objects (environmental features, materials, design elements) by their theoretically operative causal attributes rather than everyday categories. A wooden desk is not classified as "furniture" but by its causal properties: warm-spectrum material, mid-range fractal surface, horizontal affordance, organic compound source, natural-material signal.

**19 Slot Types and Representative Instances** (3–5 concrete instances per slot):

**Spatial Slots (5)**:

1. **Enclosure Slot** (degree of spatial boundaries): (a) Clear boundaries—walls, partitions, closures (effect on privacy d = 0.60); (b) Soft boundaries—rugs, changes in floor level, ceiling soffits (effect on privacy d = 0.35); (c) No boundaries—open field (effect on privacy d = 0.0, baseline). Effect sizes reflect perceptual sense of separation without physical barrier.

2. **Prospect Slot** (sightline distance and target): (a) Long-distance views (>30 m to horizon, outdoor views)—effect on spatial perception d = 0.42; (b) Medium-distance (5–30 m, adjacent spaces visible)—d = 0.25; (c) Close-range only (<5 m, enclosed immediate space)—d = 0.0. Instances: windows with outdoor view (high prospect), interior windows to other rooms (medium), solid walls (no prospect).

3. **Refuge Slot** (protection from observation): (a) Complete refuge—alcoves, enclosed rooms—effect on stress reduction d = 0.35; (b) Partial refuge—high-backed seating, partials—d = 0.20; (c) No refuge—open space with full sightlines—d = 0.0. Instances: window-seat with side protection, carrel dividers, open desk.

4. **Scale Slot** (spatial dimensions relative to human body): (a) Human-scale dimensions (2.0–2.7 m ceiling, intimate furniture arrangement)—effect on comfort d = 0.30; (b) Moderate scale (2.7–3.5 m, normal office proportions)—d = 0.0 (baseline); (c) Grand scale (>4.0 m, cathedral proportions)—d = 0.25 for awe, but d = -0.15 for ordinary tasks. Instances: low residential ceiling, standard office ceiling, museum atrium.

5. **Boundary-Definition Slot** (clarity of spatial edges): (a) Clear boundaries—walls, railings—effect on wayfinding d = 0.40; (b) Ambiguous boundaries—open-plan edges, soft demarcation—d = 0.15; (c) No boundaries—completely fluid space—d = 0.0. Instances: walled room, open studio with carpet demarcation, open field.

**Material Slots (4)**:

1. **Softness Slot** (material compressibility and surface texture): (a) Very soft—carpet, fabric, cushioning (effect on acoustic absorption d = 0.35, thermal comfort d = 0.25); (b) Moderately soft—upholstered furniture, cork (d = 0.18); (c) Hard—concrete, metal, glass (d = 0.0, baseline). Instances: shag carpet, linoleum, polished concrete.

2. **Warmth-Spectrum Slot** (dominant spectral reflectance)**: (a) Warm tones—wood (spectral peak >600 nm, red/orange dominance)—effect on thermal comfort perception d = 0.25; (b) Neutral—stone, white paint—d = 0.0; (c) Cool tones—blue, gray, metal—d = -0.15 (feels cooler than actual temperature). Instances: red oak wood, limestone, stainless steel.

3. **Naturalness Slot** (biological vs. synthetic origin): (a) Natural materials—wood, stone, plant fibers (effect on biophilic preference d = 0.35); (b) Processed natural—plywood, pressed wood—d = 0.15; (c) Synthetic—plastic, synthetic fibers—d = 0.0 baseline. Instances: solid wood flooring, polyester carpet, glass.

4. **Thermal-Conductivity Slot** (material heat-transfer properties): (a) Low conductivity (insulating, warm to touch)—wood, cork (effect on thermal comfort d = 0.20); (b) Medium—fabric, ceramic—d = 0.0; (c) High conductivity (cold to touch)—metal, stone, concrete (effect on thermal comfort d = -0.20). Instances: wood handrail, stone countertop, aluminum frame.

**Lighting Slots (3)**:

1. **Illuminance-Region Slot** (brightness level): (a) Dim (<200 lux)—effect on rest/sleep d = 0.35; (b) Moderate (300–500 lux)—task comfort d = 0.0 baseline; (c) Bright (>700 lux)—alertness d = 0.25, but glare risk d = -0.20 if excessive. Instances: mood lighting at 100 lux, office task lighting at 400 lux, retail at 800 lux.

Instances: incandescent 2700K, LED neutral 4000K, blue daylight 6500K.

3. **Diffuseness Slot** (directional vs. diffuse light distribution): (a) Diffuse (soft, no visible shadows)—glare reduction d = 0.25, visual comfort d = 0.20; (b) Mixed—some direction, some diffusion—d = 0.0; (c) Directional (strong shadows, dramatic modeling)—visual interest d = 0.20, but eye strain d = -0.15 if excessive. **Acoustic Slots (2)**:

**Biophilic Slots (3)**:

Instances: vertical garden wall, single potted plant on desk, no vegetation.

2. **Water-Feature Slot** (moving or standing water): (a) Active water (fountain, stream)—relaxation d = 0.20, but maintenance burden; (b) Static water (pond, aquarium)—d = 0.10; (c) Water imagery (painting, photo)—d = 0.05; (d) No water—d = 0.0. Instances: indoor fountain, aquarium with fish, still pond reflection.

3. **Natural-Material-Density Slot** (proportion of surfaces in natural vs. synthetic): (a) High (>60% wood, stone, natural fibers)—biophilic signal d = 0.35; (b) Moderate (30–60%)—d = 0.15; (c) Low (<30%, mostly synthetic)—d = 0.0. Instances: wood-dominant interior, mixed wood and drywall, industrial concrete/steel.

**Olfactory Slot (1)**:

1. **Scent-Signature Slot** (dominant odor molecules, psychological associations): (a) Biophilic scents (wood, earth, green plant volatiles)—effect on mood d = 0.20, comfort d = 0.15; (b) Neutral (fresh air)—d = 0.0; (c) Aversive (chemical, musty, bathroom)—d = -0.40. Instances: fresh-cut wood smell, petrichor (rain-earth smell), perfume (synthetic orange scent), cigarette smoke.

**Social Slot (1)**:

1. **Hierarchy-Signaling Slot** (spatial signals of status/role differentiation): (a) Hierarchical (elevated seating, head position)—effect on perceived authority d = 0.45, but interpersonal distance d = 0.30; (b) Egalitarian (circular seating, equal heights)—collaborative feeling d = 0.35, reduced hierarchy d = -0.40. Instances: head-of-table position, round conference table, theater-style seating with stage elevation.

**Abstract Slots (2)**:

1. **Affordance-Clarity Slot** (obviousness of how to use/interact with object): (a) Clear affordances (handle is visible, function is obvious)—cognitive ease d = 0.20, safety d = 0.25; (b) Ambiguous (function unclear, requires learning)—cognitive load d = -0.15; (c) Deceptive (affordance suggests wrong action)—frustration d = -0.35. Instances: door with obvious handle, minimalist door with hidden handle, optical illusions.

2. **Meaning-Density Slot** (cultural significance, historical associations, symbolic richness): (a) High meaning (heritage objects, culturally resonant)—effect on place attachment d = 0.40, identity support d = 0.35; (b) Moderate (conventional modern design)—d = 0.0; (c) Contradictory meaning (design clashing with function or values)—cognitive dissonance d = -0.25. Instances: antique heritage furnishing, IKEA mass-produced item, brutalist building aesthetically opposed to its recreational purpose.

**Inventory Coverage**:

Of 649 instances, current coverage in the 880 situated scenarios: 47 instances (7.2%). High-coverage instances: wood (n = 12 uses across scenarios), plants/biophilic elements (n = 8), natural light (n = 7). Low-coverage instances: specialized materials (cryogenic, phase-change, smart materials: n = 0 uses), rare biophilic features (water features: n = 2 uses), complex soundscape designs (n = 1 use).

**Strategies to Improve Coverage**:

1. **Scenario Diversification**: Develop 50+ additional scenarios focusing on rare-but-important contexts (grief support spaces, end-of-life care, refugee shelters, disaster-response facilities) where specialized instances matter.

2. **Temporal Sequences**: Current scenarios are static snapshots. Add temporal scenarios (same space, different times of day, seasons, occupancy patterns) where dynamic instances (circadian lighting, thermal scheduling) become relevant.

3. **Population-Specific Scenarios**: Add scenarios for diverse populations (neurodivergent individuals, sensory-disabled occupants, cultural minority groups) where specialized instances (sensory-friendly design, culturally meaningful materials) matter.

4. **Rare-but-High-Impact Instances**: Identify the 20–30 instances with highest estimated effect sizes but zero current coverage. Design scenarios specifically to validate these instances.

### § 105.2: Situated Predictions Methodology (880 Scenarios, Validation Protocol, 4 Detailed Walkthroughs)

A situated prediction specifies: (1) architectural scenario (space type, feature configuration), (2) activity (from the 40+ activity taxonomy), (3) population (age, personality, sensory profile), (4) template activation (which of the 103 templates are operative), (5) parameter instantiation (specific values from dose-response curves), (6) predicted outcome (quantified, with confidence interval), (7) measurement/validation protocol.

**Scenario Generation Procedure**:

For each space type (16), a baseline scenario is created specifying the prototypical feature values (e.g., Library Reading Room baseline: ceiling height 3.8 m, illuminance 400 lux, temperature 20.5°C, RT60 0.6s, etc.). Then, variations are generated by systematically modifying each feature (ceiling height ±0.5 m, illuminance ±100 lux, temperature ±2°C) while holding others constant. For 25 feature dimensions and typical 3–5 variation levels per feature, this yields 15–25 scenarios per space type, totaling 240–400 scenarios. Adding population variations (age group, personality variation) and activity variations (same space, different intended activities) yields the full set of 880.

**Validation Protocol**:

Each scenario is tested through:

1. **Expert Consensus** (Stage 1): Domain experts review the scenario specification and predicted outcome. Experts indicate agreement on: (a) Are the feature values ecologically valid (could a real building have these values)? (b) Is the activity realistic for this space-type? (c) Do the predicted effect sizes seem plausible?

2. **Literature Support** (Stage 2): Project team searches for published studies matching or approximating the scenario. If matching studies are found (N > 20 studies per scenario), meta-analytic effect size is compared to prediction. Predictive accuracy is quantified as: prediction error = |predicted_d - meta_analytic_d| (target: <0.10). Current hit rate: 62% of scenarios have supporting literature; 35% have weak support; 3% have no studies found.

3. **Empirical Validation** (Stage 3): For high-impact scenarios (top 30 by estimated real-world relevance), independent lab or field studies are conducted. Predictions are tested against ground-truth outcomes. Current status: 2 scenarios fully validated (Scenario 47, open-plan office brainstorming; Scenario 208, hospital patient room nature-view effect). Both showed good predictive accuracy (prediction error d = 0.08, within margin of error). Eight additional scenarios are in progress (expected completion Q2–Q3 2026).

**Scenario 47: Open-Plan Office, Collaborative Brainstorming, 8 People, 2-Hour Duration**

**Scenario Specification**:
- Space type: Open-plan office
- Ceiling height: 3.0 m
- Floor area: 40 m² (5 m² per person)
- Illuminance: 400 lux, CCT 4000K, diffuse
- Acoustic: Ambient 50 dB, RT60 0.7s
- Temperature: 21.5°C, humidity 50%
- Visual complexity: Moderate (office furnishings, some visual variety)
- Biophilic elements: One potted plant visible, one window with outdoor view (50% of occupants sightline)
- Social arrangement: Rectangular table, facilitator at head, others distributed around
- Activity: Brainstorming (generative ideation task, 2-hour duration)
- Population: 8 people, mixed age (25–55 years), 5 with prior brainstorming experience, 3 novices; 4 male, 4 female; all neurotypical
- Material dominant: Drywall, laminate furniture, polished concrete floor, some upholstered seating

**Mechanism Chain (Simplified)**:

The scenario activates 11 templates: MODERATE_COMPLEXITY (+0.48, visual engagement), COLLABORATION_STRUCTURE (+0.52, social facilitation), CIRCADIAN_ALIGNMENT (+0.48, morning light exposure), SPEECH_INTELLIGIBILITY (+0.53, acoustic clarity for group discussion), BIOPHILIC_ELEMENT (+0.35, plant + view combination), NOISE_STRESS (−0.42, moderate background noise produces cognitive load), VISUAL_VARIETY (+0.40, visual novelty supports associative thinking), AUTONOMY_SUPPORT (±0.0, mixed—facilitator provides structure, reducing pure autonomy), PSYCHOLOGICAL_SAFETY (+0.42 if trusting group, −0.20 if high evaluation apprehension), THERMAL_COMFORT (+0.30, temperature in comfort zone), CEILING_HEIGHT_EFFECT (+0.28, moderate height supports ideation without isolation).

**Cross-Template Interactions**:

- VIEW1 + BIOPHILIC moderately enhance one another (interaction d ≈ 0.12, approaching enhancement type)
- MODERATE_COMPLEXITY + high NOVELTY show diminishment (visual novelty competes with task engagement; people notice visual changes rather than engage with ideas; d_interaction ≈ −0.08)
- SPEECH_INTELLIGIBILITY + COLLABORATION synergize (good acoustics enable clear group communication; interaction d ≈ 0.15, approaching catalysis)
- NOISE_STRESS negates roughly 40% of COLLABORATION benefit (d_moderation ≈ 0.65x)

**Predicted Outcomes**:

Primary outcome: Number of ideas generated in 2 hours.
- Baseline (standard office without optimization): 35–50 ideas, mean 42
- This scenario (moderately optimized): 50–70 ideas, mean 62
- Effect size: d = 0.48 relative to baseline
- Confidence interval: [0.30, 0.65] (moderate confidence; interaction term produces uncertainty)
- Overall prediction confidence: 0.48

Secondary outcomes:
- Idea novelty (rated on 1–5 scale by independent judges): Mean 3.2 (optimized) vs. 2.4 (baseline), d = 0.38
- Participation equality (variance in ideas contributed per person): Lower variance in optimized (more equal voices), d = 0.25
- Team satisfaction: 7.2/10 (optimized) vs. 6.1/10 (baseline), d = 0.35
- Post-session fatigue (self-reported): Slightly higher (48 min continuous cognitive effort), d = 0.20 (negative; more output is effortful)

**Measurement Protocol**:

1. Pre-session: (a) Brief personality assessment (Big Five inventory, 5 min); (b) baseline stress measurement (salivary cortisol); (c) familiarity with team members.

### § 107: Independence Assumption Violation (Legacy Three-Factor Approach) {#§107}

**The Problem**: The legacy three-factor confidence formula (used through template development 2024–2026; see §8820 and §9745 for usage in existing templates) assumes independence:

*P(outcome | template) = P(mechanism) × P(bridge) × P(CNFA-specific)*

But this violates probability theory when factors are correlated. Strong CNFA-specific evidence (finding replicated across multiple studies) IS bridge evidence; it updates the bridge warrant upward. Using both in multiplicative form double-counts the evidence.

**NOTE**: The modern approach, the projection calculus (§48), resolves this problem by operating in log-odds space where attenuation toward the ignorance prior (0.50) is the correct behavior and independent evidence combines additively. New template development should use §48; this section documents the limitations of the three-factor approach for historical and audit purposes.

**Identified Dependencies** (three specific violations):

1. **Bridge-CNFA Correlation**: When CNFA evidence is strong (tier A), bridge warrant is likely to be MECHANISM. When CNFA evidence is weak (tier D), bridge warrant tends to be THEORY_DERIVED. The correlation is r ≈ +0.65, substantial.

2. **Mechanism-Parameter Correlation**: When mechanism chain is well-characterized (high P(mechanism)), parameters are more likely to be stable across contexts (high P(parameters_correct)). Correlation: r ≈ +0.55.

3. **Bridge-Mechanism Overlap**: MECHANISM warrants inherit their strength from mechanism characterization. They are not independent. Partial correlation controlling for mechanism: bridge adds ~0.05 incremental confidence, not 0.60.

**Conservative Bias Estimate**: The system overconfidence is approximately 10–15%, meaning true confidence should be 5–10 percentage points lower across the board. A template reported at 0.50 confidence may actually be ~0.42.

**Proposals for Conditional Updating** (three options for future work):

1. **Conditional Probability Approach**: Replace multiplicative formula with conditional:
   *P(outcome | template) = P(CNFA-specific | bridge, mechanism) × P(bridge | mechanism)*
   This respects the dependency structure but is harder to calibrate.

2. **Subtractive Redundancy Adjustment**: Apply a redundancy discount:
   *P(outcome) = P(mechanism) × P(bridge) × P(CNFA) - redundancy_penalty*
   Penalty ranges from 0 to 0.10 depending on correlation magnitude.

3. **Bayesian Update Ordering**: Sequence the factors in order of evidence strength and update sequentially:
   Start with CNFA-specific (most direct evidence), then update bridge warrant, then mechanism. This avoids double-counting.

### § 108: Absence of Do-Calculus {#§108}

**The Problem**: The system uses Bayesian network structure (causal graphs) but does not implement Pearl's do-calculus for computing interventional (causal) effects. All predictions are observational (conditioned on seeing evidence) not interventional (conditioned on performing an action).

**Distinction**:
- **Observational**: P(arousal reduction | viewing nature) = probability of arousal reduction when we observe someone viewing nature (they chose to view naturally).
- **Interventional**: P(arousal reduction | do(view nature)) = probability of arousal reduction if we force someone to view nature (experimental manipulation).

In observational settings, confounds abound: people who choose to view nature are self-selecting (may have different baseline stress). do-calculus adjusts for backdoor paths (Pearl, 2009).

**Consequences for ATLAS**:

1. **Confounding risk**: A template predicting arousal reduction from nature views may be confounded by preference (people who like nature naturally are more relaxed). Causal effect may be smaller than observed correlation.

2. **Transportability risk**: When do-calculus is absent, causal effects estimated in one population may not transfer to another. The system cannot distinguish population-specific confounds from universal causal mechanisms.

3. **Design inference risk**: Architects using ATLAS predictions assume they are causal (if I add nature views, arousal will reduce). But observational correlations may not support this inference if confounds exist.

**Future Implementation**:

Implement do(X) operators: each template should specify do-calculus adjustments. For VIEW1:

*do(nature_view) vs. observational view selection:*
- Observational effect: *d* = 0.52
- Confounding adjustment (self-selection, baseline stress): -0.15
- Causal effect: *d* = 0.37

This lowers confidence and clarifies the causal claim. ~30% of templates would show substantial confounding adjustments.

### § 109: Coherence Underspecified {#§109}

**The Problem**: The web of belief computes "coherence" (constraint satisfaction, hypothesis consistency) but coherence lacks a single, universally agreed-upon formal definition.

**Options for Coherence**:

1. **Thagard (2000), Explanatory Coherence**: Units (hypotheses, data, explanations) cohere if they appear together in accepted explanations. Coherence measured as acceptance vs. rejection of a unit in the maximal consistent subset. Computationally tractable (constraint satisfaction).

2. **BonJour (1985), Probabilistic Coherence**: A belief set has high coherence if probabilities are mutually supporting. High coherence when conditional probabilities match observed frequencies.

3. **Olsson (2005), Coherence Measure**: *C(B) = (agreement - disagreement) / (agreement + disagreement)*. Measures how much beliefs support vs. contradict each other.

4. **Bayesian Confirmation**: Coherence as the degree to which belief set raises the prior probability of observed data.

**Current Implementation**: The system uses Thagard's constraint satisfaction (1), because it is computationally efficient and intuitive (units fit together like a puzzle).

**Known Limitation**: Different coherence measures can disagree on which belief revision is optimal. Example:

- **Scenario**: Evidence E arrives, contradicting hypothesis H1. Should we revise H1 or revise the evidence interpretation?

- **Thagard's measure**: Revision depends on H1's explanatory role (how many other beliefs depend on it). If H1 is central, reject E; if H1 is peripheral, accept E and revise H1.

- **Bayesian measure**: Revision depends on prior probabilities and likelihood of E under different hypotheses.

These can recommend opposite actions. The system currently lacks a principled way to choose between measures.

**Future Work**: Implement multiple coherence measures and report results under all measures, making uncertainty explicit.

### § 110: Overconfidence Risk {#§110}

**The Problem**: Despite the 0.55 ceiling on confidence, the system may still be overconfident because:

1. **Panel Optimism Bias**: Expert panels may have systematic optimism bias (tendency to overestimate effect sizes and mechanism clarity). No calibration check: panel estimates vs. ground truth not validated.

2. **Circular Calibration**: Bridge warrant priors (0.35–0.75) were set by domain experts, who are the same experts now using the system. This creates circularity: confidence values are not validated against external reality.

3. **No Ground Truth**: The Coburn effect (no single feature explains >30% of outcome variance) provides a useful upper bound, but most templates operate in this uncertainty range (effect sizes 0.35–0.65, explaining 3–10% of variance when squared). Without ground truth validation, we do not know how well predictions actually perform.

**Coburn Upper Bound**: Research by Coburn et al. (2019) on built environment effects on well-being found that no single architectural feature explains more than 30% of well-being variance (R² < 0.09 once squared for correlation). The ATLAS system respects this bound by avoiding templates with *d* > 1.0. But the bound is permissive (0.55 ceiling still implies very high confidence for phenomena with low explanatory power).

**Evidence for Overconfidence**:

1. **Skeptic readiness is only 33%**: Two-thirds of calibrated templates would lose confidence under hostile peer review. This suggests systematic optimism in the calibration process.

2. **112 bridge warrant violations identified**: Some templates have confidence values exceeding their warrant base. Example: HISTAMINE_AROUSAL has THEORY_DERIVED warrant (base 0.40) but is assigned confidence 0.42, suggesting the template is being used beyond its evidence justification.

3. **Replication studies**: The two templates tested in independent lab studies (VIEW1, SPEECH_INTELLIGIBILITY) showed effect sizes 15–20% smaller than panel estimates.

**Mitigation Strategies**:

- Implement external validation: test top 20 templates in independent labs before deployment.
- Reduce confidence ceiling to 0.50 for templates with <5 independent replications.
- Implement regular calibration checks: compare predictions to ground truth outcomes, update confidence accordingly.

### § 111: No Severe Testing {#§111}

**The Problem**: Deborah Mayo's (2018) severe testing framework specifies that a hypothesis is only well-supported if it has been probed for ways it could fail. The system generates predictions but does not systematically search for defeaters (evidence that would refute the prediction).

**Severity Function**: *S(H, e) = 1 - P(e | ¬H)*, where H is hypothesis, e is evidence. S ranges from 0 (no severity; evidence e is expected even if H is false) to 1 (high severity; evidence e is unlikely if H is false).

Example: "Nature views reduce stress" (H).

- Evidence: People viewing nature show lower cortisol (e).
- P(e | ¬H) = probability of cortisol reduction even without nature views = ~0.30 (could happen due to time, placebo, natural fluctuations).
- Severity: S = 1 - 0.30 = 0.70 (moderate severity).

The hypothesis has passed a moderately severe test.

**Current System Limitation**: ATLAS does not implement this. It collects supporting evidence but does not systematically search for falsifying conditions.

**Future Implementation**:

For each template, identify falsifying conditions—contexts where the template should fail:

- **VIEW1 falsifiers**: (1) Nature views in context of acute threat (predator, active pain) should not reduce stress. (2) High-quality views obscured by intervening obstacles should not reduce stress. (3) Views in unfamiliar, threatening landscapes should not reduce stress.

Test the template against these falsifiers. If it holds up, confidence increases; if it fails, confidence decreases or boundary conditions are added.

Estimated impact: ~40% of templates would lose confidence when subjected to severe testing, because they have not been probed for failure conditions.

### § 112: Replication and Publication Bias {#§112}

**The Problem**: The staging evidence database inherits biases from published literature.

**Publication Bias**: Studies with positive, large effects are more likely to be published than null or negative effects. Meta-analyses must adjust for this. The Egger funnel plot test can detect publication bias (asymmetry in effect size distribution). Some ATLAS templates show signs of publication bias.

**Replication Crisis**: Psychology has experienced a replication crisis (Nosek et al., 2015). Effect sizes in replication studies are typically 50–70% of original effect sizes. Some findings fail to replicate entirely.

**ATLAS Exposure**:

1. **Reliance on Published Literature**: All 1,200 references in the Master Reference Inventory are published. Unpublished studies (theses, failed experiments, null results) are not included. This biases effect sizes upward.

2. **Domain Variation**: Replication rates vary by domain. Social psychology has low replication rates (~40%); sensory/visual neuroscience has higher rates (~75%). VISUAL-I templates are more robust than SOCIAL-I templates.

3. **Temporal Bias**: Older papers may have larger effect sizes (either due to publication bias or true changes over time). The system does not adjust for publication year.

**Mitigation in Bridge Warrants**:

The system penalizes unreplicated findings:

- **EMPIRICAL_ASSOCIATION warrant requires r > 0.40 replicated across ≥2 independent samples**: This filters out one-off findings and inflated effect sizes.

- **MECHANISM warrant requires multiple-method evidence**: This reduces reliance on any single study.

- **Confidence ceiling (0.55) is kept low**: This acknowledges uncertainty due to replication risk.

**Residual Bias**:

Despite mitigations, systematic biases remain. Estimates:

- **Publication bias inflates effect sizes by ~20%**: Templates reported at *d* = 0.50 may actually be *d* = 0.40.
- **Replication reduces effect sizes by ~15–20%**: Large effects shrink, small effects vanish.
- **Net adjustment**: Overall confidence should drop by ~0.05–0.10 across the board if biases are fully accounted for.

### § 113: Comprehensive February 2026 Audit {#§113}

**Audit Team**: Three external neuroscientists, two environmental psychologists, one architect, one computational modeler. Independent, blind to design decisions.

**Audit Period**: February 3–20, 2026.

**Overall Result**: 3.0–3.25 stars out of 5.0 (DEGRADED)

#### Detailed Findings:

**System Strengths**:

1. **Comprehensive Coverage**: 208 templates across 12 domains. No major domain of environmental psychology is completely absent.

2. **Conservative Confidence**: 0.40–0.55 range prevents false precision. Not claiming certainty where none exists.

3. **Systematic Gap Analysis**: 153 gaps identified, 116 high-severity. Gaps are documented and prioritized.

4. **Transparent Provenance**: Every template has metadata about panel review, evidence source, warrant type, tier assignment. Traceability is excellent.

5. **Explicit Mechanism Chains**: No black-box inference. Every prediction rests on documented causal mechanisms (even if imperfect).

6. **Toulmin Justification**: Each mechanism step has explicit backing, warrant, and rebuttal. Arguments are structured.

#### Detailed Weaknesses:

**1. Cross-Template Interaction Gap (71%)**:

- Possible interactions: ~1,500 theoretically plausible pairwise interactions.
- Documented interactions: ~440 empirically studied.
- Coverage: 29.3% (complementary to the 71% gap).
- Consequence: Predictions in contexts with undocumented interactions are unreliable.
- Audit comment: "The library addresses single-template effects well, but combination effects are largely terra incognita."

**2. Theoretical Default Warrants (125 templates)**:

- 125 templates have THEORY_DERIVED warrant (base 0.40), indicating no empirical support.
- Distribution: Scattered across domains, but concentrated in OLFACTORY (8 templates), THERMAL (2 templates), NEUROMOD (11 templates).
- Audit comment: "These are scaffolds masquerading as calibrated templates. They should not be used for design without additional evidence."

**3. Unregistered Variables (272)**:

- Variables used in mechanism chains are not all registered in the unified vocabulary.
- Consequence: Redundancy and inconsistency. Multiple templates may use the same causal variable under different names (e.g., "prediction error," "expectancy violation," "surprise").
- Audit comment: "Variable naming is a mess. Computational traversal is error-prone."

**4. Bridge Warrant Violations (~60)**:

- 60 templates have confidence exceeding their warrant base. Example: HISTAMINE_AROUSAL (THEORY_DERIVED, base 0.40) assigned confidence 0.42 (exceeds by 0.02).
- Audit comment: "Bridge warrants are not being enforced. Confidence values are being set by domain experts who overestimate certainty."

**5. Path Hardcoding in Web_of_Belief**:

- Some computational pathways in web_of_belief.py are hand-coded for specific templates rather than data-driven.
- Example: The coherence computation for the NOVELTY_OPTIMAL vs. RESTORATION interaction is hard-coded (not inferred from general principles).
- Consequence: System does not generalize well; each new template requires code modification.
- Audit comment: "The system needs refactoring to be fully compositional. Currently, it is a hybrid of data-driven and hard-coded logic."

#### Evidence Base Biases:

**Four Systematic Biases Identified**:

1. **Western Academic Bias**: 92% of papers in Master Reference Inventory are from Western institutions (US, Canada, UK, Western Europe, Australia). Environmental psychology as practiced in non-Western contexts (Asia, Africa, Latin America) is underrepresented. Implication: Templates may not transfer across cultures.

2. **English-Language Bias**: 97% of references are in English. Non-English literature (especially from non-Western countries) is excluded. Estimated missing: ~50–100 papers per domain in non-English literature.

3. **Visual-Spatial Modality Bias**: 73% of evidence concerns visual or spatial properties (light, color, space, views). Olfaction (3%), taste (1%), proprioception (2%) are underrepresented. Implication: VISUAL-I templates are stronger than OLFACTORY-I templates.

4. **Acute-Over-Chronic Temporal Bias**: 78% of studies measure acute effects (hours to days). Chronic effects (weeks to months) are underrepresented. Implication: Templates predict short-term effects well but cannot predict long-term habituation or adaptation.

**Audit Recommendation**: All four biases are documented in the system. Practitioners should apply caution when using templates outside Western, academic, visual-spatial, acute contexts.

#### Skeptic Readiness Breakdown:

- **33% of templates (34/103)** would survive hostile peer review.
- **67% of templates** have credible but imperfect evidence and would likely lose confidence under skeptical scrutiny.
- **Strongest templates** (tier A, >40 replications): CIRCADIAN_ALIGNMENT, SPEECH_INTELLIGIBILITY, PROSPECT, INTEGRATION, STRESS_RECOVERY_OPPORTUNITY, DOPAMINE_VALENCE (6 total at skeptic-ready level).
- **Weakest templates** (tier C-D, <2 independent replications): ENDOCANNABINOID_SIGNALING, INFLAMMATORY_REDUCTION_OLFACTORY, TASTE_OLFACTORY_COHERENCE, PROPRIOCEPTIVE_VESTIBULAR_STABILITY, and others (27 total should be downgraded to scaffold tier).

#### Audit Recommendations (Five):

1. **External Validation**: Test top 20 templates in independent labs before design deployment. Compare predictions to outcomes.

2. **Confidence Reduction**: Apply 0.05–0.10 penalty across all templates to account for overconfidence, publication bias, and replication risk.

3. **Variable Registry Completion**: Register all 272 unregistered variables. Implement name-mapping system to handle synonyms.

4. **Path De-Hardcoding**: Refactor web_of_belief.py to remove hand-coded pathways. Implement fully compositional inference.

5. **Evidence Diversity Push**: Prioritize calibration studies in non-Western contexts, non-visual modalities, and chronic timescales. Current pipeline is too Western-centric.

#### Audit Conclusion:

*"The system is ambitious and thoughtful. It documents uncertainty explicitly and avoids false precision. However, it is not ready for high-stakes design deployment. The interaction gap, evidence biases, and overconfidence risk are significant. The system should be treated as a research prototype, not a finished tool. With continued work on the recommended improvements, it could become a valuable aid to design practice within 2–3 years."*

### Next Steps for Part XIII

### Next Steps

Part XIII documents the system's known limitations, audit results, and outstanding gaps. Three research directions address the highest-priority open questions.

First, **close the most consequential parameter gaps** systematically over the next 18–24 months. The February 2026 project audit identified 125 THEORY_DERIVED flags across the template library — instances where template parameters rest on theoretical extrapolation rather than direct architectural evidence. These 125 gaps should be prioritized by: (a) **design-impact significance** (does changing this parameter change the template's practical recommendations?), (b) **research tractability** (can this gap be closed with achievable studies?), and (c) **evidence quality** (is the theoretical default rooted in laboratory evidence with bridge support, or is it pure extrapolation?). A **targeted research programme** should identify 40–60 high-priority gaps, design 15–20 focused studies addressing them, and execute these studies over 18–24 months. Examples: VF1's visual-contour habituation τ ≈ 12–18 days is based on laboratory studies of photographs; a field study of actual building occupancy over 3–6 months would test whether this decay rate holds in inhabited spaces with semantic and biographical context. L5's PE engagement frequency optimal range (0.02–0.3 cycles/min) relies on auditory rhythm research; a study manipulating visual rhythm in actual architectural views (not simplified stimuli) would validate transfer. The goal is moving the 125 theoretical defaults toward bridge evidence and direct CNFA evidence, increasing composite-credence ceilings where currently constrained by THEORY_DERIVED flags.

Second, **systematize diversity and reduce WEIRD-sample bias** in the evidence base. The current template library draws evidence primarily from Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic (WEIRD) populations and Western architectural traditions. Expanding coverage to non-Western populations, building typologies, and architectural cultures is not merely politically correct; it is epistemically necessary for validating the system's universality claims. A **structured diversity expansion programme** should: (a) commission targeted literature reviews and studies in 5–8 non-Western cultural-architectural contexts (Japanese traditional, Middle Eastern Islamic, Sub-Saharan African, Indigenous North American, South Asian, Southeast Asian, Latin American), (b) test whether core mechanisms (e.g., SC1's integration-affect relationship, L-series circadian thresholds, SPATIAL-I's proportion preferences) generalize or require population-specific parametrization, (c) identify genuinely culturally-specific mechanisms not present in WEIRD populations (for example, feng-shui principles related to spatial harmony, Islamic architecture's integration of water and courtyard spatial logic, Indigenous emphasis on wayfinding via natural landmarks rather than cognitive maps), and (d) develop population-specific template variants with documented evidence bases. This 24-month research programme would expand the system from WEIRD-normative toward globally representative and culturally competent guidance.

Third, establish a **formal system-governance and maintenance structure** ensuring the ATLAS system continues to evolve and integrate new evidence beyond the February 2026 initial calibration. Without governance, the system risks becoming a static artifact disconnected from evolving science. We recommend: (a) **Annual Review Cycle** — each January, convene an oversight committee (4–5 senior experts, rotating membership) to review the past year's relevant literature, identify templates approaching revision thresholds (evidence suggests parameter change ≥ 0.10 in credence), and recommend calibration updates; (b) **Biennial Expansion Panels** — every two years, convene domain panels for new architectural typologies or populations (children's environments, aging-in-place, neurodiverse-friendly design) and new theoretical domains emerging from practitioner feedback; (c) **Reactive Falsification Protocol** — when direct architectural studies contradict a high-credence template prediction, trigger a 3-month review process involving the template's original panelists plus relevant experts to evaluate the conflicting evidence; (d) **Living Documentation** — maintain version-controlled documentation of all template parameters, revisions, and evidence sources, analogous to clinical-practice guidelines (AGREE instrument) or software version control; (e) **Transparency and Accessibility** — publish annual audit reports documenting which templates received confidence updates, why, and with what evidence; maintain a publicly accessible repository of all panel reports, evidence databases, and decision rationales. This governance structure transforms the ATLAS system from a February 2026 deliverable into a continuously evolving knowledge system.

The ultimate measure of success is whether the ATLAS system makes better buildings. This requires sustained partnerships with practicing architects, building scientists, and occupancy researchers to test whether ATLAS-informed designs outperform standard practice across validated human-outcome measures (comfort, productivity, restoration, wellbeing, creativity, social connection). The next 3–5 years should see 20–30 field validation studies in real buildings, creating the evidence base that will justify confidence in the system's practical guidance.
