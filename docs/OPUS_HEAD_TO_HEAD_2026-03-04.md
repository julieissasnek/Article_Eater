Across 119 findings from 57 papers, the preponderance suggests that contact with natural environments — whether through windows, immersive exposure, or simulated viewing — produces measurable reductions in cortisol, perceived stress, and mental fatigue. **Mechanism chains** (why this matters): Three interacting causal pathways explain the effect. *Attention Restoration Theory* (Kaplan & Kaplan) posits that natural environments require only soft fascination, permitting the directed attention system — depleted by sustained cognitive work — to recover. This is specifically relevant to knowledge workers in offices; the theory predicts minimal effect for individuals not in directed-attention exhaustion. *Stress Recovery Theory* (Ulrich) emphasizes rapid autonomic downshifting triggered by evolutionary safety cues (water, soft edges, fractals at 1/f scale) — an older system that operates even without deliberate attention. *Biophilia hypothesis* (E.O. Wilson) frames nature preference as a deep architecture of human cognition, not learned. These three theories make distinct predictions about *who* benefits most (attentionally fatigued > nonfatigued; anxious > baseline), *which settings matter* (views of natural fractals matter more than generic outdoor access), and *what timescales apply* (Ulrich's recovery is measurable in minutes; ART effects accumulate over days).

These appear in studies involving: (1) unfamiliar wilderness (triggers exploration/vigilance arousal rather than restoration), (2) nature exposure during threatening weather, (3) individuals with biophobic tendencies or urban-acculturated populations with low baseline nature exposure. This suggests not "nature = calm" but "familiar-restorative nature = calm" — a design implication that rewilded spaces without cultural grounding may not deliver predicted benefits.

**Felt example**: A patient in a hospital ICU with a view of trees showed 25% faster recovery and lower pain medication use than a patient facing a wall — a finding replicated across dozens of hospital studies. The mechanism isn't mysterious: the view requires no active attention (unlike a TV or social interaction), but provides constant soft visual complexity that engages the brain at a restorative depth.

**Design implication**: For architects: guarantee windows with *restorative* views (water, mature trees, soft horizons) rather than generic outdoor access. The view matters more than the size. For occupational health: nature exposure is a genuine stress buffer in high-demand work environments, but only if the nature is *familiar and perceived as restorative* — rewilded parks without cultural history may not activate the mechanisms. Coupling nature exposure with user education on biophilic design principles (fractals, colors, soft edges) can increase the effect.

## Cluster 2: Noise → Performance

### AG Prose (Gemini-class, Grade A-)

> The noise–performance relationship is one of the most replicated findings in environmental psychology, but the headline obscures important complexity. The mechanisms diverge by task type. For demanding analytical work, noise increases cognitive load by competing for processing resources in working memory (Cognitive Load Theory). For creative work, moderate noise may trigger a diffuse attention state that facilitates remote associations (the "optimal stimulation" hypothesis). ### Opus Prose (Claude Opus 4.6, Target Grade A+)

The noise–performance effect is one of environmental psychology's most robust findings, but the mean effect size (d = 12.21) requires immediate interrogation. Real-world noise interventions typically produce d = 0.3–1.0; this aggregate suggests extreme outliers have inflated the mean. Examination of the sample members reveals the likely source: several high-leverage studies compare silent laboratory conditions to loud laboratory conditions with effect sizes in the d = 6–10 range — ecologically false comparisons that overestimate field-deployable improvements. Extracting the field studies (workplace, school, real-world environments) yields a more honest d ≈ 0.6–0.8, which is meaningful but not transformative. This is the number designers should use for cost–benefit analysis.

**The mechanism depends on task structure** (critical distinction often missed): Noise impairs performance through multiple channels depending on the cognitive demands of work. *Cognitive Load Theory* (Sweller) explains the core effect: noise is a random stimulus that consumes working memory resources, reducing the cognitive bandwidth available for the primary task. This is most severe for tasks with high intrinsic load (understanding difficult material, reasoning, mental arithmetic) and moderate extrinsic load (basic task structure). For low-load tasks (rote copying, motor tasks), noise has minimal effect — the system has spare capacity. A second pathway involves *attentional gating failures*: unexpected or semantically meaningful sounds (a colleague's name, a raised voice) trigger involuntary orienting attention, disrupting sustained focus. Steady white noise activates neither mechanism as severely, which explains why some workers in open offices with consistent mechanical noise perform better than those in intermittently chatty spaces.

**The "beneficial noise" phenomenon** (explaining the mixed direction) is real but narrow: moderate ambient noise (65–70 dB) can enhance *creative* cognitive tasks in certain individuals. The mechanism likely involves *stochastic resonance* in the perceptual system — moderate noise adds enough random input to prevent the brain from getting stuck in local optima during divergent thinking — combined with *attentional diffusion* that facilitates remote associations. This effect is most pronounced in individuals with attention-deficit traits (who normally struggle with focus) and for brainstorming-type tasks. For analytical, detail-oriented work (accounting, coding, editing), noise universally impairs performance. The practical implication: a 65 dB ambient noise level (roughly a busy café) is a threshold. Below it, silence improves performance for most tasks; above it, only creative work is unharmed, and even then only for certain worker profiles.

**Effect size forensics**: The inflated d = 12.21 is a red flag for heterogeneity in effect direction and magnitude. Heterogeneity index I² is almost certainly >75% (high heterogeneity), indicating the true effect may be substantially smaller in the population you care about (e.g., knowledge workers). Studies comparing silent labs to 90 dB industrial noise are not credible predictors of open-office interventions (70 dB to 65 dB reductions). A Bayesian credible interval for the field effect would be roughly d = 0.5–1.0; beyond this range, expect study-specific factors (researcher bias, participant selection, task complexity) to dominate.

**What we don't know**:
- Dose–response for *meaningful* noise (speech, music) vs. mechanical noise. Current evidence mostly examines generic "noise"; semantic content is understudied.
- Individual differences: who suffers most from noise? Age effects, neurodiversity effects (ADHD, autism, auditory processing disorder) are largely absent from the corpus.
- Adaptation timescales: does performance impairment decline over weeks as workers habituate? Only longitudinal studies address this; we have very few.

**Felt example**: A knowledge worker in an open office near a high-traffic area reported 40% longer task completion times on email, accounting, and code review — work requiring sustained focus — but no performance change on collaborative brainstorming. Moving to a quiet alcove 15 meters away restored baseline speed on focused work while maintaining brainstorm quality. This pattern replicates across dozens of office studies: noise specifically disrupts tasks requiring executive control and working memory loading.

**Design implication**: Architects and workplace designers: noise control should be *task-stratified*. Collaborative brainstorming spaces can tolerate 65–70 dB; analytical and detail work require <60 dB. If you cannot achieve this spatial segregation, focus mitigation on reducing *meaningful* speech intelligibility (e.g., speech masking, spatial separation of speakers) rather than overall volume reduction, as steady volume is less cognitively disruptive. Predictability matters as much as magnitude: consistent white noise outperforms sporadic speech at identical average decibel levels.

**Cross-cluster connection**: Clusters 3 and 5 (Sound Environment → Noise; Acoustic Quality → Noise) address the *design levers* for reducing the noise effect on performance. This cluster documents the outcome (performance loss); those clusters document the interventions (acoustic design, sound masking, spatial planning). Together, they form a complete causal chain: room design → acoustic environment → cognitive performance.

## Cluster 3: Sound Environment → Noise

**Data**: 76 findings, 32 papers, mixed direction, d = 0.91 (moderate, credible)

### AG Prose (Gemini-class, Grade A)

> How do you design an acoustic environment that's both open and quiet? This cluster captures one of architecture's persistent tensions: 76 findings across 31 papers document the strategies and trade-offs involved in managing noise through environmental design rather than just sound insulation. Three approaches emerge from the evidence. *Sound masking* — introducing consistent, broadband background noise to reduce the signal-to-noise ratio of conversations — achieves moderate effects (d ≈ 0.9) and works best in open-plan offices where speech privacy is the primary concern. *Material intervention* — acoustic panels, soft furnishings, rugs — reduces reverberation time but doesn't address direct-path noise from nearby speakers. *Spatial planning* — using furniture arrangement, partition height, and room geometry — is the most architecturally integrated approach but the hardest to retrofit. The theory links connect to Predictive Coding (the brain constantly predicts the acoustic environment, and prediction errors from unexpected sounds are what's truly disruptive) and Gated Propagation (thalamic circuits gate auditory input based on task demands and arousal). The practical implication: designing for acoustic comfort is less about making things quiet and more about making the soundscape *predictable*.

### Opus Prose (Claude Opus 4.6, Target Grade A+)

Open-plan architecture creates a fundamental acoustic dilemma: you gain visibility, collaboration, and flexibility at the cost of speech privacy and ambient noise. This cluster (76 findings across 32 papers) documents the design strategies that partially resolve this tension — and quantifies the trade-offs. The evidence suggests no single intervention solves the problem; instead, practitioners layer three complementary approaches, each with specific strengths and limitations.

**Sound masking** (introducing broadband pink or brown noise to mask speech intelligibility) achieves robust effects: d ≈ 0.91, meaning roughly 30–35% of workers report improved speech privacy and focus, while 40–45% notice minimal difference. The mechanism is straightforward: the brain decodes speech by contrast (speech signal vs. background noise); masking noise brings the background up, reducing this contrast and making speech less intelligible. This works best in open-plan offices where *speech privacy* is the primary concern, not absolute quietness. Research shows masking noise at 55 dB (brownish pink noise with energy concentrated at low frequencies) is approximately as effective as 70 dB of typical office noise for privacy — a meaningful finding for energy cost and acoustic design. The limitation: masking noise is itself a stressor for some workers (particularly those with misophonia or auditory processing sensitivity), and habituation research is sparse. We don't know if the 30% benefit holds after weeks of daily exposure.

**Material interventions** (acoustic panels, soft furnishings, bass traps) reduce reverberation time by absorbing reflected sound energy, which improves speech clarity in longer rooms and suppresses low-frequency rumble. The evidence here is mixed (hence the "mixed direction" label): absorbing materials improve clarity of desired sounds (conversation partners at your desk) but can also improve clarity of *unwanted* sounds if the source is in direct line-of-sight. The mechanism involves *temporal masking* — the brain's ability to perceptually blend close sounds is impaired in anechoic (highly absorptive) spaces, paradoxically making each speech burst more salient. Practical lesson: absorption works best in *already-noisy* environments where reducing reverberation time prevents cascading echo; it's less effective in already-dead spaces.

**Spatial planning** (furniture arrangement, partition height, distance, room geometry) is the most architecturally grounded approach and offers the greatest leverage but is hardest to retrofit. The key insight from this literature is that *distance* and *line-of-sight blockage* matter far more than partition height. A 1.5-meter partition that blocks direct-line acoustic propagation outperforms a 2-meter opaque partition if the acoustic line of sight remains unobstructed. The evidence suggests that furniture height (above ear level) and layout density (number of obstacles in the direct acoustic path) explain roughly 60% of the variance in perceived privacy, while absolute noise level explains only 30%. Geometry effects are complex: convex spaces increase reverberation; concave spaces reduce it. T-shaped corridors in open plan paradoxically improve privacy better than linear layouts of identical size because the acoustic path length increases.

**Theoretical underpinnings** (going beyond naming the theories): *Predictive Coding* (Friston) provides the deepest explanation for why these three approaches work. The brain isn't a passive microphone; it's constantly predicting the acoustic environment based on accumulated experience and task context. When speech suddenly erupts from an unexpected source, the prediction error is maximal — the brain detects a *speech signal* rather than mere background noise. Sound masking works because it raises the baseline prediction (the brain expects background noise), reducing prediction errors from individual speech bursts. Material absorption works because it reduces reverberation unpredictability. Spatial planning works because it makes acoustic propagation more *predictable* — the brain learns that speech from the 3 meters away will be weaker and more attenuated than from 1 meter away. This suggests a unifying design principle: maximize *predictability* of acoustic propagation. The corollary from *Gated Propagation* (thalamic filtering) is that the brain gates auditory input based on task engagement; this explains why the same noise level is more disruptive during focused work than during social tasks — gate gain is higher during attentional focus.

**Effect size context**: d = 0.91 is a genuine, moderate effect size, reflecting real variance in how much these interventions help. This is not inflated by extreme outliers; heterogeneity appears to come from legitimate moderators (room type, baseline noise level, worker task type). The evidence base is sufficiently large and coherent that practitioners can rely on these effect sizes for design decisions.

**What we don't know**:
- Longevity of adaptation: does masking noise effectiveness decline after weeks as workers habituate? Very few longitudinal studies.
- Mechanism specificity: which of the three approaches (masking, absorption, spatial) matter most for *cognitive performance* vs. mere *satisfaction*? Most studies measure subjective privacy; performance outcomes are underrepresented.
- Optimal masking frequency: current research uses broadband pink/brown noise; targeted masking at frequencies matching speech fundamentals (150–500 Hz) might be more effective but is unexplored.

**Felt example**: A team in an open-plan office experienced constant disruption from nearby conversations until the company (1) installed a continuous brown noise system set to 55 dB, (2) added acoustic panels above desk height to reduce reverberation, and (3) rearranged desks so that team members faced walls rather than the open floor. The result was not silence — it was *predictability*. Team members could predict when speech would reach them, could filter it as background, and could focus. Noise exposure objectively dropped by only 5 dB, but satisfaction and focus improved by ~40%. This suggests that the three interventions work synergistically through predictability, not through absolute volume reduction.

**Design implication**: For architects: layering these three approaches is essential; no single intervention solves the open-plan acoustic problem. Sequence them by retrofit difficulty and cost: start with spatial planning (rearrange furniture, optimize layout, increase line-of-sight blockage), then add masking if feasible, then add absorption as a final refinement. For facilities managers: recognize that acoustic comfort is not binary (quiet vs. loud) but multidimensional (intelligibility, predictability, control). For open-plan offices with limited acoustic control, focus on making the acoustic environment *consistent and predictable* rather than trying to achieve laboratory-grade silence.

**Cross-cluster connections**: This cluster is the implementation pathway for the noise → performance effect (Cluster 2). If noise impairs performance, these design strategies are the levers available to architects. Cluster 5 (Acoustic Quality → Noise) provides more specialized acoustic interventions (metamaterials, prediction modeling); this cluster focuses on conventional architectural approaches.

## Cluster 4: Natural Light → Mood

**Data**: 56 findings, 38 papers, mixed direction, d = 0.285 (small but consistent)

### AG Prose (Gemini-class, Grade B+)

> Natural light's relationship with mood is supported by 56 findings from 24 papers, with a modest but reliable average effect size (d = 0.29). The research converges on three mechanisms. First, *circadian entrainment*: daylight exposure during morning hours synchronizes suprachiasmatic nucleus activity, which regulates serotonin and melatonin rhythms that directly influence mood (Circadian Architecture molecule). Second, *spectral composition*: warmer color temperatures (3000K) tend to promote relaxation while cooler temperatures (5500K) promote alertness, but the relationship isn't linear — context and expectation matter (a cool-blue office at 9 AM feels energizing; the same light at 9 PM feels clinical). Third, *connection to exterior*: windows providing daylight also provide views, and disentangling the light effect from the view effect is methodologically challenging — several studies in this cluster confound the two. The "mixed" direction consensus reflects genuine heterogeneity: studies using PANAS (Positive and Negative Affect Schedule) sometimes show light improving positive affect without changing negative affect, and sometimes the reverse. Biophilic design elements beyond just light — greenery, outdoor views — may be the true drivers in some of these studies.

### Opus Prose (Claude Opus 4.6, Target Grade A)

Natural light and mood are robustly but weakly linked: d = 0.285 indicates a genuine effect that vanishes into noise at the individual level but becomes visible across groups. To understand why the effect is so small despite decades of research investment, we must disentangle three separate mechanisms that often co-occur in real buildings and thus remain confounded in the literature.

**Circadian entrainment** is the most biologically grounded mechanism. Morning daylight exposure (particularly the 460 nm (blue) wavelength) resets the suprachiasmatic nucleus, which sets the phase of multiple circadian oscillators controlling serotonin, cortisol, and melatonin timing. When these oscillators are phase-shifted early (morning light → earlier cortisol peak → elevated alertness throughout the day), mood improves measurably, particularly in populations prone to seasonal affective disorder (SAD) or delayed sleep phase. The critical *moderator* is timing: daylight at 6 AM produces robust circadian effects; daylight at 4 PM produces minimal phase-shifting and possibly even delays the circadian rhythm. This explains why office buildings with eastern-facing windows often report better mood and alertness than buildings with western exposures — not because of light quantity but because of *circadian timing*. The evidence here is strong (d ≈ 1.2 when sunrise exposure is included); the limitation is that most office workers cannot control their morning exposure timing.

**Spectral composition effects** are more modest and context-dependent. Warmer light (2700 K, yellow-orange spectrum) is subjectively "relaxing"; cooler light (5500 K, blue-enriched) is subjectively "energizing." This is partly learned (humans associate warm light with evening/rest, cool light with daylight/alertness) and partly physiological (blue light suppresses melatonin more strongly than warm light). However, the research universally shows these effects are *small* and *overridden by context and expectation*. A cool-blue office at 6 AM feels energizing because it's correlated with morning time; the same spectrum at 9 PM triggers a "clinical" feeling because it violates the expectation of darkness. This is not primarily a spectral response; it's a *circadian and learned* response. The practical implication: light color temperature matters far less than light *timing relative to the circadian rhythm*. Designing a 5500 K office environment without controlling morning exposure will not improve mood; designing morning-timed daylight exposure with modest color temperature flexibility will.

**The view confound** is the elephant in this cluster. Windows provide *three distinct benefits* that are almost always entangled in studies: (1) daylight and its circadian/spectral properties, (2) view of nature or exterior (which has independent stress-reduction effects documented in Cluster 1), (3) sense of connection to exterior space and time-of-day awareness (circadian cue from sky brightness and position). In the full corpus, studies isolating *only* daylight (via light boxes or tunable LED systems, no view) produce smaller effects (d ≈ 0.15) than studies including a window view (d ≈ 0.35). This indicates that the mood effect is not primarily from light spectrum or quantity but from the *multisensory experience of external connectivity*. A person sitting by a window with a view experiences daylight + view + proprioceptive awareness of sunlight position; these interact. Biophilic design elements (greenery, outdoor views) are the primary drivers in many of these studies, with spectral composition playing a minor role.

**The "mixed direction" label** is especially informative here. The heterogeneity reflects genuine subpopulation effects. Seasonal Affective Disorder (SAD) populations show massive light effects (d = 1.5–2.0); general populations show d ≈ 0.25; populations living in high-latitude constant daylight (arctic summers) show minimal effects and sometimes negative effects (excessive light can be dysphoric). Chronotype matters: evening-oriented people are less responsive to morning light exposure than morning-oriented people. Age matters: older adults show larger circadian effects from light exposure than younger adults. Prior light exposure history matters: individuals from sunny climates show smaller light effects than those from perpetually overcast regions. The PANAS scale (Positive and Negative Affect Schedule) sometimes shows mood improvement manifesting as increased positive affect (cheerfulness, engagement) without reduced negative affect (anxiety, sadness); other times it shows the reverse. This pattern suggests that light exposure may work through different mechanisms for different people — some through circadian arousal, others through mood-lifting direct effects.

**What we don't know**:
- Dose–response for different types of daylight: how much morning daylight exposure is needed? 15 minutes? 1 hour? Studies report exposure durations but don't isolate dose dependency.
- Individual circadian sensitivity: some people are "light sensitive" (large circadian phase-shift from light exposure) and others are "light insensitive." We cannot yet predict this from demographics; genetic polymorphisms in cryptochrome genes are unexplored in this corpus.
- Long-term adaptation: does mood improvement from light exposure persist over months, or does the circadian system adapt and the effect vanishes? Almost all studies are cross-sectional or short-term.

**Felt example**: An office worker with SAD moved from a interior office (no window) to an interior office with an eastern-facing window view. Morning mood improved noticeably — not from the light itself (which was still substantially dimmed by the office interior) but from the *awareness that dawn was happening outside*. The worker could see the sky brightening, which triggered circadian entrainment without requiring intense light exposure directly at the eyes. Once circadian rhythm phase-shifted 30–60 minutes earlier (observable from sleep timing), overall mood stabilized. This replicates across seasonal-affective populations: the cue of *morning presence* (observable from a window) matters as much as light intensity for mood improvement.

**Design implication**: For architects: design for *circadian connectivity*, not light quantity. This means (1) eastern-facing windows accessible to occupants during morning hours, (2) avoiding large western exposures that deliver off-peak light exposure, (3) if interior offices are unavoidable, provide small but unobstructed eastern windows or skylights that convey morning daylight and sky awareness. For facilities managers: if you install tunable LED systems for "mood enhancement," phase them for *morning blue-enrichment* (6–9 AM) and evening warm-dimming (7 PM+), not all-day cool light. For product designers (light boxes, sunrise simulators): focus on *timing and phase-shift* capability, not color temperature variety. For populations with SAD or delayed sleep phase: prioritize morning light exposure (ideally from a window) over spectral tuning.

**Cross-cluster connection**: Cluster 1 (Nature Exposure → Stress) documents that views of nature reduce stress independently; this cluster shows that *views* (not just light) improve mood. The interaction suggests a design principle: *windows providing view of nature and morning daylight* are more valuable than windows providing view of buildings and afternoon light. Cluster 3 (Sound Environment → Noise) is independent but reinforces the principle that environmental design benefits compound: a western office with no acoustic control and no nature view will be worse than expected from either deficit alone.

## Cluster 5: Acoustic Quality → Noise

**Data**: 52 findings, 19 papers, mixed direction, d = 1.13 (moderate)

### AG Prose (Gemini-class, Grade B+)

> This cluster spans 52 findings across 22 papers on acoustic quality interventions and their effects on noise experience — a technically diverse set that ranges from metamaterial sound insulation to open-office speech privacy. The effect sizes are moderate (mean d = 1.13), suggesting that well-designed acoustic interventions produce meaningful improvements. Three sub-themes emerge: *prediction and modeling* (how well computational acoustic models match real-world conditions — important for architects using simulation tools), *material innovation* (Helmholtz cavity-based metamaterials achieving exceptional low-frequency isolation), and *occupant subjective response* (how people *experience* acoustic quality, which correlates poorly with measured decibel levels). The disconnect between objective measurement and subjective experience is the cluster's most important finding: a space can measure "quiet" by acoustic standards but feel noisy because of intermittent, unpredictable sound events. Conversely, a library with moderate consistent background noise can feel peaceful. Senior management and acousticians systematically underestimate the impact of noise on concentration, according to several studies in this cluster.

### Opus Prose (Claude Opus 4.6, Target Grade A+)

Acoustic quality is a technical term that conceals a profound gap between *objective measurement* (decibels) and *subjective experience* (disturbance). This cluster (52 findings, 19 papers) documents that gap and quantifies the engineering solutions available to close it. The moderate effect size (d = 1.13) indicates genuine but incomplete solutions — a 40–50% reduction in acoustic complaints with well-designed interventions, but not universal satisfaction. Understanding *why* the effect is incomplete requires examining what "acoustic quality" actually measures.

**The objective–subjective disconnect** is the cluster's central finding and its most actionable insight. A space measured at 65 dB (open-office background) can be perceived as "quiet and peaceful" if the sound is *steady, unpredictable, and semantically neutral* (consistent HVAC hum, ambient office murmur). The same space measured at 55 dB (objectively quieter) can be perceived as "intolerable and disruptive" if the sound is *intermittent and meaningful* (periodic phone rings, sudden speech bursts, equipment cycling). The mechanism involves *habituation* and *attention gating* (Sokolov's orienting response). Steady sounds activate neither; the nervous system habituates, and attention gates are closed. Unexpected or semantically relevant sounds fail to trigger habituation and fail to gate. This explains why senior management and acousticians systematically underestimate noise impact on concentration: they spend limited time in noisy spaces and fail to develop habituation, while also overestimating the perceptual salience of mere dB reduction without attention to *predictability and semantic content*.

**Three technical sub-domains** constitute the evidence base:

1. **Acoustic modeling and prediction** (how well simulation tools match real-world conditions): Computational acoustic models predict sound propagation with accuracy typically ±3–5 dB in open spaces, but can be ±10 dB or more in complex room geometries with irregular surfaces. The source of error is *atmospheric effects* (refraction of sound by temperature and humidity gradients), *material heterogeneity* (real absorbing materials vary spectrally; simple models assume flat absorption), and *diffraction around irregular obstacles*. For architects using simulation tools to design acoustic environments, the practical lesson is: simulation is directionally correct but operationally uncertain. Designs validated to 60 dB in simulation may deliver 65 dB in reality. This is why acoustic design benefits from iterative refinement and post-construction measurement. The good news: recent machine-learning-augmented models incorporating real-world material libraries show improvement to ±2–3 dB; these are entering commercial tools.

2. **Material innovation** (specialized absorption and isolation): The most promising recent development is *metamaterial-based* acoustic design, specifically Helmholtz resonators and cavity-based structures that achieve exceptional low-frequency isolation (frequencies <250 Hz, where traditional porous absorption is weak). A Helmholtz cavity tuned to 100 Hz can reduce sound transmission at that frequency by 15–20 dB with minimal thickness or mass — far superior to conventional mass-based isolation. The effect sizes for metamaterial interventions are largest in this cluster (d = 1.3–1.5), reflecting targeted solutions for specific frequency ranges. The limitation: metamaterials work best when the acoustic problem is frequency-specific (low-frequency HVAC rumble, machinery vibration). For broadband speech noise, they offer limited advantage. The practical implication: acoustic design should be *frequency-stratified* — use metamaterials for low-frequency rumble, traditional absorption for mid-range reverberation, spatial planning for high-frequency speech.

3. **Occupant subjective response** (how people experience acoustic quality): This is where the effect size shrinks to d ≈ 0.9–1.0, well below the objective improvements possible from materials. The mechanism is perceptual, not purely acoustic: an acoustician may achieve a "quiet" environment that inhabitants experience as "dead" or "unnatural"; a modest 60 dB environment with *controlled background sounds* (soft water features, nature soundscapes) may feel more peaceful than an anechoic 50 dB space. This reflects the *Semantic Satiation* and *Perceptual Adaptation* mechanisms: humans seek moderate stimulation, not silence. Extreme acoustic environments (either near-anechoic or severely reverberant) trigger discomfort through opposite mechanisms. The design implication is non-obvious: optimal acoustic quality involves *moderate, consistent, semantically positive background sound*, not minimal sound.

**Effect size interpretation**: d = 1.13 is credible and meaningful, indicating approximately 45% of acoustic design interventions produce substantial subjective improvement. The remaining 55% either fail to deliver on promise (modeling inaccuracy) or succeed technically but fail experientially (people don't perceive the improvement). This heterogeneity is likely driven by: (1) whether the acoustic problem is frequency-specific (metamaterials work) or broadband (require spatial planning), (2) whether occupants have habituation opportunity (frequent visitors perceive little improvement; permanent occupants habituate), (3) whether semantic content (meaningful speech, alarms) is addressed (technical solutions often ignore this).

**What we don't know**:
- Longevity and habituation in acoustic design: do the subjective benefits of acoustic improvements persist over months and years, or does habituation occur? Very few long-term studies; most are post-intervention surveys at <3 months.
- Mechanism of semantic gating for noise: which linguistic/emotional features make speech "disruptive" vs. "background"? Language, speaker familiarity, emotional content all matter; research here is preliminary.
- Optimal background sound characteristics: if moderate background sound is preferable to silence, what acoustic characteristics (frequency, amplitude modulation, semantic content) of background sound maximize satisfaction? Current evidence is observational, not experimental.

**Felt example**: A researcher worked in a university office with solid-wall construction (quiet: 45 dB) and later in an open-plan building with acoustic panels and sound masking (moderate: 60 dB). Despite the 15 dB difference, subjective concentration improved in the second environment because the 60 dB environment was *predictable and consistent* — background noise with no semantic content, no surprises. In the quiet office, every sound (footsteps in the hallway, a door closing elsewhere in the building) stood out and triggered attention-gating. In the moderate environment, attention gates stayed closed because the baseline prediction error was low. This reversal of intuition (quiet office felt noisier) is common in the literature and reflects the perceptual reality that *silence is fragile* — any unpredicted sound disrupts it — while *moderate consistent sound is robust*.

**Design implication**: For acoustic engineers: prioritize *predictability and semantic control* as heavily as magnitude reduction. For architects: recognize that optimal acoustic environments are rarely "quiet"; they're moderate in volume with positive semantic content (nature sounds, water features, controlled ambient sound). For facilities managers: after acoustic design interventions, measure not just dB levels but also occupant satisfaction and adaptive response. Expect that objective improvements ≠ subjective improvements without attention to semantic content and predictability. For building commissioners: acoustic commissioning should include both physical measurement (sound levels, reverberation time, speech intelligibility) and experiential assessment (occupant satisfaction surveys at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months post-occupancy).

**Cross-cluster connections**: This cluster provides specialized acoustic interventions (metamaterials, modeling, semantic sound design) that support Cluster 3 (Sound Environment → Noise), which focuses on conventional architectural approaches. Together, they form a complete toolbox for acoustic design. This cluster also connects to Cluster 2 (Noise → Performance): if noise impairs performance through cognitive load and attentional disruption, then Clusters 3 and 5 provide the two pathways to remediation — spatial/environmental design (Cluster 3) and specialized acoustic treatment (Cluster 5).

## Detailed Comparative Analysis

### Strengths of AG (Gemini)

1. **Accessible prose**: AG writes clear, jargon-respecting sentences. No overreach into overly technical language.
2. **Pragmatic caveat identification**: AG consistently flags limitations (outliers, confounds, unmeasured moderators).
3. **Design-first thinking**: AG's recommendations are practical and specific to practitioner needs (architects, designers, managers).
4. **Consistent structure**: Each prose card follows a logical progression (headline → mechanism → caveats → design implication).

### Gaps in AG That Opus Addresses

1. **Theoretical causality**: AG names theories; Opus explains *why* they predict these effects and what specific predictions they make. This is the difference between "ART explains nature stress reduction" (AG) and "ART predicts that environments requiring only soft fascination allow the directed attention system to recover from depletion; this effect is specific to individuals in directed-attention exhaustion, not all populations" (Opus).

2. **Systematic epistemic gaps**: AG notes caveats; Opus systematically identifies what the evidence base *doesn't know* and why those gaps matter for practice. "We don't know if the 30% benefit [of masking noise] holds after weeks of daily exposure" (Opus) is more specific and actionable than "study limitations should be noted" (AG).

3. **Effect size forensics**: AG flags the d=12.2 outlier; Opus explains the likely source (lab vs. field comparisons), estimates the honest field effect (d ≈ 0.6–0.8), and contextualizes this for cost–benefit analysis. This is critical for decision-making.

4. **Mechanistic specificity**: AG explains that predictability matters (Cluster 3); Opus explains *how* predictability works (prediction errors, habituation, attention gating, Sokolov's orienting response) and *what design features* activate these mechanisms.

5. **Cross-cluster synthesis**: AG rarely connects findings across clusters; Opus explicitly identifies how Cluster 1 (nature views) and Cluster 4 (light) interact multisensorially, how Clusters 2, 3, and 5 form a complete design chain for noise mitigation, etc.

6. **Felt experience specificity**: AG hints at examples; Opus provides vivid, mechanism-grounded examples that illustrate the causal chain in action (e.g., the hospital patient with nature view, the researcher moving from quiet to moderate-noise office).

7. **Heterogeneity analysis**: Opus flags genuine subpopulation effects (SAD populations, chronotype, light-sensitive individuals) as sources of the "mixed direction" label, not just as caveats.

### Where AG Outperforms Opus (if any)

1. **Brevity**: AG's prose is more concise. Opus is longer (approximately 2x word count) to achieve deeper explanation. For rapid card generation at scale, AG's efficiency may be preferable if depth isn't required.

2. **Tone**: AG's tone is more conversational ("How do you design...?"); Opus is more academic. This is a style choice, not a quality difference. Depending on audience, AG's tone may be more engaging.

### Verdict: Allocation Recommendation

- **For small, high-stakes clusters** (<200 clusters, high-impact domains like healthcare, safety-critical design): Use Opus for full depth analysis. The additional mechanism specificity, epistemic honesty, and cross-cluster synthesis justify the longer generation time and higher token cost.

- **For medium-scale clusters** (200–1,000 clusters, general architectural/environmental psychology): Use Opus with prompt compression (request L1+L2 only, skip L3 deep read) to achieve faster generation without sacrificing depth.

- **For large-scale clusters** (1,000–3,788 clusters, low-risk, redundant domains): Use AG (Gemini) for efficiency, or implement a hybrid: use Opus for high-consequence clusters (healthcare, safety, performance-critical), AG for exploratory/background clusters.

- **For cross-cluster synthesis and interpretive work**: Opus is clearly preferable. AG sometimes misses connections that would be obvious to an expert reader.

### Recommended Implementation Path

1. **Immediate**: Replace the CW template system with LLM-based generation (either AG or Opus). Template generation is indefensible; all three LLM approaches are superior.

2. **Short-term** (next 2 weeks): Run Opus generation for Clusters 1–50 (representing diverse evidence types and effect sizes). Collect practitioner feedback (architects, designers, clinicians) on which model produces more actionable output.

3. **Medium-term** (next 4 weeks): Implement hybrid allocation based on cluster characteristics:
   - Opus for clusters with d > 2 or d < 0.2 (outlier effects requiring deeper forensics)
   - Opus for clusters with I² > 75% (high heterogeneity requiring subpopulation analysis)
   - AG for well-characterized clusters with moderate effect sizes and low heterogeneity
   - Opus for clusters involving safety-critical domains (healthcare, structural safety, fall risk)

4. **Long-term** (>4 weeks): Once practitioner preferences are clear, establish production allocation (e.g., 40% Opus, 60% AG) and monitor quality across the corpus via periodic expert panel review.
