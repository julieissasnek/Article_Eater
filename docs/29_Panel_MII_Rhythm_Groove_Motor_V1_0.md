## Panel Charter

**Primary Question**: How does the brain perceive rhythmic structure in music, why does rhythm produce the urge to move (groove), and what neural mechanisms couple the auditory system to the motor system during music listening and performance?

**Why This Panel Second**: Panel M-I established how the brain predicts pitch and harmonic structure. But music is fundamentally a temporal art — perhaps more so than a pitch art. Rhythm is the dimension of music most directly linked to embodied experience: we tap our feet, nod our heads, dance. The motor system's engagement with music is not optional or secondary — it is constitutive. Rhythm perception cannot be understood without the motor system, and the motor system's involvement explains phenomena (groove, entrainment, dance) that pitch-based models cannot address.

**Scope**: This panel covers beat perception, meter, syncopation, groove, sensorimotor coupling, and the basal ganglia/cerebellar timing systems. It does NOT cover melodic or harmonic prediction (Panel M-I), emotional responses to rhythmic violations (Panel M-III), or large-scale temporal form (Panel M-IV), though connections will be flagged.

## Part 1: Individual Position Statements

### 1.1 Jessica Grahn — The Basal Ganglia Find the Beat

Let me begin with a finding that surprised the field: beat perception — the ability to extract a regular pulse from a rhythmic pattern — is not an auditory cortex computation. It requires the basal ganglia (Grahn & Brett, 2007; Grahn & Rowe, 2009, 2013).

When listeners hear rhythmic sequences, two distinct networks activate depending on whether the rhythm has a clear beat (metrically simple) or not (metrically complex/non-isochronous):

**Beat-based rhythms** (clear pulse): Activate the **putamen** (dorsal striatum), supplementary motor area (SMA), and premotor cortex — the cortico-basal ganglia-thalamo-cortical motor loop. This activation occurs even when participants are instructed to lie still and not move. The motor system engages AUTOMATICALLY when a beat is present.

**Non-beat-based rhythms** (no clear pulse): Activate cerebellum and lateral prefrontal cortex — a timing network that tracks intervals between events without extracting a periodic pulse. The basal ganglia do NOT engage for these rhythms.

This double dissociation reveals something fundamental: **beat perception is a motor computation, not an auditory one**. The auditory cortex provides the temporal input, but the extraction of periodic regularity — the "finding" of the beat — is performed by the basal ganglia's pattern-detection circuitry. The BG are optimized for detecting and predicting periodic temporal patterns, likely because their core function in motor control requires exactly this capacity: generating sequences of movements timed to internal periodic signals.

**The connection to Parkinson's disease**: PD patients, who have dopaminergic degeneration in the basal ganglia, show impaired beat perception and impaired rhythm reproduction. This is not a motor output problem — they have difficulty PERCEIVING the beat even when no movement is required (Grahn & Brett, 2009). Conversely, rhythmic auditory stimulation improves their motor output (Thaut's work), because the external auditory beat compensates for the damaged internal beat-generation mechanism.

**For the panel**: The basal ganglia's role in beat perception means that rhythm perception is inherently motor. You cannot perceive a beat without engaging the motor system. This is why music makes us want to move — the beat IS a motor representation, activated automatically by periodic auditory input. Groove is not a secondary response to rhythm perception; it is an integral part of the perceptual process itself.

### 1.2 Edward Large — How Oscillators Create Beats from Sound

Let me specify the mechanism by which neural oscillators extract beat from rhythm (Large & Palmer, 2002; Large & Snyder, 2009; Large, 2008).

Neural populations in auditory and motor cortex can be modeled as **nonlinear oscillators** with several key properties:

**Entrainment**: When a periodic auditory stimulus is present, neural oscillators adjust their frequency to match the stimulus frequency. This is not passive following — it is active frequency matching with a finite entrainment range. An oscillator with a natural frequency near the stimulus frequency will entrain; one far from the stimulus frequency will not. The auditory system contains oscillators spanning a range of natural frequencies, and the strongest entrainment occurs at the oscillator whose natural frequency best matches the stimulus.

**Mode-locking**: Nonlinear oscillators can entrain not just at the fundamental frequency of the stimulus but at integer ratios: 2:1 (half-time), 1:2 (double-time), 3:2, 4:3, etc. This produces a hierarchy of entrained frequencies from a single periodic input. The beat is the frequency with the strongest mode-locked entrainment; the meter is the set of harmonically related frequencies that also entrain.

**Spontaneous pattern formation**: When multiple oscillators are coupled together (as neurons in a population are), they spontaneously organize into stable phase relationships. The strong beats of a meter emerge as modes of this self-organized oscillatory pattern. The metric hierarchy (downbeat > secondary beat > weak beat) corresponds to the amplitude hierarchy of entrained oscillatory modes.

**Prediction as phase**: Once entrained, an oscillator's phase at any moment constitutes a prediction about when the next event will occur. Phase peaks correspond to expected event times. An event occurring at a phase peak (on the beat) requires no phase adjustment. An event occurring off the phase peak (syncopated) requires phase adjustment — THIS IS THE PREDICTION ERROR, expressed as phase correction rather than information-theoretic surprise.

**Why this matters for the panel**: NRT provides a mechanistic account of HOW the basal ganglia and auditory cortex extract beat structure. The oscillators are not metaphorical — there is substantial evidence that neural populations in auditory cortex, SMA, and BG exhibit oscillatory activity that entrains to rhythmic stimuli (Fujioka, Trainor, Large, & Ross, 2012). Beta-band oscillations (~20Hz) in auditory cortex and motor cortex are modulated by rhythmic structure: beta power increases at expected beat times (reflecting prediction) and decreases at unexpected times (reflecting prediction error).

### 1.3 Maria Witek — The Goldilocks Zone of Groove

Groove — the pleasurable urge to move to music — obeys the same Goldilocks principle we have seen throughout the Article Eater project (Witek, Clarke, Wallentin, Kringelbach, & Vuust, 2014; Witek et al., 2017).

I conducted the definitive study by creating drum patterns that systematically varied in syncopation — from zero syncopation (all events on the beat, maximally predictable) to high syncopation (many events off the beat, maximally unpredictable) — and measured groove ratings and desire-to-move ratings from listeners.

**The result is a clear inverted-U**:
- **Low syncopation** (highly predictable): Moderate groove ratings. The rhythm is too predictable — there is no tension, no surprise, no reason for the motor system to actively engage.
- **Moderate syncopation** (Goldilocks zone): MAXIMUM groove ratings. Some beats are confirmed (maintaining the sense of pulse), while others are displaced (creating rhythmic prediction error). The motor system is maximally engaged because it is generating predictions that are partially confirmed and partially violated — it is in the sweet spot of active prediction.
- **High syncopation** (chaotic): LOW groove ratings. Too many beats are displaced — the listener loses the sense of pulse entirely. Without a pulse, there is no periodic prediction, and without prediction, there is no prediction error, and without PE, there is no groove. The motor system disengages because it cannot entrain.

**The neural correlate**: fMRI data show that moderate syncopation produces the greatest activation in basal ganglia (putamen), SMA, and premotor cortex — the beat perception network identified by Grahn. High syncopation activates cerebellum and prefrontal cortex instead (the interval-timing network). The transition from groove to non-groove corresponds to a switch from BG-based periodic prediction to cerebellar-based aperiodic interval tracking.

**For the panel and the broader project**: This is the rhythmic instantiation of the universal Goldilocks principle:

| Domain | Low PE | Goldilocks PE | High PE |
|---|---|---|---|
| Architecture | Monotony (blank wall) | Optimal complexity (D ≈ 1.3) | Chaos (visual noise) |
| Education | Boredom (too easy) | Desirable difficulty | Frustration (too hard) |
| Music: Pitch | Boring (nursery rhyme) | Moderate surprise (jazz) | Incomprehensible (noise) |
| Music: Rhythm | No groove (metronome) | Maximal groove (funk) | No groove (random) |

The same inverted-U, the same PE mechanism, the same Goldilocks optimum — across four domains and two sensory modalities.

### 1.4 Aniruddh Patel — ASAP: The Motor System Generates Temporal Predictions

Why does the motor system engage during passive rhythm perception? My **Action Simulation for Auditory Prediction (ASAP)** hypothesis provides the answer (Patel & Iversen, 2014; Patel, 2021).

The core claim: **the motor system is recruited during beat perception because motor planning provides more temporally precise predictions than auditory processing alone**. The auditory system is excellent at spectral processing (what sound) but only adequate at temporal processing (when sound). The motor system, by contrast, has been optimized by evolution for precise temporal control — every movement requires exquisitely precise timing. By recruiting motor planning circuits to simulate the temporal structure of rhythmic input, the auditory system can generate temporal predictions with the precision needed for beat perception.

The evidence:

1. **TMS studies**: Disrupting SMA with transcranial magnetic stimulation impairs beat perception even when no movement is required (Grube, Cooper, & Griffiths, 2010). If beat perception were purely auditory, disrupting a motor region should have no effect.

2. **Entrainment of motor cortex**: During passive music listening, motor cortex excitability fluctuates with the beat — it is highest at expected beat times and lowest between beats (Stupacher, Wood, & Witte, 2017). The motor system is simulating the beat even when the body is still.

3. **Anatomical evidence**: The auditory-motor connection via the dorsal auditory stream (auditory cortex → parietal cortex → premotor cortex → SMA) provides the anatomical substrate for motor prediction of auditory timing.

4. **Species differences**: Beat perception (and spontaneous entrainment to music) is rare in the animal kingdom. Patel's hypothesis predicts that it should occur only in species with vocal learning capacity — because vocal learning requires the same auditory-motor coupling needed for beat perception. Supporting data: parrots (vocal learners) can entrain to a beat; dogs and cats (non-vocal-learners) cannot. The notable exception is sea lions, which may use a related but distinct auditory-motor pathway.

**For the panel**: ASAP reframes the auditory-motor interaction. The motor system is not passively "responding" to the beat — it is actively generating the temporal predictions that constitute beat perception. This means groove (the urge to move) is not a secondary consequence of perceiving a beat — it is the subjective experience of the motor prediction system being maximally engaged. You feel the urge to move because your motor system IS already "moving" internally — simulating the temporal structure of the music.

### 1.5 Michael Thaut — Rhythmic Entrainment as Clinical Proof of Concept

I want to bring the clinical evidence that demonstrates just how powerful the auditory-motor coupling is (Thaut, McIntosh, & Hoemberg, 2015; Thaut & Hoemberg, 2014).

**Rhythmic Auditory Stimulation (RAS)** — providing a metronome or rhythmic music as an external timing cue — produces remarkable improvements in motor function in neurological patients:

**Parkinson's disease**: PD patients who walk to rhythmic auditory cues show immediate improvements in gait velocity (typically 15-25%), stride length, and cadence (Thaut et al., 1996). The external beat compensates for the damaged internal timing mechanism of the basal ganglia. Critically, the improvement is not just faster walking — it is more REGULAR walking, with reduced timing variability. The auditory beat entrains the motor output to a stable periodic pattern.

**Stroke rehabilitation**: Stroke patients receiving RAS-based gait training show significantly greater improvements in walking speed, stride symmetry, and functional mobility than those receiving conventional therapy (Thaut et al., 2007). The auditory rhythm provides a temporal scaffold that the damaged motor system can synchronize to.

**Traumatic brain injury**: TBI patients show impaired motor timing and coordination. RAS improves upper-limb reaching movements (timing, smoothness, and accuracy) in these patients (Thaut et al., 2015).

**The mechanism**: Auditory entrainment works because the auditory-motor pathway is bidirectional and below conscious control. The auditory beat signal reaches the motor system via subcortical (reticulospinal) and cortical (auditory → premotor) pathways. Once the motor system entrains to the external beat, it produces motor output that is temporally locked to the auditory rhythm — even in patients whose internal timing mechanisms are damaged.

**For the panel**: The clinical evidence provides a "proof of concept" for the auditory-motor coupling that Grahn, Large, and Patel have described. If the coupling were weak or optional, it would not be powerful enough to override damaged motor timing in neurological patients. The fact that it does — reliably and substantially — demonstrates that auditory-motor entrainment is a deep, phylogenetically old mechanism, not a cognitive luxury.

### 1.6 Peter Keller — Ensemble Timing: Social Prediction Through Rhythm

I want to extend the individual prediction model to the social context of music-making (Keller, Novembre, & Hove, 2014; Keller, 2014).

When two or more musicians play together, they must coordinate their temporal output with millisecond precision. This requires each musician to generate predictions about when the other musicians will produce their next events. **Ensemble coordination is a prediction problem**: each performer maintains internal models of the others' timing and uses these models to adapt their own motor output.

The mechanisms of ensemble coordination:

**Anticipation**: Performers predict the timing of upcoming events from other musicians based on their learned models of the musical structure and the other performers' tendencies. EEG studies show that performers' brain activity anticipates partners' events by ~100-200ms — they are not reacting to the other musician's sound but predicting it.

**Adaptation**: When a prediction error occurs (the other musician plays slightly early or late), performers rapidly adjust their timing. This adaptation follows a linear phase correction model: the correction is proportional to the asynchrony, with a coupling constant that determines how strongly each performer corrects toward the other. Tight coupling = rapid correction = locked timing. Loose coupling = slow correction = more temporal freedom.

**Simulation**: Performers use their OWN motor system to simulate the other musician's movements. This is the mirror system (T49) applied to music: your motor cortex simulates the movements your partner is making, generating predictions about when their next movement (and therefore sound) will occur.

**For the panel and the broader framework**: Ensemble music-making is an instance of inter-brain coupling — the same phenomenon described in the Social Brain panel for architecture (social affordance reading, T48) and proposed for education (teacher-student neural coupling, Panel E-V). In all three domains, social interaction requires prediction of others' behavior, using one's own motor/cognitive system to simulate the other's state. Music provides the purest case: temporal prediction in joint action, stripped of semantic content, revealing the motor prediction machinery in its most transparent form.

### 1.7 Virginia Penhune — Development and Sensitive Periods

The capacity for beat perception is not fully mature at birth — it develops over childhood, and early musical training during sensitive periods produces lasting changes (Penhune, 2011; Bailey, Zatorre, & Penhune, 2014).

**Developmental trajectory**:
- **Infancy** (0-12 months): Infants prefer rhythmic regularity and can discriminate between different meters (Hannon & Trehub, 2005). Some evidence for beat perception based on ERP studies. But behavioral entrainment (bouncing to a beat) is imprecise — motor system coupling is immature.
- **Toddlerhood** (1-3 years): Spontaneous movement to music appears, but synchronization is poor. Children move to music joyfully but NOT on the beat.
- **Childhood** (4-7 years): Beat synchronization ability emerges gradually. By age 7-8, most children can tap to a simple metronome with reasonable accuracy.
- **Late childhood/adolescence** (8-15 years): Complex meter processing, syncopation perception, and groove sensitivity develop. The BG-motor-auditory network matures.

**Sensitive periods**: Early musical training (before age 7) produces changes in auditory-motor structural connectivity (white matter in the corpus callosum and corticospinal tract) that do not occur when the same amount of training begins after age 7 (Bailey et al., 2014). The brain's capacity to forge auditory-motor connections has a window of enhanced plasticity — analogous to the critical period for language acquisition.

**The heritability component**: Twin studies suggest that beat perception ability is ~40-60% heritable (Seesjärvi et al., 2016). There is genetic variation in the precision of the oscillatory timing mechanism. This does not mean beat perception is "innate" — environment (musical exposure) is essential — but it does mean that some brains are constitutionally better tuned for rhythmic prediction than others.

### 1.8 Joyce Chen — SMA: The Internal Metronome

I want to close with the specific role of the supplementary motor area (SMA) in rhythm processing (Chen, Penhune, & Zatorre, 2008; Chen, Zatorre, & Penhune, 2006).

The SMA performs a function distinct from both the BG (beat extraction) and auditory cortex (temporal encoding): **SMA generates and maintains INTERNAL temporal predictions when the external stimulus is ambiguous or absent**.

The evidence:
- When listeners hear a rhythm and then it stops, SMA activity CONTINUES at the beat frequency — it "plays" the beat internally even when no sound is present (Grahn & Rowe, 2009). This internal continuation is necessary for resuming synchronization when the rhythm returns.
- SMA is more active for metrically ambiguous rhythms (where the beat could be interpreted in multiple ways) than for metrically clear rhythms — suggesting it is providing top-down disambiguation when bottom-up cues are insufficient.
- Disrupting SMA (via lesion or TMS) selectively impairs the ability to maintain an internal beat representation when the external stimulus is interrupted. The BG can still FIND the beat when it's present, but SMA is needed to KEEP it when it's absent.

**For the panel**: SMA provides the "flywheel" of the beat system. The BG extract the beat from auditory input (bottom-up). SMA maintains the beat as an internal prediction (top-down). The two work together: BG set the frequency; SMA maintains it and provides the top-down signal that the BG use for phase correction. This is exactly the top-down/bottom-up loop of the predictive coding framework (Panel M-I, Template M2), instantiated in the temporal domain.

## Part 2: Cross-Examination and Debate

### 2.1 Is Beat Perception Uniquely Motor, or Multiply Realized?

**Grahn**: I want to be precise about my claim. The BG are NECESSARY for beat perception — PD patients show deficits, and BG activation correlates with beat strength. But are they SUFFICIENT? Could a purely auditory mechanism also extract beat, with the BG providing an additional route?

**Large**: My oscillatory model doesn't require the motor system per se — auditory cortex oscillators can entrain to periodic input on their own. But in practice, the coupling between auditory and motor oscillators makes the system more precise. The motor system acts as a resonant amplifier: it receives the auditory periodic signal, entrains its own oscillators, and sends the entrained signal back to auditory cortex via the dorsal stream. This recurrent loop produces more stable, more precise entrainment than either system alone.

**Patel**: ASAP makes the stronger claim: the motor system is necessary for the specific PREDICTIVE component of beat perception. You can detect that sounds occur at regular intervals using auditory cortex alone. But to generate expectations about WHEN the next beat will occur — to predict the future — requires motor planning circuitry. The distinction is between tracking periodicity (auditory) and predicting the next event time (motor).

**Chen**: My SMA data support Patel's distinction. SMA is needed specifically when the beat must be maintained INTERNALLY — when the external signal is absent or ambiguous. If beat perception were purely auditory, there would be no need for SMA engagement during silences. The motor system provides the PREDICTION; the auditory system provides the INPUT.

**Witek**: The groove data are decisive here. If beat perception were purely auditory, there would be no reason for rhythmic complexity to produce an urge to move. The fact that groove — a fundamentally motor experience — is maximized at moderate syncopation proves that the motor system is not merely accompanying beat perception but is constitutively involved. Groove IS the motor system's response to being in its optimal prediction zone.

### 2.2 Groove, Dopamine, and the Reward Circuit

**Witek**: The inverted-U for groove parallels the inverted-U for musical pleasure from Panel M-I. Is groove pleasure? Or is it a distinct phenomenon?

**Grahn**: The BG connection suggests a dopaminergic component. The putamen and caudate are dopamine-rich structures. Beat perception activates these structures. It is plausible that successful beat prediction produces a dopamine signal — a small reward for each confirmed temporal prediction. Groove, on this account, is the subjective experience of a steady stream of dopaminergic reward from successful beat-tracking, punctuated by the enhanced reward of processing syncopated events that violate and then resolve within the metric framework.

**Patel**: This connects to the Salimpoor et al. (2011) finding from Panel M-I. Anticipatory dopamine (caudate) during the buildup to a musical peak may be partly driven by rhythmic entrainment — the motor system's temporal predictions becoming increasingly precise as the music builds toward a predictable arrival point. The "shivers of anticipation" have a rhythmic component.

**Large**: The resonance model predicts that maximal groove corresponds to a state of maximal oscillatory stability at the beat frequency combined with moderate instability at the subdivision level. Moderate syncopation perturbs subdivision-level oscillators while the beat-level oscillator remains stable. This combination — stable global prediction, unstable local prediction — maximizes the dynamic range of the system and may maximize the reward signal.

### 2.3 Cross-Cultural Beat: Universal or Learned?

**Penhune**: The developmental data suggest that basic beat perception (detecting periodicity) is very early-emerging and possibly innate. But the ability to process complex meters and culturally specific rhythmic structures is clearly learned. Where is the line?

**Patel**: The ASAP hypothesis predicts that beat perception should exist in any species with vocal learning — because vocal learning requires auditory-motor coupling. This suggests a biological foundation, not a cultural one. But the SPECIFIC meters and rhythmic patterns that a listener can process are culturally shaped. A Western listener processes 4/4 and 3/4 easily but struggles with the asymmetric meters common in Balkan music (7/8, 11/8). The reverse holds for listeners raised in those traditions. The capacity for beat perception is biological; the specific metric competencies are culturally learned.

**Large**: The oscillatory framework handles this naturally. The oscillators have natural frequency preferences (roughly in the range 0.5-4 Hz for beat perception, peaking around 2 Hz = 120 BPM). These preferences are likely biological, reflecting properties of neural populations. But the MODE-LOCKING patterns — which integer ratios are stable — are shaped by experience. A listener raised with complex Afro-Cuban rhythms develops stable mode-locking at ratios (like 3:2) that a listener raised on simple Western pop does not.

## Part 3: Templates — Rhythm, Groove, and Motor System

### Template M7: Basal Ganglia Beat Extraction

**Core claim**: The basal ganglia (particularly putamen and caudate) extract periodic temporal regularity (beat) from auditory input, automatically and obligatorily engaging the cortico-BG-thalamo-cortical motor loop even during passive listening.

**Neural substrate**: Auditory cortex → putamen/caudate (beat extraction) → SMA/premotor cortex (motor prediction) → back to auditory cortex (precision-weighted temporal prediction). This loop operates automatically when periodic structure is present; it does NOT engage for non-periodic (random or complex aperiodic) temporal patterns.

**Formal structure**:
- Input: Temporal onsets from auditory cortex
- Process: Detection of periodicity via striatal pattern matching (possibly through coincidence detection of cortical oscillatory inputs)
- Output: Beat signal = periodic motor prediction at the detected frequency
- PE: Events that deviate from predicted beat times → phase correction proportional to asynchrony

**Key parameters**:
- Preferred tempo range: ~60-150 BPM (1-2.5 Hz), peaking at ~120 BPM (2 Hz) — the "resonance frequency" of the human motor system
- Entrainment strength: Proportional to clarity of periodic structure in input
- Dopaminergic modulation: DA levels modulate BG sensitivity to periodic patterns (PD = impaired beat perception)

**Clinical application**: Rhythmic Auditory Stimulation (Thaut) compensates for BG dysfunction by providing external beat that bypasses the damaged internal beat-extraction mechanism.

**Maturity**: ★★★★ (Strong neuroimaging, patient, and TMS evidence)

**Key references**: Grahn & Brett (2007); Grahn & Rowe (2009, 2013); Thaut et al. (2015)

### Template M8: Oscillatory Entrainment and Metric Hierarchy

**Core claim**: Neural oscillators in auditory and motor cortex entrain to the periodic structure of musical rhythm at multiple hierarchically related frequencies, creating a metric hierarchy (beat, subdivision, hyperbeat) through nonlinear mode-locking.

**Neural substrate**: 
- Auditory cortex: Delta-band (1-4 Hz) oscillations entrain to beat; theta-band (4-8 Hz) to subdivisions
- Motor cortex/SMA: Beta-band (15-30 Hz) oscillations modulated by beat structure (power increase at expected beat times)
- BG: Mediates coupling between auditory and motor oscillators

**Formal structure**:
- Oscillator: dθ/dt = ω + Σ K_n · sin(n·φ_stimulus - θ) for harmonics n = 1, 2, 3...
- Mode-locking: Stable states at integer frequency ratios (1:1, 2:1, 3:1, 3:2, etc.)
- Metric hierarchy: Amplitude of mode-locked oscillations → strength of metric position (downbeat > backbeat > subdivision)
- Metric ambiguity: Multiple mode-locking solutions possible → listener "chooses" metric interpretation

**Perceptual consequence**: The oscillatory hierarchy determines which time points are "strong" and which are "weak" — this is the neural basis of meter. Events on strong metric positions (at oscillatory peaks) are more expected; events on weak positions are less expected. Syncopation = events at oscillatory troughs rather than peaks.

**Maturity**: ★★★ (Theoretically elegant with growing empirical support; full hierarchy model needs more testing)

**Key references**: Large & Palmer (2002); Large & Snyder (2009); Fujioka et al. (2012)

### Template M9: Groove as Motor Prediction Optimization

**Core claim**: Groove — the pleasurable urge to move to music — is the subjective experience of the motor prediction system operating in its optimal zone: temporal predictions are strong enough to entrain (beat is present) but rhythmic complexity produces moderate prediction errors (syncopation creates PE that is resolvable within the metric framework).

**Neural substrate**: 
- BG (beat extraction) + SMA (internal beat maintenance) + premotor cortex (motor prediction generation)
- Dopaminergic reward from successful temporal prediction
- Maximal activation of BG-motor loop at moderate syncopation
- Switch to cerebellar/prefrontal network at high syncopation (loss of beat → loss of groove)

**Formal structure**:
- Groove = f(syncopation_level), following inverted-U
- Syncopation_level = proportion of events displaced from metric strong positions
- Groove_max at syncopation ≈ 0.3-0.5 (30-50% of events syncopated)
- PE_rhythmic = Σ |phase_actual - phase_expected| across events in a pattern
- Groove ∝ PE_rhythmic when PE_rhythmic < threshold; Groove ∝ 1/PE_rhythmic when PE_rhythmic > threshold

**The Goldilocks parallel**: This is Template T2 (visual complexity preference) expressed in the temporal domain. The mechanism is identical: moderate PE → optimal processing → maximal reward. The domain changes (spatial → temporal); the principle does not.

**Maturity**: ★★★★ (Witek's inverted-U is replicated and robust; neural mechanism plausible but not fully confirmed)

**Key references**: Witek et al. (2014, 2017); Vuust & Witek (2014); Janata, Tomic, & Haberman (2012)

### Template M10: Action Simulation for Auditory Prediction (ASAP)

**Core claim**: The motor system is recruited during beat perception because motor planning provides temporally precise predictions that auditory cortex alone cannot generate. Beat perception is not auditory perception followed by motor response — it is auditory-MOTOR perception from the outset.

**Neural substrate**: 
- Dorsal auditory stream: Auditory cortex → parietal cortex → premotor cortex → SMA
- This pathway provides the anatomical basis for motor involvement in auditory temporal prediction
- SMA generates internal temporal predictions; premotor cortex generates motor plans (simulated movements at beat frequency)

**Formal structure**:
- Auditory input → BG detects periodicity → SMA generates internal beat signal → premotor cortex simulates movement at beat frequency → simulation output sent to auditory cortex as temporal prediction
- Prediction precision: Motor-generated temporal predictions are more precise than auditory-only predictions (motor system evolved for precise timing)
- The motor simulation IS the prediction; groove IS the phenomenology of the simulation

**Evolutionary constraint**: ASAP predicts beat perception requires auditory-motor coupling that evolved for vocal learning. Predicts beat perception in vocal learners (parrots ✓, songbirds — mixed) and absence in non-vocal-learners (dogs, cats — ✓; sea lions — notable exception).

**Maturity**: ★★★ (Theoretically elegant; supported by TMS, neuroimaging, and comparative data; some predictions not yet fully tested)

**Key references**: Patel & Iversen (2014); Patel (2021); Stupacher et al. (2017)

### Template M11: Auditory-Motor Developmental Plasticity

**Core claim**: The auditory-motor connectivity that supports beat perception and musical rhythm processing is shaped by a sensitive period in early childhood (~before age 7), during which musical training produces lasting structural changes in white matter connectivity that do not occur with equivalent training begun later.

**Neural substrate**: 
- Corpus callosum (interhemispheric auditory-motor coordination)
- Corticospinal tract (motor output timing)
- Arcuate fasciculus (auditory-frontal connectivity)
- Sensitive period plasticity: Enhanced myelination and axonal pruning during early childhood in these tracts

**Formal structure**:
- Connectivity_adult = f(genetic_baseline, early_training, late_training)
- Early training (< age 7) produces permanent connectivity enhancement
- Late training (> age 7) produces functional improvement but less structural change
- Genetic variation contributes ~40-60% of variance in beat perception ability (heritability)

**Educational parallel**: This is the developmental trajectory theme from the education panel (Panel E-I-E-VI planned). Brain systems mature at different rates; interventions are most effective during sensitive periods. For music: early rhythm exposure during the auditory-motor sensitive period. For education: early cognitive enrichment during the hippocampal-prefrontal maturation period.

**Maturity**: ★★★ (Longitudinal training studies and twin studies provide moderate evidence; sensitive period boundaries imprecise)

## Part 4: Panel Synthesis — How Rhythm Engages the Body

### The Unified Account

The eight panelists converge on an account of rhythm processing that fundamentally challenges the traditional separation of perception and action:

**Beat perception is an auditory-motor computation** (Grahn, Patel, Chen). The basal ganglia extract periodicity from auditory input, the SMA maintains the beat internally, and the premotor cortex generates motor predictions at the beat frequency. This motor loop engages automatically when periodic structure is present in the auditory input. Beat perception is not "hearing a beat" — it is "hearing and motorically predicting a beat" simultaneously.

**The mechanism is oscillatory entrainment** (Large). Neural oscillators in auditory and motor cortex entrain to the periodic structure of rhythm, creating a hierarchy of mode-locked frequencies that constitute the metric framework. Entrainment provides both temporal prediction (phase-based expectation of when events will occur) and the basis for groove (the motor system's active engagement with the rhythmic prediction).

**Groove is the motor Goldilocks zone** (Witek). Moderate syncopation — enough rhythmic prediction error to engage the motor prediction system without overwhelming it — produces maximal groove. The inverted-U function for groove mirrors the inverted-U functions for visual complexity preference (architecture), desirable difficulty (education), and harmonic surprise (music pitch, Panel M-I). The Goldilocks principle is confirmed for a fourth domain.

**The auditory-motor coupling is deep, phylogenetically old, and clinically exploitable** (Thaut). The coupling is robust enough to compensate for damaged motor timing in neurological patients, and it develops early enough to have a sensitive period in childhood (Penhune). This is not a cognitive luxury — it is a fundamental neural mechanism.

**Social music-making extends prediction to interpersonal coordination** (Keller). When musicians play together, each performer's motor prediction system models the other performers' timing, creating a multi-brain prediction network. This connects rhythm to the social brain themes of the architectural and educational panels.

### 5 Testable Predictions

1. **Dopamine release in BG will correlate with groove ratings**: PET or pharmacological studies should show that moderate-syncopation rhythms produce greater dopamine release in the putamen than either low- or high-syncopation rhythms, paralleling the behavioral inverted-U. *Status: Not yet directly tested; PD medication studies provide indirect support.*

2. **SMA disruption will selectively impair beat maintenance, not beat detection**: TMS to SMA during a rhythm continuation task (listen to a beat, then tap during silence) should impair continuation tapping more than synchronized tapping. BG disruption (as in PD) should impair both. *Status: Partially supported by existing lesion data; specific TMS protocol needed.*

3. **Auditory-motor structural connectivity will predict groove sensitivity**: DTI measures of white matter integrity in the dorsal auditory stream (auditory cortex → premotor cortex) should predict individual differences in groove ratings and the shape of the syncopation inverted-U. *Status: Predicted; not yet tested.*

4. **Cross-cultural meter preferences will reflect statistical exposure**: Listeners raised in cultures with predominant 4/4 meter should show strongest groove for syncopation within 4/4, while listeners raised with complex meters (e.g., 7/8 in Balkan music) should show groove for syncopation within those meters. The Goldilocks zone should be culture-specific. *Status: Hannon & Trehub (2005) show infant sensitivity to culture-specific meter; adult groove version not yet tested.*

5. **Oscillatory phase at event onset will predict groove response trial-by-trial**: Single-trial EEG analysis should show that events occurring at the trough of beta oscillations (maximal prediction — "expected beat time") produce the strongest groove response, while events at off-peak phases produce weaker groove. The relationship should follow an inverted-U as a function of the phase deviation from the expected peak. *Status: Technically feasible with current EEG methods; not yet conducted.*
