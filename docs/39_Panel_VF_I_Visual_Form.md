## Panel Charge

This panel addresses the remaining gaps in **A6 Visual Pattern & Form** (coverage ★★★ Moderate in Doc 33). The existing templates T1 (fractal fluency / spectral match) and T2 (complexity Goldilocks zone) provide strong coverage of visual complexity and fractal dimension. What is NOT formalized: (1) the curvature-rectilinearity preference (Vartanian et al., 2013, ~500 citations; Bar & Neta, 2006, ~600 citations), (2) the visual rhythm analogy to musical rhythm (column spacing, arch sequences, window repetition as visual temporal structure), (3) the Meyers-Levy ceiling height processing-style effect (high ceilings → abstract processing, low ceilings → concrete processing), and (4) the contour-prediction mechanism that may underlie the curvature preference.

This is a focused mini-panel. The existing templates handle complexity and fractal structure well; this panel targets the SHAPE and CONTOUR dimensions that T1 and T2 do not cover. The panel is charged with producing 2–3 templates that formalize: (a) how contour geometry (curved vs. angular) engages threat assessment and approach-avoidance systems, (b) how visual architectural rhythm creates temporal prediction during visual scanning analogous to auditory rhythm, and (c) how spatial proportions (ceiling height, room shape) modulate cognitive processing style through spatial prediction.

## Panel Composition

### Neuroaesthetics and Visual Form Processing

**1. Oshin Vartanian** (University of Toronto, Department of Psychology; Defence Research and Development Canada)
Author of the landmark fMRI study demonstrating differential neural responses to curvilinear vs. rectilinear architectural spaces: curvilinear spaces activated the anterior cingulate cortex (approach/reward) more than rectilinear spaces, and beauty judgments for curvilinear spaces were higher (Vartanian et al., 2013, ~500 citations; Vartanian et al., 2015, ~200 citations). Also showed that perceived enclosure activated the amygdala, connecting spatial geometry to threat processing. His work bridges neuroscience and architectural aesthetics directly.
*Role*: Establishes the neural evidence for curvature preference in architectural contexts. Provides the approach-avoidance neural circuitry (ACC vs. amygdala) that contour geometry activates. The key question for this panel: is the curvature preference about contour prediction (smooth curves are predictable, angles are discontinuous) or about threat assessment (sharp angles signal danger, curves signal safety)?

**2. Moshe Bar** (Bar-Ilan University, Gonda Multidisciplinary Brain Research Center; formerly Harvard/MGH)
Research on visual prediction and object recognition. His seminal finding: the brain generates rapid predictions about visual objects based on coarse, low-spatial-frequency information processed through a fast magnocellular pathway from early visual cortex to prefrontal cortex (Bar, 2004, ~1,500 citations). His specific finding on contour curvature: humans prefer curved visual objects across categories, and sharp angular contours activate the amygdala more than curved contours (Bar & Neta, 2006, ~600 citations; Bar & Neta, 2007, ~400 citations). This preference is evident within 100ms of stimulus onset, suggesting a pre-attentive, automatic evaluation.
*Role*: Provides the mechanistic account of WHY curves are preferred: the visual prediction pathway processes curved contours more fluently (smooth gradients allow continuous prediction) while angular contours produce prediction discontinuities (the contour changes direction abruptly at each angle, violating the local curvature prediction). This is a PE account: curved = low contour PE = processing fluency = positive valence; angular = high contour PE at vertices = processing effort = threat-related negative valence.

**3. Anjan Chatterjee** (University of Pennsylvania, Center for Cognitive Neuroscience; author, *The Aesthetic Brain*)
Neuroscientist who developed the framework for neuroaesthetics — the study of how the brain processes beauty, art, and aesthetic experience (Chatterjee, 2011, ~700 citations; Chatterjee & Vartanian, 2014, ~500 citations). His "aesthetic triad" model proposes that aesthetic experience arises from the interaction of sensory-motor, emotion-valuation, and knowledge-meaning neural systems. Has applied this framework to architecture, identifying contour, complexity, and spatial configuration as the primary visual dimensions of architectural aesthetics.
*Role*: Provides the broader neuroaesthetic framework within which the specific contour-preference and visual-rhythm templates sit. His aesthetic triad ensures the templates do not reduce architectural beauty to a single mechanism — sensory processing (contour fluency), emotional evaluation (approach-avoidance), and semantic meaning (cultural associations of form) all contribute.

**4. Joan Meyers-Levy** (University of Minnesota, Carlson School of Management)
Author of the ceiling height effect: high ceilings activate "relational" (abstract, creative, freedom-associated) processing, while low ceilings activate "item-specific" (concrete, detail-oriented, confinement-associated) processing (Meyers-Levy & Zhu, 2007, ~1,200 citations). This finding has been replicated and debated, with some studies finding weaker or context-dependent effects. The mechanism she proposed — freedom-confinement priming — has not been connected to the PE framework, though Doc 33 (A2 section) sketched a PE account: high ceilings = wide spatial prediction → abstract processing mode; low ceilings = narrow spatial prediction → precise/constrained processing mode.
*Role*: Provides the spatial-proportions evidence and the processing-style modulation claim. The panel must evaluate whether the ceiling height effect is better explained by freedom-confinement priming (Meyers-Levy's account), spatial PE range (Doc 33's sketch), or approach-avoidance activation (Vartanian's enclosure-amygdala finding). These may not be competing explanations — they may be different descriptions of the same mechanism at different levels.

### Visual Rhythm and Temporal Pattern

**5. Nikos Salingaros** (University of Texas at San Antonio, Department of Mathematics)
Mathematician and architectural theorist who has developed a quantitative approach to architectural pattern, connecting mathematical concepts (fractal geometry, symmetry groups, scaling hierarchies, information theory) to architectural beauty and habitability (Salingaros, 2005, ~400 citations; Salingaros, 2006, ~200 citations). His "life in architecture" thesis holds that buildings with adequate sub-symmetries, scaling hierarchies (detail at multiple size scales), and color harmony promote human wellbeing, while buildings that strip these away (minimalist modernism) produce sensory deprivation.
*Role*: Provides the mathematical framework for visual rhythm and scaling hierarchies in architecture. His concept of "scaling coherence" — that a building should have recognizable structure at every scale from the city block down to the door handle — maps onto the fractal fluency mechanism (T1) but extends it from spectral statistics to spatial-hierarchical structure. Visual rhythm (column spacing, window repetition, ornamental pattern) is a subset of his scaling framework.

**6. Richard Taylor** (University of Oregon, Department of Physics; Materials Science Institute)
Physicist who quantified the fractal structure of Jackson Pollock's drip paintings and demonstrated that humans prefer images with fractal dimension D ≈ 1.3 across a wide range of stimuli (Taylor, Micolich, & Jonas, 2001, ~1,000 citations; Spehar, Clifford, Newell, & Taylor, 2003, ~300 citations). His recent work extends fractal fluency to architectural applications — building facades, streetscapes, and interior surfaces — and provides the psychophysical methodology for measuring the fractal dimension of architectural scenes.
*Role*: Provides the quantitative methodology for measuring visual complexity and fractal structure in architecture. His D ≈ 1.3 optimum anchors T1 empirically. For this panel, he extends the fractal framework to include visual rhythm: periodic structures (column series, arcade sequences) have characteristic spatial frequencies that can be analyzed alongside fractal statistics to produce a comprehensive visual-complexity profile.

### Architecture Theory and Visual Composition

**7. Peter Eisenman** (returns from Panel SC-I)
Returns specifically to address the curvature-rectilinearity debate from the DESIGN perspective. His work is resolutely angular — non-orthogonal grids, sharp geometries, vertices that challenge the body's expectation of smooth contour. If curved forms are neurally preferred (Bar, Vartanian), why do Eisenman's angular buildings produce profound aesthetic and intellectual responses? The answer must involve PE: angular architecture produces HIGHER visual PE that, in the right context, produces intellectual engagement rather than threat.
*Role*: Provides the counter-case to the curvature preference, as he did for the Goldilocks model in SC-I. Ensures the templates do not oversimplify: if the curvature preference were absolute, angular architecture could never be beautiful or intellectually powerful. The template must accommodate intentional contour PE.

**8. Zaha Hadid Architects / Patrik Schumacher** (Zaha Hadid Architects, London)
Representing the parametric design movement — architecture that uses computational algorithms to generate complex curved forms that exceed what traditional drafting can produce. Hadid's buildings (Heydar Aliyev Center, MAXXI Museum, Guangzhou Opera House) exemplify extreme curvature — flowing, continuous surfaces with no visible angles, producing spatial experiences that are simultaneously novel (high PE: you have never seen a space shaped like this) and fluid (low contour PE: every edge is a smooth gradient). The parametric approach produces buildings that are the MOST curved in architectural history.
*Role*: Provides the design-practice case for extreme curvature. If Bar and Vartanian are right that curves activate approach/reward, parametric architecture should be the most neurally rewarding built form ever produced. Is it? The answer is more nuanced: extreme novelty (you have never been in a space where ALL surfaces curve) may produce high spatial PE that competes with the low contour PE. The template must address the interaction between contour prediction (local: smooth curves are predictable) and spatial prediction (global: novel spatial volumes are unpredictable).

**9. Stephen Palmer** (University of California, Berkeley, Department of Psychology)
Developer of the "ecological valence theory" of color preference — the proposal that color preferences are driven by the valence of objects associated with those colors (Palmer & Schloss, 2010, ~800 citations). Blue is preferred because sky and water are positive; brown-yellow is disliked because biological waste is negative. The theory provides an evolutionary and associative account of color that is more mechanistically specific than "conditioned association."
*Role*: Provides the color-preference framework. Doc 33 rated color as weakly addressed (mostly conditioned associations). Palmer's ecological valence theory adds mechanistic depth: color preferences reflect the statistical association between chromatic signals and survival-relevant objects in the ancestral environment. For architecture, this predicts that building colors that match positive ecological associations (sky blue, vegetation green, earth tones) are preferred over colors that match negative associations (waste brown-yellow, poisonous saturated reds/yellows in unnatural combinations).

## Position Statements

### CONTOUR PREDICTION AND THE CURVATURE PREFERENCE

**Position Statement 1: Moshe Bar — Curved Contours Are Predictable Contours**

The visual system generates predictions about contour continuation. When you see the beginning of a line, the visual system predicts where it will go next — this is the basis of Gestalt "good continuation" (Wertheimer, 1923). For a curved contour, the prediction is smooth: each point on the curve predicts the next point with low error because the curvature changes gradually. For an angular contour, the prediction fails at each vertex: the line was going in one direction, and suddenly it goes in another. The vertex is a contour prediction error — a point where the visual prediction about contour continuation is maximally violated.

My finding that the amygdala activates more for angular objects than for curved objects (Bar & Neta, 2006, ~600 citations) has two possible interpretations. The **threat interpretation**: angular forms resemble sharp, dangerous objects (thorns, teeth, weapons), and the amygdala responds because it detects potential threat. The **prediction-error interpretation**: angular contours produce more contour PE at vertices, and the amygdala responds to the prediction error itself, which the brain treats as requiring urgent evaluation (because in natural environments, prediction errors often signal danger). These interpretations are not mutually exclusive — the amygdala may respond to both the specific threat association (sharp = dangerous) and the general prediction error (unexpected contour = requires evaluation).

The architectural relevance: buildings with predominantly curved contours (Hadid, Gehry, Aalto) produce lower visual contour PE and activate approach circuits. Buildings with sharp angles and vertices (Eisenman, Libeskind, Brutalist concrete) produce higher contour PE and activate evaluation/avoidance circuits. But this is at the LOCAL contour level. At the GLOBAL spatial level, the prediction is different: a familiar rectangular room has low spatial PE because you have experienced thousands of rectangular rooms. A novel curved room (Hadid's Heydar Aliyev Center) has HIGH spatial PE because you have never been in a space shaped like this. The total visual PE is the combination of local contour PE (low for curves) and global spatial PE (potentially high for novel curved spaces). This is why extreme parametric architecture can feel simultaneously fluid (the contours flow) and disorienting (the space is unprecedented).

**Position Statement 2: Oshin Vartanian — Architecture-Specific Neural Evidence**

The fMRI evidence is specific to architectural spaces, not just to abstract shapes. When participants viewed photographs of curvilinear architectural interiors vs. rectilinear interiors matched for complexity, brightness, and color: (a) curvilinear spaces were rated as significantly more beautiful, (b) curvilinear spaces activated the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) more strongly — the ACC is associated with approach motivation and reward anticipation, (c) beauty judgments for curvilinear spaces correlated with ACC activation, (d) enclosure (regardless of curvature) activated the amygdala — consistent with T5 threat detection for confined spaces (Vartanian et al., 2013, ~500 citations).

Importantly, the curvature preference was NOT modulated by expertise — architects and non-architects showed the same ACC activation pattern for curves. This suggests the preference is not learned but is a basic property of visual form processing. However, BEAUTY JUDGMENTS differed: architects were more likely to rate rectilinear spaces as beautiful (perhaps because their training teaches appreciation of angular design), while non-architects showed a stronger curvature-beauty correlation. The neural preference (ACC activation) was the same; the aesthetic judgment (conscious beauty rating) was modulated by expertise. This dissociation between implicit neural response and explicit aesthetic judgment is important for the template: the mechanism is automatic and pre-attentive, but the conscious evaluation is modulated by training and cultural context.

In PE terms: the ACC activation for curves may reflect low contour PE → processing fluency → reward signal (the Reber/Schwarz fluency-as-pleasure pathway). The amygdala activation for enclosure may reflect high spatial PE → threat evaluation → avoidance signal. These are independent dimensions: a curvilinear enclosed space (Hadid's parametric interiors) would produce low contour PE (ACC reward) AND high enclosure threat (amygdala evaluation) simultaneously — competing signals that could produce the characteristic ambiguity of such spaces (beautiful but claustrophobic).

**Position Statement 3: Anjan Chatterjee — The Aesthetic Triad and Architectural Form**

The curvature preference is real but it is not the whole story of architectural visual aesthetics. The aesthetic triad model (Chatterjee & Vartanian, 2014, ~500 citations) identifies three interacting neural systems:

**Sensory-motor**: How the form is processed by the visual and motor systems. Contour fluency (curves vs. angles), complexity (T2 Goldilocks), fractal structure (T1), and embodied simulation (the motor system silently simulates interacting with the space — running a hand along a curved wall, navigating around an angular obstacle) all contribute. This is where the contour PE mechanism operates.

**Emotion-valuation**: How the form is evaluated for biological significance. Approach-avoidance (ACC vs. amygdala), threat detection (T5 for sharp angles and enclosure), and reward anticipation (the "inviting" quality of some spaces). This is where the curvature preference has its affective component.

**Knowledge-meaning**: How the form is interpreted through learned associations, cultural context, and semantic knowledge. A Gothic cathedral is beautiful partly because of its visual complexity (sensory-motor) and partly because the viewer knows it is a CATHEDRAL — a sacred space with a thousand years of cultural meaning. A Brutalist concrete building may be beautiful to an architecture student who understands its theoretical commitments and visually aversive to a passerby who sees only raw concrete. This is where MAT5 (material-cultural conditioning) and Eisenman's intellectual-engagement case connect.

The template must not reduce architectural visual aesthetics to contour PE alone. Contour PE is the sensory-motor component; the emotion-valuation and knowledge-meaning components interact with it to produce the full aesthetic response.

**Position Statement 4: Joan Meyers-Levy — Ceiling Height and Processing Mode**

The ceiling height effect demonstrates that spatial proportions do not merely produce aesthetic responses — they modulate COGNITIVE PROCESSING STYLE (Meyers-Levy & Zhu, 2007, ~1,200 citations). Participants in a high-ceilinged room (10 ft / 3m) generated more abstract, relational category groupings and more creative associations than participants in a low-ceilinged room (8 ft / 2.4m), who generated more concrete, item-specific groupings and more detail-oriented associations. The effect was mediated by concepts of freedom (high ceiling) and confinement (low ceiling), activated implicitly by the spatial proportions.

The PE reinterpretation sketched in Doc 33 (A2 section) is compelling: high ceilings create a large spatial prediction volume — many things are possible in a tall, expansive space, so the prediction is broad and unconstrained, which maps to abstract, relational cognition. Low ceilings create a small spatial prediction volume — fewer things are possible in a constrained space, so the prediction is narrow and specific, which maps to concrete, detail-oriented cognition. This is not a metaphor; it is a computational prediction: if the brain allocates prediction resources proportional to the prediction volume, then larger volumes engage broader, more relational prediction modes and smaller volumes engage narrower, more focused modes.

But I want to note the limitations: the effect has not replicated consistently at smaller ceiling differences (the original study used a 2-foot difference, which is substantial). Some replications find the effect only for specific tasks or specific populations. And the mechanism may be simpler than either freedom-priming or spatial-PE: high ceilings may simply produce positive affect (through prospect/openness), and positive affect is known to broaden cognitive scope (Fredrickson's broaden-and-build theory, ~3,000 citations). The panel should evaluate whether spatial PE, freedom priming, or positive affect is the most parsimonious mechanistic account.

**Position Statement 5: Nikos Salingaros — Scaling Hierarchies and Visual Nourishment**

Architecture provides visual nourishment or visual starvation. The visual system evolved to process natural environments, which exhibit structure at every spatial scale: the overall landscape, the tree canopy, the branch structure, the leaf venation, the cellular texture. Each scale provides information that the visual system processes in parallel, engaging fractal processing (T1) and complexity assessment (T2) at multiple resolutions simultaneously. This multi-scale engagement is what I call "visual nourishment" — the visual system has adequate computational work at every scale, producing sustained, pleasurable processing (Salingaros, 2005, ~400 citations; Salingaros, 2006, ~200 citations).

Traditional architecture provided visual nourishment through **scaling hierarchies**: the building has structure at the urban scale (overall massing), the facade scale (window rhythms, horizontal bands, cornices), the intermediate scale (column capitals, arch moldings, door surrounds), and the detail scale (carved ornament, material grain, hardware). Modernist architecture systematically removed the intermediate and detail scales — glass curtain walls have structure at the building scale and at the glass-panel scale, with NOTHING in between. The result is visual starvation at the intermediate scales: the visual system seeks information at those frequencies and finds none.

Visual rhythm — the repetition of elements like columns, windows, or arches at regular intervals — is one component of the scaling hierarchy. A colonnade provides: (a) a global rhythm (the regular column spacing), (b) local detail (each column's capital, base, and fluting), and (c) a fractal relationship between global and local structure (the capital's scrollwork echoes the column spacing at a smaller scale). This multi-scale rhythm engages the visual prediction system at multiple frequencies simultaneously — the brain predicts the next column, the next capital scroll, and the next fluting groove, all in parallel. The PE is moderate at each scale (the prediction is mostly confirmed but with sufficient variation to sustain interest), and the multi-scale engagement is the visual analog of the multi-modal convergence principle (L3, MAT4).

**Position Statement 6: Richard Taylor — Quantifying Visual Complexity in Architecture**

The fractal dimension D ≈ 1.3 optimum that we established for abstract patterns (Taylor et al., 2001, ~1,000 citations) and natural scenes (Spehar et al., 2003, ~300 citations) extends to architecture. We have measured the fractal dimension of building facades, streetscapes, and interior surfaces and found that preferred architectural scenes cluster near D ≈ 1.3–1.5, with aversive scenes falling below D ≈ 1.1 (too simple) or above D ≈ 1.8 (too complex) (Taylor et al., 2005, ~200 citations; Hagerhall, Purcell, & Taylor, 2004, ~400 citations).

The visual rhythm adds a dimension that fractal analysis alone does not capture: periodicity. A fractal pattern is self-similar across scales but does not necessarily have a regular spatial frequency. A colonnade has both fractal structure (the columns have detail at multiple scales) and periodic structure (the columns are regularly spaced). These are independent properties: you can have fractal-but-aperiodic (a random forest) or periodic-but-non-fractal (a row of identical smooth cylinders). Architecture typically combines both — fractal surfaces (natural materials, ornamental detail) with periodic structure (column spacing, window grids, cornice lines) — and the visual system processes them through different but complementary mechanisms.

The prediction-error framework captures both: fractal dimension determines the MAGNITUDE of visual PE at each scale (D ≈ 1.3 produces moderate PE, neither too predictable nor too unpredictable). Periodicity determines the TEMPORAL STRUCTURE of visual PE during scanning (a regular column spacing produces a rhythmic PE pattern as the eye scans across the facade, with prediction confirmed at each column and a small PE at the inter-column space). Together, they define the visual prediction SCORE for a facade or interior — the spatiotemporal pattern of prediction and confirmation that the visual system experiences during sustained viewing.

**Position Statement 7: Peter Eisenman — Angular Architecture as Intellectual Engagement**

I return to this panel to make the same point I made in SC-I: if curved forms are neurally preferred (Bar, Vartanian), my buildings should be neurally aversive. But they are not. The Wexner Center, the House series, the Memorial to the Murdered Jews of Europe — these are angular, non-orthogonal, deliberately contour-disruptive, and they produce aesthetic and intellectual responses that curved buildings rarely match.

The resolution is that architectural beauty is not contour fluency. Contour fluency is one input — the sensory-motor component of Chatterjee's triad. But the knowledge-meaning component can override the sensory-motor preference: when the viewer understands that the angles have MEANING (the rotated grid encodes the historical street pattern, the non-orthogonal walls challenge the assumption of normative space, the sharp vertices produce the anxiety that the memorial intends), the high contour PE becomes productive rather than aversive. The viewer experiences the PE not as threat but as intellectual challenge — a demand to accommodate new spatial-conceptual schema, similar to but distinct from AX3 awe.

This means the curvature preference template must include a knowledge-meaning modifier: for naive viewers, contour PE tracks affect inversely (more PE → less pleasure). For expert or contextually informed viewers, the relationship becomes non-monotonic (high PE can produce pleasure THROUGH intellectual engagement, IF the PE is meaningful rather than arbitrary). This parallels the agency modifier in SC-I's Debate 3: high spatial PE + agency + purpose = productive; high spatial PE + no agency + no purpose = stressful.

**Position Statement 8: Patrik Schumacher / Zaha Hadid Architects — Parametric Curvature in Practice**

Zaha Hadid's architecture represents the most sustained exploration of curvature in the history of the built environment. The Heydar Aliyev Center in Baku is a building where not a single surface is planar — the roof flows into the walls, the walls flow into the floor, and the entire envelope is a continuous undulating surface. The interior spaces are bounded by curves in all three dimensions.

The experiential reports from occupants and visitors are instructive: the dominant experience is not simply "beauty" but a quality we might call FLOW — a sense that the space is continuous, that boundaries dissolve, that rigid categories (wall/ceiling, inside/outside, structure/skin) no longer apply. This flow experience is consistent with low contour PE (every transition is gradual, every surface is smooth) combined with high CATEGORICAL PE (the normal categories of architectural elements — wall, floor, ceiling, column — do not apply). The visitor's visual prediction system finds the contours easy to track but the object-recognition system finds the categories difficult to assign.

This suggests a dissociation that the template should capture: contour PE (local, processed by early visual cortex contour-continuation mechanisms) and categorical PE (global, processed by object-recognition systems in inferotemporal cortex). Curved architecture reduces contour PE while potentially increasing categorical PE. Angular architecture increases contour PE while preserving categorical clarity (walls are obviously walls, corners are obviously corners). The total visual PE — and the aesthetic response — is the combination of both.

**Position Statement 9: Stephen Palmer — Color in Architecture: Ecological Valence**

Architectural color preferences follow the ecological valence theory: people prefer colors that are associated with positive ecological objects and dislike colors associated with negative objects (Palmer & Schloss, 2010, ~800 citations). Blue (sky, water) and green (vegetation, healthy growth) are universally preferred. Brown-yellow (waste, decay) and dark desaturated tones (contaminated water, rotting material) are universally disliked. Red and yellow are contextually variable (fruit and flowers = positive; blood, fire, poison = negative).

For architecture, this predicts that building colors work through the same ecological-association pathway: buildings with sky-blue, vegetation-green, earth-brown, and stone-grey tones activate positive ecological associations. Buildings with artificial-yellow, industrial-grey, or unnatural-saturated tones activate negative or absent ecological associations. This is mechanistically shallow — it operates through conditioned association (MAT5's mechanism, not a deep computational principle) — but it is empirically robust and cross-culturally consistent.

The interaction with materials (MAT3, MAT4) is important: a material's COLOR is one component of its multi-modal identity. Natural wood is warm-brown; natural stone is grey or earth-toned; vegetation is green. These colors contribute to the natural-material convergence effect (MAT4) through the visual channel: the color confirms the material identity prediction (wood should be brown, stone should be grey), and deviations (painted wood, dyed concrete) produce cross-modal PE because the color violates the material-identity prediction. This is a visual-material interaction, not a purely visual phenomenon.

## Cross-Examination and Debate

### Debate 1: Is the Curvature Preference Contour PE or Threat Association?

**Challenge**: Bar proposes that curves are preferred because they produce low contour PE (processing fluency). But an alternative account is that angular forms are disliked because they resemble sharp, dangerous objects (threat association, T5). These predict the same behavioral outcome (curves preferred) through different mechanisms. Can they be distinguished?

**Bar**: The contour PE account predicts that the preference should scale CONTINUOUSLY with contour curvature — the sharper the angle, the greater the PE, the greater the amygdala response. The threat association account predicts a CATEGORICAL distinction — sharp objects (below some angle threshold) activate threat; rounded objects do not. The data support the continuous account: amygdala activation increases gradually as contour angles become sharper, without a threshold (Bar & Neta, 2007). This is more consistent with PE (continuous prediction error) than with threat (categorical classification).

**Vartanian**: The architecture data add another test. Enclosed curvilinear spaces (like a vaulted ceiling pressing down) activate the amygdala for ENCLOSURE, not for contour. If the threat account were the whole story, curvilinear enclosure should not activate the amygdala because curves do not resemble sharp objects. But it does, because enclosure triggers the spatial-confinement threat (T5) independently of contour shape. This means there are TWO independent dimensions: contour geometry (curved vs. angular → contour PE → fluency/threat) and spatial enclosure (open vs. enclosed → T5 threat assessment). They can combine in any combination: curved-open (most pleasant), curved-enclosed (pleasant contour, threatening space), angular-open (challenging contour, safe space), angular-enclosed (most threatening).

**Panel consensus**: **Both mechanisms contribute, but contour PE is the more fundamental.** Threat association explains the extreme case (very sharp angles resemble weapons); contour PE explains the full continuum (every vertex is a prediction error, and the preference scales with vertex sharpness). The template should include contour PE as the primary mechanism and threat association as a supplementary mechanism activated at extreme angles. Spatial enclosure (T5) is a separate dimension that interacts with contour geometry but is not part of the contour mechanism.

### Debate 2: Is the Ceiling Height Effect Spatial PE, Freedom Priming, or Positive Affect?

**Challenge**: Three possible mechanisms for Meyers-Levy's ceiling height effect. Which is correct?

**Meyers-Levy**: Our data showed that priming "freedom" vs. "confinement" concepts mediated the ceiling height effect — high ceilings activated freedom-related concepts, which promoted relational processing. This is a semantic priming account, not a spatial PE account.

**Vartanian**: But freedom-confinement priming may itself be downstream of the spatial-affective response. High ceilings → low enclosure → reduced amygdala activation → reduced threat → the cognitive system interprets this as "freedom." Low ceilings → high enclosure → elevated amygdala → threat → interpreted as "confinement." The freedom-confinement concepts are not the mechanism — they are how the conscious mind labels the pre-attentive spatial-affective response.

**Chatterjee**: The positive-affect broadening account (Fredrickson) predicts the same outcome without requiring spatial PE: high ceilings → positive affect (through prospect/openness) → broadened cognitive scope → relational processing. Low ceilings → neutral or negative affect → narrowed cognitive scope → item-specific processing. This is more parsimonious because positive-affect broadening is well-established and does not require a new mechanism.

**Bar**: The spatial PE account adds a specific prediction that the other accounts do not: the processing-style effect should depend on the DISCREPANCY between the current ceiling height and the ceiling height PREDICTION (formed by recent experience). Moving from a low ceiling to a high ceiling should produce a LARGER processing-style shift than simply being in a high-ceilinged room, because the PE is larger when the prediction is violated than when it is simply present. Neither freedom priming nor positive-affect broadening predicts this transition-dependent effect.

**Panel consensus**: **All three accounts are partially correct and describe different levels of the same mechanism.** Level 1 (neural): spatial proportions activate approach-avoidance circuits (Vartanian), modulating threat/safety assessment. Level 2 (affective): reduced threat → positive affect, which broadens cognitive scope (Fredrickson). Level 3 (conceptual): the broadened/narrowed state is labeled as "freedom" / "confinement" by the semantic system (Meyers-Levy). The PE addition (Bar) adds the prediction that TRANSITIONS between ceiling heights should produce larger effects than static conditions, which is testable and distinguishes the PE account from the other two. The template should capture the multi-level mechanism and flag the transition prediction as a critical test.

## Panel Synthesis

### The Three Dimensions of Architectural Visual Experience

**Chatterjee**: This panel reveals that visual form in architecture operates along three largely independent dimensions, each with its own mechanism:

**Dimension 1: Complexity (T1 + T2 + VF2)** — How much visual information is there? Fractal dimension (T1), overall scene complexity (T2), and multi-scale periodic structure (VF2) jointly determine whether the visual prediction system has too little (boring), adequate (engaging), or too much (overwhelming) computational work. The Goldilocks principle operates at this level.

**Dimension 2: Contour geometry (VF1)** — What shape does the architecture take? Curved contours produce low contour PE → approach/fluency. Angular contours produce high contour PE → evaluation/challenge. This operates independently of complexity: a building can be complex-and-curved (Gaudi), complex-and-angular (Eisenman), simple-and-curved (Ando), or simple-and-angular (Mies). The contour dimension adds an approach-avoidance signal that the complexity dimension does not capture.

**Dimension 3: Spatial proportions (VF3 + SC2)** — How does the space feel to be IN? Ceiling height, enclosure, and room geometry modulate threat assessment, affect, and cognitive processing style. This operates at the embodied, spatial-experience level rather than the visual-surface level.

**Taylor**: The T1 + VF2 combination is worth emphasizing. Before this panel, the project had T1 (fractal statistics) and T2 (overall complexity) for visual prediction. VF2 adds the PERIODIC component that T1 does not capture. Together, T1 + VF2 describe the full spatial-frequency content of an architectural visual scene: T1 for the 1/f aperiodic component (self-similar structure across scales), VF2 for the periodic component (repeating elements at specific scales). This is directly analogous to spectral analysis in acoustics: T1 is the noise spectrum (broadband), VF2 is the harmonic spectrum (discrete frequencies). Architecture, like music, is a combination of both.

**Salingaros**: And the scaling hierarchy principle shows WHY both are needed: a building with 1/f statistics but no periodicity (a random fractal surface) looks "natural" but not "architectural" — it has T1 engagement but no VF2 rhythm. A building with periodicity but no fractal detail (a row of smooth identical cylinders) has VF2 rhythm but no T1 engagement — it looks "mechanical" and "sterile." Great architecture provides BOTH: fractal detail at every scale (visual nourishment at all frequencies) AND periodic structure at intermediate scales (visual rhythm that organizes the detail). This is the visual equivalent of the multi-modal convergence principle: multiple visual mechanisms confirming simultaneously that this is a rich, organized, rewarding visual environment.

### Where the Evidence Is Strong, Where It Is Contested

**Bar**: VF1 (contour PE) is well-supported. The curvature preference is replicated across labs, cultures, and stimulus types. The amygdala response to angular contours is reliable. The architecture-specific fMRI evidence (Vartanian et al., 2013) confirms the effect for buildings specifically. The PE interpretation adds a mechanistic account (contour prediction at vertices) that the data support.

**Meyers-Levy**: VF3 (spatial proportions → processing style) is supported but with replication concerns. The original ceiling height study has been cited 1,200 times and has been partially replicated, but some replications find weaker or context-dependent effects. The multi-level mechanistic account (enclosure → affect → cognitive broadening → processing style) is plausible and connects to well-established theories (Fredrickson's broaden-and-build), but the full causal chain has not been tested in a single experiment. The PE addition (transition-enhancement prediction) is entirely untested.

**Salingaros**: VF2 (visual rhythm and scaling hierarchy) is the weakest template. The mathematical framework is clear (scaling hierarchies, periodic structure) and the architectural argument is compelling, but the EMPIRICAL evidence specific to architectural visual rhythm is thin. The analogy to musical rhythm is theoretically motivated but experimentally untested. This is the template that most needs calibration.

## Testable Predictions and Critical Experiments

**Prediction 1: The Contour PE Continuum**

**Bar**: VF1 predicts a continuous relationship between vertex sharpness and amygdala activation for architectural forms. Present participants with architectural facades that vary parametrically from fully curved (all rounded corners and transitions) to fully angular (all sharp vertices), with complexity matched. VF1 predicts: amygdala activation increases monotonically with vertex sharpness; ACC approach activation increases monotonically with curvature. If there is a THRESHOLD (no amygdala response until some angle is reached), the threat-association account is correct. If the response is continuous, the contour PE account is correct.

**Prediction 2: The Transition-Enhancement Test for Ceiling Height**

**Meyers-Levy**: VF3's PE addition predicts that moving from a low to a high ceiling produces a LARGER cognitive-broadening effect than being in a high ceiling from the start. Test: three conditions — (A) perform creative task in a high-ceiling room after entering from a low-ceiling corridor; (B) perform the same task in the same high-ceiling room after entering from another high-ceiling room; (C) perform the task in the low-ceiling corridor. VF3 predicts A > B > C on creative task performance. The A > B comparison specifically tests the PE transition prediction and distinguishes the PE account from the static-proportions account.

**Prediction 3: The Visual Rhythm Groove**

**Taylor**: VF2 predicts that moderate variation in architectural rhythm produces more aesthetic engagement than either perfect regularity or random variation — the visual groove analogy. Present participants with colonnades that vary in regularity: (A) perfectly even spacing, (B) 10% random variation in spacing, (C) 30% variation, (D) 60% variation (near-random). VF2 predicts an inverted-U function peaking at moderate variation (B or C), paralleling M9's groove Goldilocks zone for musical rhythm. If preference increases monotonically with regularity, VF2's rhythm model is wrong and simple fluency is sufficient.

**Prediction 4: The Scaling Hierarchy Preference**

**Salingaros**: VF2 predicts that buildings with complete scaling hierarchies (structure at every scale from massing to detail) are preferred over buildings with incomplete hierarchies (structure at some scales but gaps at others). Present participants with building images systematically varied for scaling completeness: (A) full hierarchy, (B) macro + micro but missing intermediate (glass curtain wall with intricate handles), (C) macro + intermediate but missing micro (articulated facade but smooth featureless surfaces), (D) macro only (a pure box). VF2 predicts: A > B ≈ C > D. If A does not significantly exceed B and C, the multi-scale engagement claim is wrong and single-scale processing is sufficient.

## Panel VF-II Prospectus and Scope Exclusions

### What a Second Visual Form Panel Would Do

**VF-II Priority 1: Calibrate VF2 (Visual Rhythm) — The Rhythm Groove Experiment**

The visual-rhythm Goldilocks function (Prediction 3) must be empirically established. VF-II would design and run the parametric colonnade experiment to determine whether moderate rhythmic variation produces peak aesthetic engagement. If confirmed, VF-II would extend to facade analysis: measure the rhythmic regularity of historically preferred facades (Palladio, Brunelleschi, Islamic geometric) and test whether they cluster near the visual groove optimum.

**Additional panelist needed**: A visual perception researcher with experience in temporal pattern processing — possibly Grahn (Western University, rhythm perception) or Kourtzi (Cambridge, visual learning and prediction).

**VF-II Priority 2: Test VF3 Transition-Enhancement Prediction**

The PE-specific prediction that ceiling-height transitions produce larger cognitive-style effects than static proportions (Prediction 2) is the critical test that distinguishes the PE account from alternative mechanisms. VF-II would design and run this experiment.

**VF-II Priority 3: Parameterize VF1 for Architectural Scales**

Bar and Vartanian's data are from photographs of buildings or abstract shapes. VF-II would extend to REAL-SCALE immersive environments (VR or actual buildings) to test whether the contour PE effect holds at architectural scale and whether the magnitude differs from photograph-based studies.

### Scope Exclusions

**Color**: Addressed by Palmer's ecological valence theory but judged mechanistically shallow for a dedicated template. Color in architecture operates primarily through conditioned associations (MAT5's mechanism) and cross-modal material prediction (a material's color is part of its identity signal, handled by MAT3). A dedicated color template is not warranted unless future evidence reveals a deeper computational mechanism.

**Ornament debate**: The modernism-vs-ornament debate (Loos's "Ornament and Crime," Venturi's "Complexity and Contradiction") is an aesthetic and cultural argument. The template framework can inform it (T1, T2, VF2 predict that some ornament optimizes visual PE; removing ALL ornament risks visual starvation), but the cultural question of whether ornament is appropriate is beyond the scope of mechanistic psychology.

**Individual differences in visual form preference**: As with all panels, individual and cultural variation in aesthetic response is a framework-level issue that requires a dedicated methods panel, not a domain-specific template.

### Estimated Impact of VF-II on Coverage

If VF-II accomplishes Priorities 1–2, A6 coverage would consolidate at ★★★★ with VF2 gaining empirical calibration and VF3 gaining PE-specific validation.
