## BLOCK A: ONTOLOGICAL STATUS OF THE T-LEVELS

### A. Carl Craver (Constitutive Mechanisms & Levels)

**Response to A1-A5**

Let me begin with what I think you have gotten right, and then explain why I believe your answer about ontological status is unstable — you have stronger claims than you realize, and they deserve more careful grounding.

You describe the T-levels as "mostly (c) with some genuine (a)" — mostly epistemic hierarchy, some genuine ontological stratification. I want to push back: I think you have either more genuine ontological structure than you're claiming, or less, and the difference matters profoundly for what your system can do.

My distinction between "levels of mechanisms" and "levels of nature" is crucial here (Craver & Bechtel, 2007). A level of nature is a real partition of causal systems — cells have genuine boundaries, contain genuine parts, exhibit genuine interaction patterns. A level of mechanism, by contrast, is an epistemic object: it picks out the right grain of causal description for explaining a given phenomenon. When we ask "why does this neural circuit exhibit this behavior?", we might invoke the neurons (implementation level) or the circuit's overall logic (algorithmic level). Both are real, but they answer different explanatory questions.

Your T-levels appear to be levels of mechanisms, not levels of nature. Your T1 frameworks (Predictive Processing, Neuromodulatory Systems) are not natural kinds with clear boundaries — they are organizing principles that physicists might disagree about. Your T1.5 theories (ART, SRT) are even more obviously epistemic — they are *theories in the literature*, not partitions of nature. And your T2 templates are mechanism schemas: causal chains formalized in a way that makes certain causal structures explicit and others invisible.

This is not a criticism. Mechanistic explanation *must* work at levels of mechanisms, not levels of nature. Craver & Darden (2013) shows that productive scientific explanation often requires precisely this kind of epistemic stratification. The mistake would be to claim *ontological* status for what is *epistemically useful*.

Here is the crucial question: are your levels **constitutively relevant**? That is, does each level isolate the causes that are genuinely relevant for explaining phenomena at that level and above?

Consider your T1 → T1.5 reduction claim. You say T1 frameworks explain 60-86% of T1.5 variance. The residual (14-40%) you characterize as "irreducible." In my framework, this residual matters only if:

1. It consists of causal factors that are genuinely *absent from* the T1 description, not merely *unformalized within* it. If the residual is just "we didn't measure the right T1 variables," that is epistemic ignorance, not a genuine mismatch of explanatory levels.

2. It is constitutively relevant — removing it would break the explanatory chain. This is the hard part to test.

My advice: you should shift from variance-based reduction (60-86% coverage) to **constitutive relevance testing**. For a specific empirical case — say, Stress Recovery Theory applied to a particular phenomenon — ask: *what* precisely in the phenomenon fails to be explained by the T1 mechanisms, and is that failure because (a) we haven't measured the right variables, or (b) the phenomenon genuinely involves a causal structure that T1 mechanisms don't capture?

A concrete example: Place Attachment shows 60% T1 reduction. Ask: what is the 40% that is irreducible? Is it:
- Variables we haven't measured (unmapped T1 mechanisms)?
- Social/cultural variables that are independent of T1?
- Temporal dynamics (adaptation, nostalgia, developmental windows)?
- Identity-constitutive properties (the place is part of *who I am*)?

If it is the first, you do not have a genuine T1.5 level — you have incomplete T1 knowledge. If it is one of the others, you *might* have genuine T1.5 structure, but you need to show that these variables are not epiphenomena of some deeper T1 mechanism.

**Regarding T1 atoms**: You are correct to be concerned about mixed empirical maturity. Divisive normalization (Carandini & Heeger, 2012) is empirically established in multiple cell types and animals. "Affordance computation" is a psychological-level postulation with weak neural grounding. These should not be in the same ontological category. I would propose:

- **Canonical computations**: Elements with direct neural evidence (divisive normalization, prediction error in specific forms, lateral inhibition).
- **Computational hypotheses**: Postulated elements inferred from behavior and theory but lacking direct neural evidence.

These are different kinds of entities, and conflating them undermines your claim to have a principled foundational level.

**Regarding functional circuits**: This is where I most sharply disagree with your approach. You say they are "molecules whose internal structure follows a specific T2 archetype pattern." But what makes a functional circuit *real* as a causal structure is not its computational pattern — it is whether it constitutes a level of mechanism for explaining behavior.

Take your "fluency monitor" circuit. You define it as: prediction error + perceptual organization + metabolic cost → ease signal → hedonic marking. But I ask: *is this a constitutive level for explaining anything?* That is, if I were explaining why an experience feels fluent, would removing or altering this circuit change the explanation, or would I simply need to appeal to the underlying T1 atoms and archetypes? If the circuit is truly a level of mechanism, there must be some phenomena where *circuit-level* description is causally relevant.

Your answer should not be: "the circuit is composed of these T1 atoms." That is compositional, not mechanistic. Your answer should be: "when you explain X, you must invoke the circuit's properties (not just its parts)." Do your circuits have emergent properties that are causally relevant for any phenomenon?

**My verdict**: You have the structure of a sound mechanistic hierarchy, but you need to:
1. Be explicit that you are working with levels of mechanisms, not levels of nature.
2. Replace variance-reduction criteria with constitutive relevance testing.
3. Separate canonical computations (well-grounded) from computational hypotheses (speculative).
4. Demonstrate that functional circuits are genuinely mechanistic levels (not just convenient descriptions) by showing phenomena that require circuit-level explanation.

### B. Nancy Cartwright (Capacities, Causal Powers, Dappled World)

**Response to A1-A5**

Your question asks whether the hierarchy is ontological, pragmatic, or epistemic. I think you are asking the wrong question — or rather, the trichotomy is false. Let me explain why, using your own hierarchy to show what I mean.

In my account (Cartwright, 1999, 2007), causal explanation works by identifying causal capacities — powers that objects have to produce effects. A capacity is real, not merely epistemic, but it is always *context-dependent*. Aspirin has the capacity to reduce pain, but only in the right context (you must ingest it, your digestive system must work, you must not be allergic, etc.). The capacity is ontologically real, but it is not universalizable across all contexts.

Your T-levels seem to presume that there are context-invariant causal structures. Your T1 frameworks are presented as universal principles: Predictive Processing explains phenomena in visual perception, pain, language, and planning. Your T2 templates are presented as reusable mechanism chains: the same chain (Daylight → Circadian Entrainment → Alertness) is supposed to work wherever you instantiate it.

But here is what I suspect is happening in practice: when you apply "Predictive Processing" to two different phenomena, you are not applying the same universal mechanism. You are recognizing that both phenomena exhibit a certain *causal pattern* — discrepancy reduction through model refinement. But the capacities that implement this pattern are different in each domain. Visual prediction error is a different capacity from reward prediction error, even if they share a formal structure.

This matters because it means your hierarchy is not carved at ontologically fundamental joints. It is carved at the level of *formal structures that recur*. Your T1 frameworks, T2 archetypes, and T2 templates are not ontological levels of nature; they are organizational schemes for recognizing recurring causal patterns.

Recognized patterns are scientifically useful — I am not denying that. But they are not the same as discovering fundamental levels. The "dappled world" I described in *Hunting Causes and Using Them* is a world where universal laws are rare and local capacities are common. Your taxonomy sounds like it is trying to find universality (one set of T1 frameworks that explain everything), but what you are actually discovering is a collection of locally-operating capacities that happen to exhibit formal similarities.

This reframes your questions about T1.5, atoms, and circuits. They are not levels of ontology. They are *refinements of causal pattern recognition*. T1.5 theories are a subset of molecules that have published provenance — they are distinguished not by deeper causal truth but by sociological fact (someone wrote them down). T1 atoms are postulated computational elements that seem to recur, but their "recurrence" is a pattern we recognize, not an ontological primitive. Functional circuits are particularly troubling in this regard: you define them by their computational pattern alone, which makes them purely formal — they are equivalence classes over mechanistically different configurations.

**Here is what you should do:** Stop thinking of your hierarchy as ontological levels, and instead think of it as a *taxonomy of causal capacities organized by scope and specificity*.

- T1 frameworks: broad causal patterns that work in multiple domains (recognizing what they do in common).
- T1.5 theories: domain-specific elaborations of these patterns, with published provenance (historically realized capacities).
- T2 templates: specific instantiations of these patterns in particular causal chains.
- T1 atoms: hypothesized elementary capacities from which larger patterns are composed.

This reframing has several advantages:

1. **It acknowledges context-dependence**: You stop claiming universality and instead claim "this pattern recurs in these contexts."
2. **It makes the sociological boundary of T1.5 less troubling**: T1.5 theories are distinguished by provenance because they are historically-realized capacities. That is not a defect; it is a feature.
3. **It softens the problem of reduction**: T1 frameworks don't fully "explain" T1.5 theories because capacities are context-dependent. The 60-86% coverage is not failure; it is success at recognizing the common formal pattern while acknowledging that each instantiation adds context-specific causal power.
4. **It clarifies the ontology of functional circuits**: They are not "real" or "unreal" in a context-free sense. They are real as equivalence classes of capacities — useful for organizing knowledge but not ultimate natural kinds.

**My verdict**: Your hierarchy is scientifically valuable, but you are overreaching ontologically. Reframe it as a taxonomy of recurring causal capacities, organized by scope, specificity, and social provenance. This removes the pressure to prove that T1.5 is "real" and instead lets you ask: what causal patterns are historically significant and worth organizing?

Stop worrying about whether functional circuits are "genuine mechanisms." Ask instead: do these circuits (as equivalence classes of capacities) enable you to recognize, predict, and manipulate causal patterns in the world? That is the test that matters.

### C. James Woodward (Interventionism & Causal Explanation)

**Response to A1-A5**

Your question about ontological status cannot be separated from the question of what kind of causal explanation your hierarchy supports. Let me focus on that.

In my framework (Woodward, 2003), an explanatory relationship is a causal relationship that satisfies certain invariance conditions. Roughly: Y is explained by X if (a) X causes Y, and (b) interventions on X produce changes in Y in a systematically explorable way. The invariance must be robust enough that we can answer "what if we had intervened differently?" questions.

Your T-levels mix causal and non-causal relations, and I do not think you have been explicit about this. Let me identify where:

**T1 → T1.5 relation**: You say T1 frameworks "explain" T1.5 theories. What does this mean? If it means T1 mechanisms *cause* the T1.5 phenomena, then it is a causal claim. But you quantify this with "60-86% of variance." This suggests a statistical dependency, not a causal relationship. Statistical explanation and causal explanation are very different things.

Interventionist causal explanation requires that we can specify *interventions* on T1 variables that would reliably produce changes in T1.5 outcomes. Can you do this? Take Stress Recovery Theory. Can you specify *what interventions on the Predictive Processing framework would produce changes in recovery trajectories*? If not, the "explanation" is not causal — it is descriptive or analogical.

**T2 templates as causal chains**: Your T2 templates are presented as mechanism chains: "Daylight exposure → Circadian Entrainment → Alertness." These *look* like causal chains, and if they are, they should satisfy interventionist criteria. Specifically:

- There must be identifiable interventions on the first variable (daylight exposure) that reliably change the outcome (alertness).
- The mechanism chain (circadian entrainment) should be the route through which this happens.
- We should be able to answer: "If we had intervened on circadian entrainment directly (bypassing daylight), would alertness still change?"

Your T3 evidence (e.g., "nature views reduce cortisol by 15-20%") is often correlational, not interventional. Extracting T2 templates from correlational data is risky; you may be reading in causal structure that is not actually there.

**T3 → T2 → T1.5 direction**: You say T3 beliefs provide evidence for T2 templates which support T1.5 theories. This is an evidential direction, not an explanatory one. Confusing evidence direction with explanation direction is a common mistake. The fact that T3 data supports T2 templates does not mean T2 templates explain T3 phenomena — it means the templates are consistent with the data and help account for it.

**My specific concerns**:

1. **Your hierarchy has causally heterogeneous edges**. The arrow from T1 to T1.5 is not the same kind of causal relationship as the arrow from T2 templates to T3 beliefs. One is compositional/reductive, the other is evidential. Mixing them into one hierarchy obscures what is actually happening.

2. **Your T2 templates need interventional grounding**. For each template, you should ask: what is a natural intervention on the first variable, and does it reliably produce the outcome through the postulated mechanism? If you cannot answer this, the template is a description of a correlation, not an explanation of a causal structure.

3. **Your T1.5 reduction percentage is misleading**. Saying T1 frameworks explain 60% of T1.5 variance sounds like progress. But what it probably means is: "T1 mechanisms correlate with T1.5 phenomena to some degree, but we do not understand the full causal chain." This is not a partial explanation; it is a sketch that needs filling in.

**My verdict**: Your hierarchy is partly causal (T2 templates are meant as causal chains, T1 atoms postulate causal capacities) and partly non-causal (T1 explaining T1.5 looks more like subsumption or analogy). You should:

1. **Separate causal and non-causal relations** in the hierarchy. Do not treat "T1 explains T1.5" the same as "T2 templates causally mediate T3 outcomes."

2. **Anchor T2 templates in interventional data** where possible. For each template, document whether it is grounded in observational, experimental, or intervention-based evidence. This matters for what the template can be used for.

3. **Clarify the direction of evidential support**. T3 data supporting T2 templates is a validation relationship, not an explanation. The T2 template explains the T3 phenomenon; the T3 data is evidence for whether the template is correct.

4. **Distinguish explanatory depth from empirical coverage**. Explaining 60% of variance is better than explaining 40%, but both could be shallow explanations. The question is whether you have identified the actual causal structures responsible for the phenomenon, not how much variance you have accounted for.

### D. Michael Strevens (Kairetic Depth & Difference-Making)

**Response to A1-A5**

I want to come at your ontological question from a different angle: not "what is real?" but "what is relevant to explanation?" Your T-levels need to satisfy a principle I call kairetic depth: include all and only the factors that make a difference to the phenomenon you are explaining.

This principle is radical in its implications. It means that a level can be genuinely real (exist in nature, have causal powers) and still be *explanatorily irrelevant* because it does not make a difference to the specific phenomenon at hand. Conversely, something that is not "fundamental" in an ontological sense can be explanatorily central if it is difference-making.

Apply this to your hierarchy. Your T1 atoms (prediction error, lateral inhibition, etc.) are postulated as foundational. But many of them may not be difference-making for the phenomena you are trying to explain. If removing prediction error from your model would not change the explanation of a particular phenomenon, then prediction error is not kairetically relevant to that phenomenon, regardless of whether it is fundamental to neural computation in general.

Here is a concrete case: your "fluency monitor" circuit is defined as (prediction error + perceptual organization + metabolic cost). For explaining why a stimulus feels fluent, is prediction error *really* a difference-making factor? Or is fluency explained adequately by perceptual organization and metabolic cost alone, with prediction error as an epiphenomenal passenger?

Your answer should not be "prediction error is part of the circuit." The question is whether altering prediction error in the right way would make a difference to fluency. If it would not, then prediction error is not part of the *explanation*, even if it is part of the underlying mechanism.

This principle suggests that your T1 atom level may be *over-complete*. You have ~30 atoms, but kairetic depth would ask: for explaining environmental psychology phenomena, how many of these atoms actually matter? My prediction: fewer than 20 are genuinely difference-making. The others are neural realities that have zero impact on the phenomena you are trying to explain.

Similarly, your T2 archetypes may violate kairetic depth in the opposite direction. You have 6 archetypes, but you may be over-aggregating. Two phenomena that both involve "competitive selection" may differ in explanatorily relevant ways that the archetype obscures. "Competitive selection" between action options is not the same as "competitive selection" between perceptual interpretations, even if the formal structure is similar. The difference may be explanatorily crucial.

**My verdict**: Use kairetic depth as a pruning criterion:

1. For each T1 atom, ask: for the specific phenomena in the built environment literature, does this atom make a difference? If not, remove it or relegate it to "potentially relevant but not explanatory."

2. For each T2 archetype, ask: are there phenomena that require finer-grained distinction? If two distinct mechanisms fall under one archetype but differ in explanatorily relevant ways, split the archetype.

3. For T2 templates, audit them to ensure each step in the causal chain is difference-making. A template like "Daylight → Circadian Entrainment → Alertness" is fine only if removing any of these steps would be explanatorily wrong.

The principle behind this is: explanation does not require knowing everything about a system. It requires knowing *what matters*. Your hierarchy is in danger of being so comprehensive that it loses sight of what actually matters for understanding environmental effects.

### E. Sandra Mitchell (Integrative Pluralism)

**Response to A1-A5**

Your question about whether the T-levels constitute a genuine hierarchy is central to whether you are doing integrative pluralism or bad pluralism. Let me explain the distinction.

Integrative pluralism (Mitchell, 2003, 2009) says: different levels of explanation are needed because different phenomena exhibit causal organization at different scales and scopes. Vision requires neural-level explanation. Epidemiology requires population-level explanation. You cannot reduce one to the other without loss of explanatory power. But the levels are *integrated* — they share mechanisms, they inform each other, they constrain each other.

Bad pluralism says: we have multiple explanatory frameworks because we do not know which one is true, or because they are useful for different purposes, but there is no principled integration. This is "pluralism as ignorance," and it is scientifically problematic.

Your taxonomy risks being bad pluralism. Here is why: you have not articulated a principled *reason* why these particular levels exist. You have defined them operationally (T1 is frameworks with >3 domains, T1.5 is theories with residuals >14%, T2 is causal templates, etc.), but operational definitions do not make levels genuine.

A genuine level has:
1. A characteristic scale or grain of causal organization.
2. Phenomena that are irreducible to finer-grained levels without loss of predictive or explanatory power.
3. Integration with other levels — mechanisms that cross-cut the boundary and force communication between levels.

Let me test your T-levels against these criteria:

**T1 frameworks**: Do these constitute a genuine level? Your examples (Predictive Processing, Neuromodulatory Systems, Interoceptive Constructionism) are so different in their scope and specificity that I am skeptical they form a unified level. Predictive Processing is a broad computational principle. Neuromodulatory Systems is a description of a neural implementation strategy. These are not even the same kind of entity. Genuine levels share a common causal grain.

**T1.5 theories**: This is where I am most skeptical. A "level" defined as "molecules with published authors" is not a level in any scientific sense. It is a historical artifact. The Kaplans wrote Attention Restoration Theory, so it is a T1.5 theory. But if the Kaplans had not written about it, but the identical causal structure had been discovered and published by someone else in different form, would it still be T1.5? The fact that T1.5-hood depends on whether someone wrote it down means it is not a genuine ontological level.

**T2 templates**: This looks more like a genuine level — specific causal chains with identifiable inputs and outputs. I see the integrative work here: templates compose into molecules, templates are supported by T3 evidence. This is integration.

**T3 beliefs**: These are ground-level empirical claims. Genuine level? Depends on whether the claims are indeed atomic (irreducible empirical units) or artifacts of how the literature is written. You treat "nature views reduce cortisol by 15-20%" as atomic, but this is not ontologically atomic — it is pragmatically atomic (you cannot decompose it further given your current extraction methods).

**My diagnosis**: You have one genuine level (T2 templates), two questionable levels (T1 frameworks and T3 beliefs, which may be too coarse or too fine), and one pseudo-level (T1.5, defined by sociology rather than science).

**What would make this genuine integrative pluralism?** You would need to show:

1. **Multiple causal grains are genuinely needed**. That is: some phenomena require T1-level explanation; others require T2 or T3 level. You should have examples of phenomena that are inexplicable at coarser or finer grains.

2. **The levels constrain each other**. T1 mechanisms constrain what T2 templates are possible. T2 templates constrain what T3 phenomena should occur. T3 data constrains T2 templates and forces revision of T1 frameworks. Currently, your hierarchy seems more like a filing system than a constraining system.

3. **The boundaries are sharp where they matter and blurry where they don't**. T1.5 should not be a distinct level — it should dissolve into "molecules that have been studied by the Kaplans and others." But some other boundary (perhaps between T1 and T1.5) should be sharp and principled.

**My verdict**: You are on the way to integrative pluralism, but you have not yet established that the T-levels are *necessary* levels rather than *convenient* categories. To become genuinely integrative pluralism:

- **Eliminate T1.5** as a distinct level. T1.5 theories are T2 + molecules with published authors. That is not a level.
- **Tighten T1 frameworks**. Either unify them under a single principle (if Friston is right about FEP, your T1 should just be FEP) or split them into finer-grained types. Do not have multiple distinct "frameworks" that are all equally fundamental unless you can show why multiple fundamentals are necessary.
- **Replace T3 "beliefs" with measured variables**. T3 should not be "salivary cortisol reduced by 15-20%" (which embeds an effect size). T3 should be "salivary cortisol level measured in control/treatment groups." This atomic unit is measurement-independent and can be reinterpreted across studies.
- **Test irreducibility empirically**. For each T1 framework, show a phenomenon that cannot be explained adequately at the T2 or T3 level. This is the test of whether the level is genuinely needed.

### F. Robert Batterman (Asymptotic Explanation & Universality Classes)

**Response to A3-A5**

Your question about T2 archetypes is whether they are "natural kinds or family resemblances." I want to offer a third option: universality classes.

In statistical physics (Batterman & Rice, 2014), a universality class is a set of systems with radically different microscopic details but identical large-scale behavior. Water and other fluids have the same critical exponents near their phase transitions, despite wildly different molecules. Magnetic systems have the same scaling laws despite different underlying materials. These are not family resemblances — they are rigorous mathematical structures. They are not natural kinds in the traditional sense — they do not carve nature at fundamental joints. But they are *real* in a precise way: they identify robust invariants that are independent of microscopic details.

This might be what your T2 archetypes are. Consider "homeostatic regulation." This archetype appears in circadian entrainment (maintaining rhythms), temperature regulation (maintaining body heat), pH balance (maintaining acid-base homeostasis), and emotional regulation (maintaining affective state). The microscopic mechanisms are completely different. Circadian entrainment uses specific genes and proteins. Temperature regulation uses hypothalamic neurons and vascular control. pH balance uses chemical buffering.

But the large-scale structure is mathematically similar: a variable is measured, compared to a setpoint, and error signals drive compensatory processes. This is not analogical similarity; it is mathematical similarity. The systems are in the same *universality class* — they exhibit the same scaling laws and asymptotic behavior.

If your archetypes are universality classes, then:

1. **They are not arbitrary**: The fact that 10 homeostatic systems all map onto one archetype is evidence that the archetype picks out genuine mathematical structure.

2. **They are not natural kinds**: They are equivalence classes under a transformation. Many different mechanisms produce the same large-scale behavior.

3. **They are explanatorily powerful**: Because they identify robust invariant structure, they allow prediction and design even without knowing microscopic details.

4. **They are not the deepest level**: Universality classes are emergence phenomena. The archetype abstracts away from lower-level detail, but those details are necessary to explain *why* the system behaves this way under perturbation, or why it breaks down under extreme conditions.

**How to test this**: For each archetype, ask: do the systems instantiating this archetype have the same quantitative scaling laws? Same ratio of timescales? Same response to perturbations?

If yes, the archetype is a universality class — genuinely real, not just convenient.

If no, the archetype is merely a morphological pattern — real perhaps, but not with the ontological depth of a universality class.

**Regarding Friston's FEP**: You worry that FEP unifies all archetypes under variational inference, making them non-fundamental. I have a different take. If FEP is true, your archetypes are universality classes within the FEP framework. That is, they are invariant structures that emerge from FEP dynamics across different systems. They would be *more* interesting, not less, because they would be rigorously justified.

But this requires that you show how each archetype emerges from FEP. That is non-trivial and would strengthen your taxonomy if you could do it.

**My verdict**: Test whether your T2 archetypes are universality classes. If they are, your taxonomy gains a form of objectivity (mathematical structure) that transcends particular mechanisms. If they are not, they are useful patterns but not fundamental structures, and your taxonomy should demote them accordingly.

### G. Judea Pearl (Causal Hierarchy & Causal Models)

**Response to A1-A5**

Your hierarchy conflates the observational, interventional, and counterfactual levels of causal explanation. This is the core problem, and it affects every aspect of your taxonomy.

In my causal hierarchy framework (Pearl & Mackenzie, 2018), there are three distinct levels:

1. **Observational**: What is the joint probability distribution? What correlations exist?
2. **Interventional**: If I do X, what happens to Y? This requires causal models with do-operators.
3. **Counterfactual**: If I had done X instead of Y, what would have happened? This requires causal models with potential outcomes.

Your T3 beliefs are mostly observational: "nature views reduce cortisol by 15-20%." This is an association in data. Your T2 templates are written as causal chains ("Daylight → Circadian Entrainment → Alertness"), which suggests interventional claims. But are they grounded in intervention data, or inferred from observations?

Your T1 frameworks are even less clear. "Predictive Processing" is a computational principle, not a causal model. It does not specify do-operator semantics. This means your T1 frameworks cannot be used to compute counterfactuals, and counterfactual reasoning is essential for understanding causation.

**Specific problems**:

1. **T2 templates lack do-operator semantics**. For the template "Daylight → Circadian Entrainment → Alertness," can you compute: P(Alertness | do(Daylight = high))? If not, this is not a proper causal model. You may have inferred a correlation, but you do not have causal knowledge.

2. **T1.5 reduction percentages are meaningless as causal claims**. Saying T1 frameworks explain 60% of T1.5 variance is an observational statement. It says there is covariation, not that T1 mechanisms are the causes. To claim causation, you would need to show that interventions on T1 variables change T1.5 outcomes in a way that the T1 framework predicts.

3. **Functional circuits are defined functionally, not causally**. Your fluency monitor is defined by its input-output profile. But the causal structure — what variable causes what — is not specified. This means you cannot use the circuit for causal reasoning or prediction under intervention.

**How to fix this**:

1. **Distinguish observational, interventional, and counterfactual layers**. For each T2 template, specify:
   - What correlations exist (observational)?
   - What happens if we intervene (interventional)?
   - What would have happened if we had intervened differently (counterfactual)?

2. **Build causal models, not just templates**. A causal model specifies a directed acyclic graph (DAG) with conditional independencies. Your templates should be CAM-compliant — they should specify the DAG structure that justifies your causal claims.

3. **Use causal identification techniques**. Before claiming that Daylight → Alertness through Circadian Entrainment, you should use identification techniques (backdoor criterion, instrumental variables, do-calculus) to show that you can estimate this causal effect from your data. If you cannot identify it, you do not have causal knowledge yet.

4. **For counterfactual reasoning**: Your system should be able to answer: "Given this person's data and this environmental intervention, what would their cortisol be if we had done something different?" This requires fully specified causal models at every level.

**My verdict**: Your hierarchy is a useful descriptive organization, but it is not yet a causal hierarchy. To become one, you need to build causal models (with DAGs and do-operator semantics) at multiple levels and ensure that your causal claims are identified from your data. The T-levels could then be genuinely causal, not just correlational descriptions with causal language.

### H. Angela Potochnik (Idealization & Explanatory Aims)

**Response to A1-A5**

Your taxonomy serves a dual purpose: scientific understanding and engineering guidance for architects. This is a crucial detail, because idealization serves different functions depending on your explanatory aim.

In *Idealization and the Aims of Science* (Potochnik, 2017), I argue that the same phenomenon can have multiple correct explanations at different levels of idealization, depending on what you are trying to do. A physics teacher explaining why a ball falls uses Newtonian mechanics with air resistance ignored. An engineer designing a parachute uses detailed aerodynamic models with air resistance central. Both are correct; they idealize differently because they have different aims.

Your dual aim (understanding + engineering) creates a unique problem: **your idealization choices are constrained by engineering need, not just by explanatory aim**. This distorts the scientific picture.

Example: Your "molecules" are units that make sense for architects ("apply ART principles"). But are molecules scientifically real? Or are they pragmatic bundles of T2 templates that happen to be useful for engineering? The fact that a molecule translates neatly to architectural guidance suggests that molecules are *engineered idealizations* rather than *scientifically grounded decompositions*.

Similarly, T1.5 theories (ART, SRT) are distinguished because they have published authors and clear construct definitions. This is pragmatically useful for architects ("here is an established theory with known principles"). But scientifically, this criterion (published authorship) is orthogonal to what makes a theory real or fundamental.

**The core risk**: You are in danger of optimizing for engineering usability at the expense of scientific accuracy. Your hierarchy may reflect the joint constraints of (neural mechanisms) AND (architectural decision points) rather than reflecting the causal structure of reality.

**How to diagnose this**:

1. Build the hierarchy purely from scientific considerations (what makes a genuine level of explanation?).
2. Then, separately, ask: what is useful for engineering?
3. Compare. Where they diverge, you have evidence that engineering is warping your science.

For example, if the purely scientific hierarchy had fewer levels or different boundaries than your current taxonomy, that would be diagnostic. You could then ask: are we adding levels for engineering convenience?

**My specific recommendations**:

### B. Herbert Simon (Near-Decomposability & Hierarchical Structure)

**Response to C5**

You worry that multi-loading of T2 templates violates my near-decomposability criterion for hierarchical structure. Let me explain why near-decomposability is important and what your violation means.

If every part were connected to every other part (no scale separation), you could not understand the system level by level. You would need to understand everything at once, which is cognitively impossible and evolutionarily implausible.

Biological hierarchies exist because they have near-decomposability properties: cells have fast internal processes and slow external interaction (through membranes). 1. **The templates are not truly part of molecules** — they are underlying shared mechanisms that multiple molecules recruit. In this case, molecules are not real compositional units; they are *functional clusters* of shared templates. This is a different ontology than you claim.

2. **The molecules are not real decompositions** — they are pragmatic groupings (useful for architects) that do not correspond to natural separations in the causal structure.

3. **The template definitions are too coarse** — the same "alertness template" in different molecules is actually implementing different causal structures. ### C. Konrad Kording (Neural Data Science & Identifiability)

**Response to D1-D4, B1-B4**

I want to focus on the practical question: **can you identify your T2 archetypes and functional circuits from neural data?**

In machine learning and neuroscience, we often have models that are theoretically sensible but practically unidentifiable: the data cannot distinguish between them because they make identical predictions. This is a serious problem.

Your T2 archetypes (predictive coding, homeostatic regulation, etc.) are defined computationally. But neural data does not directly show you the computation; it shows you spiking patterns. The question is: given neural recordings, could you uniquely identify which archetype is being implemented?

Example: Predictive coding says neurons represent prediction errors. But "prediction error" can be encoded in many ways: firing rate, population code, temporal patterns, etc. In practice, if you see a neuron that covaries with stimulus surprise, you attribute it to prediction error. But you cannot rule out that it is representing something else (attention, salience, learning signal) that happens to covary with surprise.

This is the **identifiability problem**: your archetypes may not be empirically distinguishable.

**What this means for your taxonomy**:

1. **Your archetypes may be empirically unfalsifiable**. If you cannot specify what data would definitively show that a system is NOT implementing predictive coding (as opposed to some other archetype that happens to produce similar neural patterns), the archetype lacks empirical content.

2. **Your functional circuits are even more problematic**. A "fluency monitor circuit" defined by input-output profile could be implemented by many different neural mechanisms. Unless you can identify the specific circuit in neural data, it is a theoretical convenience, not an empirical discovery.

**How to address this**:

1. **Specify neural signatures**: For each archetype and circuit, specify what neural data would identify it. What patterns of firing, connectivity, or dynamics are *diagnostic* for this archetype?

2. **Use identifiability analysis**: For each theoretical signature, ask: could an alternative archetype produce the same neural patterns? If yes, your archetypes are not identifiable, and you need more constraints.

3. **Validate on neural data**: Take published neural datasets (visual processing, motor control, decision-making) and ask: can you reliably identify which archetype is being implemented? If not, your archetypes are not empirically grounded.

4. **Be honest about proxy measures**: If you are using behavioral data (response times, choice patterns) to infer archetypes, be explicit about this. The archetype is identified *behaviorally*, not *neurally*. This is useful but less fundamental than neural identification.

**Regarding functional circuits**: These are the most problematic because they require identifying not just a computation but a *functional module*. This requires showing that the circuit is causally separable (disrupting it changes behavior in expected ways) and internally integrated (components work together).

Very few circuits meet these criteria. Most neural systems are distributed and multifunctional. A region implementing "part of the fluency monitor" may simultaneously implement other functions. Calling this a "circuit" may mislead you into thinking it is more separable than it actually is.

**My verdict**: Validate your archetypes and circuits using neural identifiability analysis. If you cannot uniquely identify them from neural data (or specify how you would), be honest about whether they are theoretical conveniences or empirical discoveries.

If this is true, your taxonomy is not a universal structure; it is a historically contingent developmental achievement.

### B. Peter Godfrey-Smith (Biological Individuality & Levels of Selection)

**Response to E1-E3**

Your question asks whether your levels are "real" in the way biological levels are. But what does it mean for a biological level to be "real"?

In biology, we have clear levels: genes, cells, organisms, populations. These are real in a specific sense: they have clear boundaries (you can point to where one level ends and another begins), they have characteristic timescales and interaction patterns, and they have evolutionary significance (selection can act at each level).

Molecules contain templates; are molecules "made of" templates, or are they patterns that templates participate in? Functional circuits contain atoms; are circuits "made of" atoms, or are they organizational patterns?

Biological levels have clearer boundaries. A cell has a membrane. An organism has a body boundary. A population is defined by reproductive isolation. You have no equivalent boundaries for your T-levels.

**Timescales**: Biological levels have distinct timescales. Genetic changes occur at evolutionary timescales (generations). Cellular processes occur at millisecond-hour scales. Behavioral responses occur at second-minute scales. These timescale separations make level-distinctions natural.

Your T-levels do not have characteristic timescales. Do T1 frameworks operate at a different timescale than T1.5 theories? Are they slower? Faster? Or do they operate on the same timescale but at a different grain of abstraction?

**Evolutionary significance**: Biological levels matter for evolution because selection can act at each level. Genes replicate; cells compete; organisms reproduce; populations diverge. Each level has units that can be selected.

What are the units of selection at your levels? Do T1 frameworks compete and replicate? Do molecules evolve? This seems wrong. Your levels are not units of selection; they are descriptions at different grains.

**My diagnosis**: Your levels are real as *levels of description* but not real as *levels of nature* in the biological sense. They are useful for organizing knowledge, but they do not correspond to the natural divisions that biology finds.

This is not necessarily bad. There is nothing wrong with an epistemic hierarchy (levels of description). But you should be honest about it. Do not claim your levels are "real" in the sense that biological levels are real.

**What would make them real in a biological sense?**

The periodic table is not "real" in the sense that atomic structure is real, but it was incredibly useful for organizing chemistry.

This is not weakness; this is honest science.

**My verdict**: Reframe your hierarchy as an epistemic organization rather than an ontological one. This removes impossible burdens (proving levels are "really real") and focuses attention on what matters: whether the hierarchy helps you explain phenomena and build better science.

   **Option B** (Reframe): Acknowledge that molecules are not decomposition levels but functional clusters. Keep structure but relabel: "Functional clusters" instead of "levels of mechanisms."

Recommendation: Try Option A first (preserve hierarchy claim). If it fails, shift to Option B (preserve practical utility but be honest about ontology).

### Weakness 8: Molecule Abstraction Absorbs Everything Easily

**Panelist Verdict**: This is actually a *feature*, not a bug, but with caveats.

**What panelists said**:
- **Bechtel**: The ease of absorption might indicate you are conflating multiple levels (functional, spatial, type).
- **Smith**: If the abstraction is too flexible, it constrains nothing. You need to ask what structures can *not* be molecules.
- **Potochnik**: The fact that functional circuits fit easily into molecules suggests molecules are "engineered" for pragmatic use, not discovered as real levels.

**Recommendation**:

1. **Define molecule constraints explicitly**:
   - What does a collection of templates need to satisfy to be a molecule?
   - Current: "templates that co-activate frequently"
   - Better: "templates that co-activate AND whose constituent T1 atoms are locally integrated AND which produce a functionally distinct output"

2. **Define what CANNOT be a molecule**:
   - Single templates should not be molecules (too fine-grained)
   - Unconstrained template collections should not be molecules (too coarse-grained)
   - Templates that conflict (inhibit each other) should not be in same molecule

3. **Test whether molecules constrain downstream work**:
   - Do molecules predict question-answering structure better than ungrouped templates?
   - Do molecules improve architectural guidance quality?
   - If no: molecules are convenient but not constraining. Be honest about this.

If molecules pass these tests, the ease of abstraction is a feature (good design). If they fail, reorganize.

### Weakness 9: Domain Distribution Is Biased

**Panelist Verdict**: This reflects literature bias, not necessarily an error requiring correction.

**What panelists said**:
- **Thelen**: Your circuit distribution is adult-centric. Where are developmental, aging, and cultural circuits?
- **Smith**: Your distribution may be reasonable if it reflects where mechanistic evidence is strongest. The question is whether the omission is epistemically honest.

**Recommendation**:

1. **Audit literature for coverage**:
   - How much of your 800-paper corpus addresses perception (where your circuits are concentrated) vs. social/cultural effects (where your circuits are sparse)?
   - Is the circuit distribution proportional to literature distribution? If yes: bias is in literature, not system. Be explicit about this.
   - If no: your system is systematically over-representing some domains. Correct by seeking evidence in under-represented domains.

2. **Explicitly document what the system cannot represent**:
   - Temporal aspects (circuits are static)
   - Cultural variation (circuits are universal)
   - Individual differences (circuits assume population-level homogeneity)
   - Developmental change (circuits are age-insensitive)
   - Context-dependence (circuits assume stable input-output mappings)

3. **For each omission, ask**: Is this a limitation of current knowledge, or a limitation of the taxonomy? If limitation of the taxonomy, redesign to accommodate.

**Specific actionable step**: If you claim circuits for "social" (currently sparse), conduct targeted review of social neuroscience literature. Either find evidence for social circuits or acknowledge that social processing does not decompose into circuits the way perception does. This choice is scientifically important.

### Weakness 10: Three Relations Are Independently Indexed

**Panelist Verdict**: Unanimous that this is correct structurally and should be formalized.

**What panelists said**:
- **Woodward, Bechtel, Pearl, Smith**: These are fundamentally different relations. Representing them separately is right.
- **Craver**: The three relations (explanatory, evidential, compositional) have different logical properties and should be kept distinct.

**Recommendation**:

Formalize the decomposition (which CW already has in code) at the documentation and schema level:

1. **Create three explicit hierarchy structures**:

   ```
   Structure 1: ExplanatoryHierarchy
   - Edges: "explains"
   - Direction: T1 → T1.5 → T2 (top-down)
   - Semantics: X explains Y if X provides causal principles that account for Y
   - Index: _explanation_index

   Structure 2: EvidentialHierarchy
   - Edges: "is_evidenced_by"
   - Direction: T3 → T2 → T1.5 (bottom-up)
   - Semantics: T3 data supports/validates T2, which exemplifies T1.5
   - Index: _evidence_index

   Structure 3: CompositionHierarchy
   - Edges: "composed_of"
   - Direction: bidirectional
   - Semantics: X is composed of Y, where Y are parts/constituents
   - Index: _composition_index
   - Note: Is this functional, spatial, or type composition?
   ```

2. **Query methods for each**:
   ```
   ExplainedBy(X, Y) → traverses explanation_index
   EvidencedBy(X, Y) → traverses evidence_index
   ComposedOf(X, Y) → traverses composition_index
   ```

3. **In documentation, explicitly state**:
   - When you ask "how does T1 explain T3?" you traverse both explanation and composition hierarchies.
   - When you ask "what evidence supports T2?" you traverse evidence index.
   - When you ask "what are molecules made of?" you traverse composition index.

4. **For future extensions**:
   - Consider adding temporal hierarchy (timescale relationships)
   - Consider adding causal hierarchy (Pearl's observational/interventional/counterfactual)
   - Document whether these are aligned or orthogonal to the three main structures

This formalization removes conceptual confusion and allows future integrations cleanly.

## CONCLUDING META-COMMENTARY

This panel has done something useful: it has exposed the architecture of your taxonomy. The good news: you have built a coherent system with genuine structure. The bad news: some of that structure is theoretically unjustified or empirically underdeveloped.

The panelists disagree about many things (ontological status, whether FEP unifies, whether circuits are real types). But they agree on five core points:

1. Your AI-extracted T3 data needs validation against extraction bias.
2. Your T1 atoms mix empirical maturity levels inappropriately.
3. Your T1.5 boundary is currently sociological and needs structural grounding.
4. Your hierarchy conflates three distinct relations and should formalize their separation.
5. Your functional circuits need explicit acknowledgment of degeneracy and equivalence class nature.

If you address these five points, you will have a taxonomy with solid epistemological grounding and more honest ontological claims.

The remaining disagreements (whether levels are "real," whether archetypes are universality classes, whether composition is genuinely hierarchical) are open questions in philosophy of science. You cannot settle them with more data alone. But you can set up your system so that empirical discoveries could bear on them. That is the role of Tier 2 and Tier 3 recommendations.

Finally: this process has value beyond the specific recommendations. You have subjected your system to genuinely adversarial review by experts who understand mechanisms, causation, ontology, development, and empirical grounding. The fact that you can absorb this critique, revise systematically, and keep improving is a sign of scientific maturity. The taxonomy is not perfect. But it is perfectible. That is the best any knowledge system can do.
