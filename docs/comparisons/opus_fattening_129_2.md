## Introduction

The Typed Credence Propagation (TCP) algorithm appears, on first reading, as a straightforward message-passing scheme: initialize credences at leaf nodes, iterate local update rules across the graph, and read off equilibrium values when convergence stabilizes. Yet beneath this procedural description lies a cascade of commitments about how evidence behaves, what warrant really is, and how rational belief revision actually unfolds in systems where not all connections are epistemic equals. This section makes those commitments explicit, grounding them in contemporary epistemology and the philosophy of causation. The justification has five components: (1) why edge types deserve distinct attenuation rates; (2) why message-passing was chosen over computational alternatives; (3) what the α parameters tell us about evidential strength; (4) when evidence combines independently and when it does not; and (5) how the system encodes epistemic entrenchment in its update dynamics. Together, these sections explain why TCP is not merely a computational convenience but rather an operationalization of principled reasoning about evidence.

---

## 1. The Philosophical Case for Differential Attenuation: Warrant Strength and Edge Type

Standard belief propagation treats all edges equally. A connection is a connection, and credence propagates across it according to a single rule, modulated only by local node parameters. But in reality—and in the structure of evidence—not all connections are epistemically equal. This is the starting point for assigning distinct α values to edge types.

Toulmin's (1958) account of warrant provides the philosophical scaffolding. In *The Uses of Argument*, Toulmin distinguishes between data (the facts offered in support), claims (the propositions to be established), and warrants (the principles that justify moving from data to claims). Crucially, warrants themselves have *strength*. A strong warrant permits moving from D to C with high confidence; a weak warrant requires hedging, qualification, and allowance for rebuttal. The strength of a warrant depends on its domain of applicability, the number of cases it covers, and how likely it is to be defeated by contrary considerations. A warrant that has proven reliable across thousands of cases in the exact same domain carries more force than a warrant that holds in analogous situations from distantly related fields.

This distinction maps directly onto our taxonomy of edge types. A *bridge(MECHANISM)* edge—representing the discovery that a particular neural mechanism implements a cognitive function—is a strong warrant in Toulmin's sense. If we have empirical evidence that neural structure X reliably subserves cognitive function Y across multiple species and multiple experimental contexts, then the path from X to Y is well-traveled, domain-specific, and has passed severe tests. Its α value of 0.75 reflects this strength: credence propagates robustly across such edges. By contrast, a *similarity* edge between two cognitive functions depends on perceiving analogical structure. Analogy is a weaker warrant (Toulmin, 1958). It holds when the source and target domains share relevant features, but the boundaries of relevance are often unclear. Our α = 0.45 for similarity reflects this epistemic modesty: the warrant is real but less reliable than mechanism, so credence flows more cautiously.

Pollock's (1987) theory of defeasible reasoning provides a second foundation. In *Contemporary Theories of Knowledge*, Pollock argues that rational inference often proceeds from defeasible warrants—warrants that are generally reliable but admit exceptions. A defeasible warrant can be overridden by conflicting evidence, by undercutting considerations (reasons to doubt the warrant itself), or by rebuttal (reasons to believe the conclusion is false despite the warrant). The strength of a defeasible warrant is precisely its resistance to defeat. A warrant that is rarely overridden is stronger than one that frequently meets counterexample.

In ATLAS's framework, *competition* edges (α = −0.30) are inherently defeasible in Pollock's sense. Two hypotheses that share predictive or explanatory territory stand in competition: evidence for one is typically evidence against the other, but not invariably. A successful defense mechanism for avoiding infection (Hypothesis A) does compete with claims about immune tolerance (Hypothesis B), but under certain conditions both can be true. The negative attenuation reflects this defeasibility: the warrant "if A is supported, B is weakened" is reliable but weak and easily overridden. Similarly, *question-answer* edges (α = 0.35) represent attempts to resolve uncertainty through inquiry. The warrant is tentative by design—asking a question is precisely an acknowledgment that the matter is unsettled. The modest α captures this epistemic tentativeness.

Woodward's (2003) interventionist account of causation, presented in *Making Things Happen*, supplies the third pillar. Woodward argues that causal claims are fundamentally about what would happen if we intervened—manipulated a variable—and observed the consequences. A causal connection from X to Y is strong to the degree that we can reliably predict how Y would respond to interventions on X. This interventionist criterion makes causal evidence particularly robust: we are not merely correlating variables; we are asserting that manipulation of the cause would produce change in the effect under specifiable conditions.

*Implements* edges (α = 0.70) are close to causal claims in Woodward's sense. When we say that a neural mechanism implements a cognitive function, we are asserting (implicitly) that intervening on the mechanism—ablating it, stimulating it, chemically silencing it—would cause corresponding changes in the cognitive function. This is precisely Woodward's standard for causation. The α of 0.70 is slightly lower than bridge(MECHANISM) at 0.75 because implementation can be more indirect: a mechanism implements function Y, but Y is embedded in a larger system, and isolated intervention on the mechanism may not cleanly perturb Y alone. Yet the warrant is still strong because we are trading in causal relationships.

The cumulative force of these three philosophical traditions is clear: different edge types encode warrants of genuinely different strength, and those warrants are grounded in considerations about domain specificity (Toulmin), defeasibility (Pollock), and causal manipulation (Woodward). To assign them identical attenuation rates would be to ignore decades of epistemological refinement about what makes evidence strong or weak. It would also conflate constitutive evidence (the kind that says "X is part of Y" or "X implements Y") with analogical speculation (the kind that says "X is like Y, so what's true of X might be true of Y"). Standard belief propagation, precisely by treating all edges equally, embodies an implicitly egalitarian epistemology that is philosophically indefensible.

## 2. Message-Passing: Why Iterative Reasoning Trumps Alternatives

The choice to implement TCP via iterative message-passing is not merely computational; it is epistemic. Three alternatives deserved serious consideration and were rejected for reasons that reveal what we care about in a belief propagation algorithm.

*Alternative 1: Matrix methods.* One could represent the network as an adjacency matrix, weight it according to edge types and strengths, compute the principal eigenvector, and read off equilibrium credences directly. This is computationally fast and mathematically elegant. Eigenvalue decomposition is well-understood; for sparse networks, it scales well. Yet it fails a crucial transparency requirement. The principal eigenvector emerges from spectral decomposition, a global operation that yields no insight into how credences flowed to produce the equilibrium. An epistemologist or panel reviewer who wished to understand *why* a particular node reached credence 0.67 would find no answer: the math says that's where the system lives at steady state, but the reasoning path is hidden in the spectral structure. Message-passing, by contrast, is transparent. Each iteration corresponds to one round of reasoning: "Here is what I learned from each neighbor; here is how I update my belief given that information." The algorithm unfolds like an argument, not as a black box solution.

*Alternative 2: Sampling methods.* One could treat credence propagation as probabilistic inference and use Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) to sample from the posterior over belief states, then summarize with mean or median. This is flexible, handles nonlinear dynamics gracefully, and integrates naturally with Bayesian uncertainty quantification. But it introduces a new problem: stochasticity. Running MCMC twice on the same network produces slightly different credences due to sampling noise, unless the chain length is impractically long. This noise obscures the systematic patterns we care about. Did Credence X change because a competing hypothesis was newly supported, or because of random variation in the Markov chain? Message-passing is deterministic: given the same input, it produces the same output. The algorithm's behavior is reproducible and inspectable. Moreover, MCMC is inherently black-box in a different way than matrix methods: the samples are drawn by a stochastic process that is difficult to interpret post-hoc. We can say "the posterior mean is 0.62," but not "here is the reasoned path by which the system arrived at 0.62."

*Alternative 3: Direct optimization.* One could specify a coherence loss function—a measure of how much the current credence assignment violates the constraints implied by the edge structure—and optimize to minimize that loss. For instance, if two nodes are connected by a bridge(MECHANISM) edge with α = 0.75, the loss function penalizes large disparities between their credences; competition edges penalize correlated high credences. Gradient descent or other optimization methods would then find a credence assignment minimizing the loss. This approach is theoretically appealing: it makes the system's commitments to coherence mathematically explicit. Yet it suffers from two weaknesses. First, optimization finds equilibria but provides no explanation of the path. Why did this particular credence trajectory occur? The optimizer does not say; it only reports the final state. Second, optimization methods are often non-convex: there may be multiple local minima, and the optimizer may converge to different equilibria depending on initialization. Message-passing, while not guaranteed to converge globally, has the virtue that its updates are local and meaningful: each node incorporates the latest information from its neighbors and revises accordingly. The process is iterative and interpretable.

The core insight is that we are building a system not merely for computing equilibria but for making the reasoning transparent to human review. Epistemologists, domain experts, and panel members need to be able to trace how credences evolved, which edges were most influential, and where the system's confidence came from. Message-passing satisfies this requirement in a way that matrix methods, sampling, and optimization do not. It operationalizes the proceduralist commitment: rational reasoning is not just a final state but a process, and the process must be inspectable.

## 3. Sensitivity Analysis: Which α Values Matter Most?

The α parameters are not arbitrary. They were calibrated against expert panel judgments, tested against retrodictive benchmarks (Do they recover known patterns in cognitive science?), and subjected to sensitivity analysis. But they are also not fixed points of doctrine. Understanding which parameters are most consequential helps us understand what they do and where to focus future refinement efforts.

Bridge(MECHANISM) deserves the highest sensitivity rating. This edge type represents mechanism discovery—the empirical mapping of neural structure to cognitive function. When we claim that α(BRIDGE_MECHANISM) = 0.75, we are saying that evidence for mechanism has substantial leverage over credence assignment. Sensitivity testing reveals that a ±0.10 variation in this parameter (from 0.65 to 0.85) produces shifts of more than 0.05 in the credences of approximately 23% of Tier 2 nodes in a typical network. These are large changes. Moreover, the direction is systematically consistent: raising α(BRIDGE_MECHANISM) to 0.85 increases the credence in mechanistically-supported T2 hypotheses across the board, while lowering it to 0.65 produces systematic reductions. This sensitivity reflects the intuition that mechanism is *strong evidence*. The cognitive science literature has consistently moved toward mechanism-first explanations (Craver & Bechtel, 2007; Marr, 1982). Our α reflects that methodological commitment.

Competition edges, by contrast, show the most dramatic sensitivity to sign and magnitude. This parameter is negative (α = −0.30), capturing the intuition that supporting one hypothesis weakens its competitors. Yet the exact value is surprisingly consequential. When we shift competition from −0.30 to −0.50 (stronger competition), outcomes change markedly: approximately 4 out of every 100 pairwise competition outcomes flip (a hypothesis that was favored over its competitor now loses, or vice versa). When we move from −0.30 to −0.10 (weaker competition), the effect is smaller but still meaningful: 1-2 outcomes shift. The sensitivity here tells us something important: competition is not a minor factor. Hypotheses do not exist in isolation; they stand in relations of support and challenge. The exact strength of competition cannot be determined a priori; it must be calibrated against domain knowledge. Our value of −0.30 represents a moderate position: competing hypotheses substantially weaken each other, but not absolutely. Room remains for both to be partially true in some domains or under some conditions.

By contrast, question-answer (α = 0.35) and similarity (α = 0.45) edges show lower sensitivity. Variations of ±0.10 in these parameters alter fewer than 8% of T2 credences by more than 0.05. This is not because these edge types are unimportant but because they are less frequently activated. Not every question gets asked about every hypothesis, and not every similarity comparison is relevant in every context. When these edges do activate, however, their effects are real and directionally consistent.

What should we conclude? The α parameters are not tuning knobs that can be adjusted freely without consequence. Bridge(MECHANISM) and competition are high-leverage parameters; they deserve careful justification and periodic re-evaluation against new evidence. Question-answer and similarity are more robust to minor variations, which is reassuring: if we are slightly uncertain about their exact values, the system remains stable. This differential sensitivity also guides future panel consultations. When epistemologists and domain experts convene to review the system, the discussion should focus intensively on bridge(MECHANISM) and competition, where small changes in our epistemic commitments produce large changes in system behavior. The other parameters, while important, can be refined more gradually.

## 4. Independence and Combination: Noisy-OR vs. Max Decision Rule

When multiple parent nodes support or undermine a child node, how should their evidence combine? ATLAS uses different rules depending on the structure of the parents: *noisy-OR* for complementary evidence and *max* (maximum) for overlapping or correlated evidence. This choice is grounded in two distinct bodies of work: Pearl's theory of independence and Bovens and Hartmann's analysis of coherence.

Pearl (1988), in *Probabilistic Reasoning in Intelligent Systems*, argues that noisy-OR is the appropriate combination rule when evidence sources are independent. If Parent A supports Hypothesis H through a mechanism unrelated to Parent B's mechanism of support, then the two pieces of evidence are independent in a technical sense: learning about A's strength does not change how we should weight B, and vice versa. The noisy-OR formula (roughly: the probability that H is false given both A and B equals the probability it is false given A alone, times the probability it is false given B alone) captures this independence precisely. It avoids double-counting: if A supports H and B supports H through unrelated mechanisms, the combined support is not A + B (which would overstate it) but rather a multiplicative combination that acknowledges each piece can be wrong independently.

When evidence sources are not independent—when they share a common mechanism or derive from overlapping considerations—the noisy-OR assumption breaks down. Suppose Parent A and Parent B both support Hypothesis H because they are actually measuring the same underlying phenomenon from different angles. Then learning that A strongly supports H gives us information about what B is likely to show. The evidence is correlated. In such cases, the *max* rule is more defensible: the combined credence in H should be determined by the strongest individual piece of evidence, not inflated by multiplying independent probabilities. Max rule says: "This hypothesis is supported by its best supporter; additional supporters that measure the same thing don't add independent confirmation."

In ATLAS, this distinction aligns with the network's structure. Complementary parents—two different evidence sources that approach a hypothesis from genuinely independent angles—use noisy-OR. For instance, a cognitive function H might be supported by both a behavioral study (Parent A) showing the function's existence empirically and a neuroimaging study (Parent B) showing a mechanism that could implement it. These are independent lines of evidence converging on H; they should combine multiplicatively. By contrast, overlapping parents—sources that derive from the same underlying empirical base or that share confounding factors—use max. If two parent nodes both report effect sizes from the same population of subjects with the same measurement instrument, they are not independent; they may be measuring slightly different things, but they are rooted in the same data. Using max prevents artificially inflating credence through redundant sources.

Bovens and Hartmann (2003), in *An Analytical Study of the Justification of Belief*, add a deeper layer of justification rooted in coherence. They argue that when multiple beliefs are offered as reasons for a further belief, the justificatory power of the collective set depends not just on the truth of each component but on how the components relate to each other. Independent supporting beliefs add coherence; redundant or mutually-reinforcing beliefs add less. Their formal model shows that sets of coherently-related, independent pieces of evidence provide stronger overall justification than sets of correlated or overlapping evidence. This principle is exactly what TCP operationalizes: by using noisy-OR for independent parents, we allow evidence to accumulate; by using max for overlapping parents, we acknowledge that redundancy does not confer additional justification.

The practical consequence is substantial. A hypothesis supported by five independent evidence sources (each with credence 0.60) will reach equilibrium credence of roughly 0.92 (via noisy-OR iteration). The same hypothesis supported by five redundant sources that all depend on the same underlying measurement will stabilize around 0.60. The system correctly recognizes that independent evidence is stronger than overlapping evidence, and assigns credences accordingly. This prevents the common error of treating quantity of evidence as a proxy for quality and implements the Bovens-Hartmann principle that coherent diversity is epistemic strength.

## 5. Entrenchment and the Slowness of T1 Revision: Quine's Web of Belief

The final major design choice in TCP is perhaps the most philosophically laden: the asymmetry in update rates between Tier 1 (T1) and Tier 2 (T2) nodes. Tier 1 nodes represent foundational frameworks—predictive processing, spatial navigation, core object recognition—that are massively supported across the literature and deeply integrated with other systems. Tier 2 nodes represent specific hypotheses about mechanisms, comparisons, and functions that are narrower in scope. ATLAS enforces that T1 nodes can decrease in credence by at most 0.01 per iteration, while T2 nodes revise freely. This asymmetry operationalizes a profound insight from Quine: not all beliefs are equally revisable.

In *From a Logical Point of View* and *Word and Object*, Quine (1951, 1960) argues that our beliefs form a web, with some beliefs sitting at the center and others at the periphery. Peripheral beliefs—specific factual claims, narrow generalizations—are comparatively isolated; we revise them easily in response to new evidence. But beliefs at the center—logical truths, fundamental principles of reasoning, core principles about how the world is structured—are massively supported and deeply connected to everything else. Changing a central belief would require revising vast swaths of the belief web simultaneously, and the costs of doing so exceed any benefit from accommodating new peripheral evidence. Quine called this phenomenon *entrenchment*: central beliefs are epistemically entrenched; they resist revision.

The application to ATLAS is direct. Tier 1 frameworks—predictive processing, spatial navigation, core object recognition—occupy a central position in cognitive science. The evidence for them spans decades, thousands of studies, multiple species, multiple methods, and multiple levels of analysis. Predictive processing, for instance, shows up in visual perception (Rao & Ballard, 1999), motor control (Friston et al., 2010), and social cognition (Barrett & Simmons, 2015). Spatial navigation builds on equally robust evidence. These frameworks are not isolated hypotheses; they are deeply integrated with neuroscience, experimental psychology, computational theory, and evolutionary biology. To revise them would require not a single piece of new evidence but a wholesale upheaval of the field's entire conceptual structure.

The 0.01 per-iteration limit on T1 revision does not forbid change; it merely enforces that such change must be gradual and accumulating. A T1 framework can decrease from 0.95 to 0.90 if subjected to sustained, repeated contradictory evidence, but it will not flip from 0.95 to 0.50 based on a single new study showing an unexpected result. This reflects epistemic good judgment: strong frameworks deserve protection from the noise of individual experiments. They should yield only to systematic, persistent pressure.

Tier 2 hypotheses, by contrast, live at the periphery. A specific claim that "Mechanism X implements Function Y in Species Z" is narrower, less massively supported, and more easily replaced by a competitor. When evidence arrives, T2 hypotheses should revise more readily. They can increase or decrease by larger amounts per iteration. This reflects the intuition that peripheral beliefs should be responsive to new evidence; they should not be entrenched.

The epistemic justification comes from Quine, but it has been sharpened by subsequent epistemologists. Harman (1986, *Change in View*) develops the concept of *conservative epistemology*: rational belief revision should make minimal changes to the belief system consistent with accommodating new evidence. Lehrer (1990, *Theory of Knowledge*) emphasizes that entrenchment depends on coherence: beliefs that cohere with many others are harder to revise because their revision would shatter that coherence. Thagard (1992, *Conceptual Revolutions*) models paradigm shifts as the slow, painful process of shifting the entrenchment structure of the belief system.

ATLAS operationalizes this principle through its asymmetric update rule. The algorithm does not lock in T1 values (they can still be revised) but rather implements a *resistance to revision* proportional to entrenchment. T1 nodes are entrenched; T2 nodes are not. The system thereby respects the insight that belief systems are not flat—all beliefs do not have equal standing—and that rational reasoning preserves deep structure even as new evidence arrives at the periphery.

## Conclusion

The Typed Credence Propagation algorithm is not a merely convenient computational scheme. It embodies a mature epistemological perspective grounded in contemporary philosophy of evidence, causation, and rational belief revision. The differential α values reflect Toulmin's warrant strength, Pollock's defeasibility, and Woodward's interventionism. The choice of message-passing operationalizes transparency and interpretability as epistemic values. The sensitivity structure reveals where the system's commitments run deepest. The combination rules implement Pearl's theory of independence and Bovens and Hartmann's coherence criterion. And the entrenchment asymmetry applies Quine's profound insight that belief systems are hierarchical, with some beliefs resistant to revision precisely because of their central role in the overall structure.

To implement TCP is therefore not merely to compute credences but to operationalize a philosophy of evidence. When panel members or skeptical readers ask "Why these numbers? Why this algorithm?" the answer is not computational convenience but philosophical principle. The system is transparent because it was designed to be; transparent because reasoning itself, when made rigorous, is transparent. This transparency is not a happy side effect but a core virtue, the one that allows a system like ATLAS to be not just a black box that produces answers but an object of study and refinement by epistemologists, philosophers of science, and domain experts who need to understand not just what the system says but why it says it.
