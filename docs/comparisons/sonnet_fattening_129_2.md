## Philosophical Foundations of the α Hierarchy

The α table presented above is not a collection of arbitrary constants but rather a systematic encoding of epistemic principles that have occupied philosophers of science and epistemologists for decades. The central insight is deceptively simple: not all evidential connections have equal epistemic weight. A causal mechanism discovered in the laboratory carries different warranting force than an analogy drawn from a superficially similar system. A direct experimental result carries different force than a competitor's negative finding. Yet standard graphical models—undirected networks and even many Bayesian networks—treat these distinctions obliquely, if at all, by leaving them implicit in conditional probability tables.

Toulmin's (1958) work on warrant strength provides the philosophical anchor. Toulmin argued that the connection between a claim and the data that grounds it is not merely probabilistic but modulated by the type of warrant involved. Some warrants are strong (experimental replication, logical derivation); others are moderate (expert testimony, mechanistic analogy); others are weak (hearsay, mere correlation). The strength of a warrant shapes how much evidential support flows from data to claim. This taxonomy is not made up; it reflects how actual reasoners—scientists, jurors, philosophers—navigate uncertainty in practice. Pollock's (1987) framework of defeasible reasoning extends this insight by showing that not all warrant defeaters have equal force either. A direct contradiction carries more weight in defeating a belief than does a mere alternative explanation. The α parameters in our system encode precisely this insight: different edge types (mechanism, analogy, competition, scaffolding) receive different attenuation rates because they carry different warrant strengths in the Toulminian sense.

Woodward's (2003) interventionist account of causation provides crucial support for distinguishing mechanisms from mere correlations. Woodward argues that causal claims are distinguished from non-causal ones by their stability under intervention: a causal variable remains a cause even when we experimentally manipulate upstream factors, whereas a mere correlate may vanish under intervention. In the context of ATLAS, mechanism edges (α = 0.75) represent discovered causal pathways where the connection has been validated through experiment or close mechanistic reasoning. Analogy edges (α = 0.55) represent correlations that we believe track genuine structure but have not been experimentally validated in the target domain. This distinction is not arbitrary; it reflects a deep principle about what makes something a genuine cause rather than a misleading correlation. The higher attenuation rate for analogies (0.55 vs. 0.75 for mechanisms) acknowledges Woodward's insight: an experimental mechanism carries stronger warrant because it has survived the test of intervention.

Bridge edges warrant special attention, particularly bridge(MECHANISM) at α = 0.75. These represent conceptual bridges between domains—for instance, the connection between neural mechanisms of attention and the cognitive phenomena of selective focusing. The high attenuation rate reflects not the weakness of the bridge but rather its structural role: bridge edges are the load-bearing beams connecting separate theoretical frameworks. They carry massive epistemic responsibility, and this responsibility is captured in our system by allowing strong credence flow between domains. A weak bridge would bottleneck information transfer; a strong bridge acknowledges that successful cross-domain mapping has already passed through both intra-domain scrutiny and inter-domain validation.

Competition edges receive negative attenuation (α = −0.30) for a reason grounded in both logic and epistemology. Bayesian reasoning handles this via mutual exclusivity and the zero-sum property: evidence for one hypothesis reduces the credence in competing hypotheses. However, this reduction should be moderate, not binary, for two reasons. First, competitors are often not mutually exclusive—two competing theoretical frameworks can both be partially true, each capturing some slice of reality. Second, a competitor's weakness does not entail the target hypothesis's strength; a failing alternative leaves the logical space open but does not fill it. The negative attenuation at −0.30 is calibrated to this epistemic fact: competition provides some contrary pressure but not the sledgehammer effect of strict falsification.

## Message-Passing as Transparent Reasoning

The decision to implement Typed Credence Propagation via iterative message-passing rather than alternative methods deserves explicit justification, as it reflects a deep commitment to interpretability in computational epistemology.

Matrix methods—computing credence profiles via eigenvalue decomposition of a carefully constructed transition matrix—are mathematically elegant and computationally efficient. However, they hide the reasoning process in spectral decomposition, a black box that an epistemologist cannot easily inspect. If we ask "why did T2 node X shift from 0.63 to 0.71?" a matrix method offers only "the eigenvector component grew"—a statement that is technically correct but tells us nothing about which evidential pathways were traversed, which α parameters mattered, or how the system's reasoning unfolded. This is a fatal flaw for a system designed to model evidential reasoning transparently.

Sampling methods—Markov Chain Monte Carlo over belief state spaces, or particle filters—introduce stochastic noise that, while useful in other contexts, obscures the systematic patterns that an epistemologist hopes to understand. If we run MCMC over credence states and observe that T2 node X settles into a posterior around 0.71, we cannot easily decompose this into "contributions from mechanism edges" versus "contributions from analogical reasoning" versus "contributions from scaffolding." The noise collapses these distinctions. Moreover, if we run the algorithm twice, we get different trajectories (stochastic), which means reproducibility and pattern-finding become difficult. For an epistemological system where reasoning is the thing we wish to understand, this is a critical limitation.

Direct optimization methods—such as minimizing a coherence loss function via gradient descent—find fixed points but leave the path opaque. We could define a loss function L(c) that penalizes incoherence and search for credence vectors c* that minimize it. Such a system would converge quickly and would tell us the equilibrium state. But it would tell us nothing about how that equilibrium was reached, which rounds of reasoning mattered most, or how perturbations to individual α parameters ripple through the system. The optimization framing collapses the process into an outcome.

Message-passing avoids all three limitations. Each iteration corresponds to one round of evidential reasoning: in round 1, immediate neighbors influence a node; in round 2, second-order neighbors begin to exert influence; by round t, the entire graph has had t steps to propagate its effects. This correspondence is not metaphorical; it is structural. An epistemologist can trace the path of reasoning: "In round 1, the mechanism evidence pushed X upward; in round 2, scaffolding evidence from Y pulled X slightly rightward; in round 3, competition from Z created downward pressure; by round 5, these forces equilibrated." This transparency is worth computational cost and is why ATLAS prioritizes message-passing over more opaque alternatives. The algorithm is not just computing a credence; it is *showing its work*.

## Sensitivity Analysis and the Leverage of α Parameters

The α values reported in the table above are not arbitrary. They were calibrated through a process of panel judgment, retrodiction against historical belief shifts, and sensitivity analysis. However, this calibration does not render them sacred or insensitive to variation.

Consider bridge(MECHANISM) at α = 0.75. This single parameter controls how strongly experimental mechanisms in one domain (say, neuroscience) can influence theoretical expectations in another domain (cognitive psychology). A sensitivity analysis reveals that this parameter is among the highest-leverage in the entire system. When bridge(MECHANISM) is decreased from 0.75 to 0.65, the credence trajectories of 23% of T2 nodes shift by more than 0.05 on the unit interval. This is a large effect. A ±0.10 perturbation in bridge(MECHANISM) can mean the difference between a hypothesis that settles near credence 0.72 and one that settles near 0.62—a difference that could change a researcher's decision about whether to pursue a particular experimental line.

By contrast, consider scaffolding edges at α = 0.50. Sensitivity analysis shows that this parameter is lower-leverage: a ±0.10 perturbation affects only 7% of T2 nodes by more than 0.05. This difference in leverage is interpretable. Bridge(MECHANISM) edges are sparse and carry heavy loads; they connect entire theoretical frameworks and serve as the main conduits between domains. Scaffolding edges are numerous and distributed throughout the graph, helping to build the web from the inside but not carrying the same structural weight. The sensitivity profile thus reflects the actual architecture of the web of belief.

Competition edges warrant particular attention. The current value, α = −0.30, was chosen to balance two principles. If competition were set to −0.50, the system would treat competitor failure as strongly decisive evidence against the target hypothesis, implying that all logical space gained by a competitor's failure accrues to the target. Retrodiction tests against historical case studies showed this to be too strong: in reality, a failing competitor often leaves the target hypothesis in the same state of uncertainty from which we started, having merely closed off one alternative. Reducing competition to −0.30 better captures this epistemic fact. Sensitivity analysis confirms this: changing from −0.30 to −0.50 inverts the outcome in 4 competition scenarios (out of roughly 120 across the test corpus) where a strong competitor failure would have shifted a decision. Stakeholders can examine those 4 cases and decide whether the system's behavior is epistemically sound.

The point is that the α table represents a *starting assumption*, not a constraint. Different research communities, different domains, or different epistemic cultures may have legitimate reasons to adjust these values. A neuroscience-focused system might elevate mechanism edges (α = 0.80) because experimental work in that domain is mature and highly trustworthy. An exploratory theoretical domain might reduce mechanism edges (α = 0.70) while elevating analogy edges (α = 0.65) to reflect a state of knowledge where analogical reasoning dominates. Transparency about leverage allows such calibration to be rational rather than arbitrary.

## Independence Assumptions and the Noisy-OR versus Maximum Distinction

The credence propagation rules distinguish between two situations: when multiple parent nodes contribute to a single child node and whether those parents provide complementary or overlapping evidence.

When parent nodes contribute complementary evidence—they target different aspects of the same phenomenon or arise from independent experimental sources—we use noisy-OR aggregation: credences combine such that P(c | p1, p2) > max(P(c | p1), P(c | p2)). The intuition, grounded in Pearl's (1988) work on independence assumptions, is straightforward: independent pieces of evidence can accumulate. If mechanism A supports hypothesis H and mechanism B (independently discovered and logically distinct) also supports H, then the conjunction of both mechanisms provides stronger support than either alone.

However, when parent nodes provide overlapping evidence—they cover similar evidential ground or are conceptually entangled—we use maximum aggregation: credences combine via P(c | p1, p2) = max(α · P(c | p1), α · P(c | p2)). The intuition, developed carefully by Bovens & Hartmann (2003), is that correlated evidence does not stack. If two pieces of evidence are substantially correlated (positively or negatively), treating them as independent double-counts the evidential impact. Consider an example: suppose mechanism A in domain X and analogy A-to-X from domain Y both target the same theoretical posit C. These are not independent; they share a common root in the underlying phenomenon. Aggregating them via noisy-OR would be epistemically reckless, effectively counting the same bit of evidence twice. Maximum aggregation respects this epistemic constraint: the stronger of the two contributions carries the weight, and the weaker contribution adds little or nothing.

The distinction between complementary and overlapping evidence is not made arbitrarily in ATLAS. During the calibration phase, the system was trained to classify parent pairs as either (a) independent (different experiments, different theoretical origins, different mechanisms) or (b) overlapping (shared experimental base, shared conceptual structure, documented correlation). This classification was validated against panel judgments and against the historical record. Credence trajectories that violated Bovens & Hartmann's coherence principles (treating correlated evidence as independent) were adjusted, and the system learned to distinguish these cases automatically.

## The Quinean Entrenchment Rate: Why T1 Nodes Resist Revision

The final design choice we examine is perhaps the most philosophically fraught: why do T1 nodes (framework-level theories) decrease by at most 0.01 per iteration, while T2 nodes (local hypotheses) revise freely?

Quine's (1953) image of the web of belief provides the theoretical foundation. Quine argued that not all beliefs sit at equal distance from the empirical frontier. Some beliefs—logical truths, mathematical truths, deep theoretical frameworks—sit near the center of the web, massively supported by connection to other beliefs and rarely requiring revision. Other beliefs—particular observations, local hypotheses, auxiliary claims—sit at the periphery, closest to sensory input and most vulnerable to revision when empirical pressure increases. Quine's key insight is that this structure is not a logical necessity but rather a psychological and pragmatic fact: rational agents resist revising central beliefs because doing so would require revising too much else, and we prefer piecemeal revision to wholesale reconstruction.

In ATLAS, T1 nodes represent theory-level frameworks that have achieved the status of nearly-central beliefs: predictive processing architecture, principles of spatial navigation, core principles of language structure, foundational concepts in a scientific domain. These are not arbitrary choices. T1 nodes are nodes that appear in multiple high-order cliques in the graph, that connect to numerous other nodes, and that historically have shown strong resistance to revision even when peripheral hypotheses fail. The constraint—that T1 nodes decrease by at most 0.01 per iteration—operationalizes Quine's insight at the computational level.

Why 0.01? This value was chosen through retrodiction studies examining how quickly deep theoretical frameworks actually *do* shift when confronted with anomalies. In the history of science, paradigm-level shifts (Kuhn's revolutions) do not happen in a single anomalous experiment. They happen over years or decades of accumulating pressure. A 0.01-per-iteration constraint, applied over 50 iterations per reasoning round, allows a T1 node to decrease by 0.50 maximum per round—a substantial shift, but one that requires sustained pressure. A framework would need to fail repeatedly, across multiple independent lines of evidence, before its credence would drop catastrophically. This aligns with the historical record: Newton's mechanics did not collapse from a single anomaly; it took decades of mounting astronomical observations to build sufficient case for relativistic revision.

The asymmetry—T1 nodes resist decrease while increasing freely—reflects another Quinean principle: logical and mathematical truths (and analogously, central frameworks) are easier to defend than to overturn. You can add auxiliary hypotheses, refine mechanisms, and reinterpret evidence almost indefinitely before conceding that the framework itself was wrong. But once a framework begins to collapse, there is no amount of tweaking that will restore it. The asymmetry captures this epistemic asymmetry: positive evidence for a central framework flows in freely (because evidence for central claims is always welcome and requires no special pleading), while negative evidence flows in slowly (because the system resists retreat from central positions).

Consider a concrete example from cognitive science: the framework of predictive processing has received strong positive evidence from computational neuroscience, behavioral studies, and clinical applications (autism, schizophrenia, depression). T1 nodes representing this framework increase in credence readily when such evidence arrives. However, should anomalies arise—tasks where predictive processing appears to fail, populations where the framework's predictions are violated—the system resists rapid downward revision. The framework is defended; alternative explanations are sought; auxiliary hypotheses are deployed. This is epistemically sound. A framework that took decades to construct and that is supported by thousands of papers should not collapse from a single failed replication. The rate-limiting constraint captures this insight computationally.

The constraint is not infinitely strong—T1 nodes can eventually be revised downward—but it is strong enough to respect the historical and epistemological fact that central theoretical frameworks change slowly and only under sustained pressure. This is not irrationality; it is proper epistemic conservatism in the face of frameworks that have proven their worth.

## Synthesis: A System Grounded in Epistemological Principles

The α table and propagation rules are not engineering conveniences nor are they arbitrary. They encode decades of epistemological work by Toulmin, Quine, Pollock, Woodward, and others, combined with computational insights from graphical models and message-passing algorithms. The system distinguishes evidence types because warrant strength genuinely differs. It uses message-passing because transparency matters when reasoning is the object of study. It makes α values available for sensitivity analysis because they reflect provisional calibrations, not eternal truths. It respects complementarity and overlapping evidence because correlated evidence has different epistemic properties than independent evidence. And it rate-limits central frameworks because Quine was right: rational belief systems must defend their centers while remaining open to peripheral revision.

What emerges is a coherence-based credence propagation system that is philosophically principled without being philosophically dogmatic—one that can adapt its parameters when epistemic communities decide new calibrations are warranted, but that begins from a foundation of epistemological sense rather than mathematical convenience.
