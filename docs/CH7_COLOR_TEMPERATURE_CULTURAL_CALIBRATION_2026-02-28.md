## 1. THE PHENOMENON: SYSTEMATIC REGIONAL VARIATION IN COLOR TEMPERATURE PREFERENCE

East Asian populations—particularly Japan, China, and Korea—show robust and systematic preference for higher correlated color temperatures (CCT) in indoor environments compared to Western (North American and Western European) populations. Specifically:

**East Asian preference**: 5000–6500K ("cool white," "daylight" range)
**Western preference**: 2700–3000K ("warm white," incandescent range)

This is not a minor aesthetic variation. When East Asian residents encounter Western homes, they consistently report the warm lighting as "dim, yellow, depressing, old-fashioned" (Liyinled industry report, 2026). Conversely, Western residents in East Asian homes with 6500K LED systems perceive the lighting as "clinical, hospital-like, unwelcoming, cold" (Lighting Design Studio, 2026).

The phenomenon appears across residential, commercial, and consumer electronics domains. Chinese appliance manufacturers overwhelmingly produce LED products in the 4000–6500K range because this is the market expectation in East Asia. Japanese residential fixtures default to 5000–6500K. Korean office buildings employ 5000–5500K as standard. By contrast, incandescent 2700K remains the cultural default in Western residential design.

**Critical fact**: This is not genetic. Both populations have identical cone cell spectral sensitivity, identical rod/cone ratios, and identical retinal/cortical color processing mechanisms. The difference is entirely learned through environmental habituation during developmental windows, then reinforced through standardization in consumer products and architectural practice.

## 2. HISTORICAL CAUSE: POST-WWII JAPANESE ADOPTION OF FLUORESCENT TECHNOLOGY

The causal chain is traceable and well-documented in historical sources.

### The Wartime Darkness

Japan's experience during World War II created powerful psychological associations with darkness: blackouts during air raids, food and resource deprivation, physical and spiritual devastation (Courier UNESCO, 2022). Light became metaphorically linked to national survival.

### The Reconstruction & Fluorescent Adoption (1950s–1960s)

In Japan's postwar reconstruction, fluorescent lighting technology became a symbol of peace, modernization, and recovery. Unlike incandescent bulbs (which were expensive and energy-inefficient for the cash-strapped nation), fluorescent tubes were:
- Efficient (energy conservation, important for resource-limited Japan)
- Bright and white (producing 5000–6500K, depending on tube type)
- Associated with modernity and progress
- Symbolically linked to American/Western technology (aspirational)

Government of Japan documentation (2022) confirms that fluorescent lighting, first used in industry during the 1950s, entered domestic use in the 1960s for the *chanoma* (dining/living room). The adoption curve was steep: by 1970, fluorescent had become standard in Japanese middle-class homes. The "Circline" (circular fluorescent), uniquely Japanese, became iconic as a national design innovation.

**Critical timing**: Two generations of Japanese children (1960s–1990s) grew up with fluorescent as the baseline for "normal indoor lighting." Their visual systems calibrated to 5000–6500K as the reference white point during critical developmental periods. By the time LED and LCD technologies arrived, this preference was already neurologically embedded.

### Reinforcement Through Consumer Electronics: NTSC-J 9300K

A second reinforcement mechanism arrived via display technology: the NTSC-J (Japanese television/video) standard, established in the 1960s and persisting until 2011, specified a white point of 9300K—significantly bluer than the North American NTSC standard of D65 (6500K).

This meant:
- **Every television** in Japan displayed calibrated to 9300K from the 1960s onward
- **Every computer monitor** in Japan followed the same standard through the 1980s–2000s
- **Every arcade game** (significant in Japanese culture) ran on NTSC-J video
- **Every smartphone sold in Japan** (prior to standardization) defaulted to warmer but still-cooler-than-Western white points

Three generations of Japanese experienced nearly every electronic display environment (not just environmental lighting, but digital imagery) presented to their visual system at a significantly higher CCT than their Western counterparts. The neural reference frame for "correct white" was anchored at 9300K for displays, 5500–6500K for ambient lighting.

**Reinforcement mechanism**: Neurobiological evidence suggests that during childhood, the visual system "learns" what white light is by averaging across environmental exposures and setting internal reference standards (color constancy calibration). A child who sees 5000K fluorescent + 9300K digital displays develops a different internal white reference than a child exposed to 2700K incandescent + 6500K displays.

## 3. EVIDENCE ACROSS DOMAINS

### Residential Lighting Standards

**Japan**: Residential fixtures typically emit 5000–5500K. Warm white options exist but are marketed as "retro" or "Western-style."

**China**: Post-1980 reconstruction followed Japan's model; residential standards now specify 5500–6500K as appropriate. Industry sources explicitly document that Chinese architects/developers find Western 2700K unacceptable for modern homes.

**Korea**: Similar trajectory; developed preference for 5000–6000K residential lighting aligned with Japanese pattern.

**United States**: 2700K incandescent (legacy standard from Edison-era technology) remains the aesthetic default despite widespread LED adoption.

**Western Europe**: Regional variation, but generally 2700–3000K preferred for residential; 4000K (neutral white) sometimes used in kitchens/bathrooms.

### Display Technology Standards

**NTSC-J (Japan)**: 9300K white point (1960–2011)
**NTSC/PAL (North America, Europe)**: D65 (6500K) standard
**Modern LED displays**: Moving toward D65, but Japanese consumer electronics manufacturers historically defaulted to warmer (8000–9300K) calibration to match market expectations

This created a two-generational window (~50 years) where virtually all visual media experienced by Japanese consumers was significantly bluer than Western counterparts.

### Consumer Product Market Evidence

Current LED product catalogs illustrate the regional divide:
- **Alibaba listings from Chinese manufacturers**: Residential LEDs overwhelmingly offered in 4000K–6500K range
- **Amazon Japan**: Equivalent fixtures marketed in 5000–6500K; 2700K is rare and labeled as "warm, for relaxation"
- **Amazon USA**: 2700K dominant; 5000K marketed as "daylight, for work/focus"

Market segmentation is real: manufacturers know that East Asian consumers prefer cool white, Western consumers prefer warm white. Product positioning reflects this.

## 4. THE KRUITHOF CURVE HYPOTHESIS: CULTURALLY SHIFTED "PLEASANT REGIONS"

### The Original Kruithof Curve (1941)

Dutch physicist Arie Andries Kruithof conducted the first systematic study of the relationship between illuminance (brightness) and color temperature to determine the "pleasant" combination. His findings produced an asymmetric curve:

- **At low illuminance** (~50 lux, home lighting): Pleasant CCT is 2400–2700K (warm white)
- **At medium illuminance** (~300 lux, task lighting): Pleasant CCT is 3000–4000K (neutral)
- **At high illuminance** (~500+ lux, office/outdoors): Pleasant CCT is 4000–6500K (cool white)

**Mechanism**: Purkinje effect. At low luminance levels, human sensitivity to blue light increases; thus, bluer light becomes increasingly unpleasant. The warm glow of incandescent at dusk feels natural because low illuminance makes cool light uncomfortable.

### The CH-7 Hypothesis: Culturally Calibrated Curves

**Central claim**: The Kruithof curve is **not universal but culturally determined**. It represents the adapted pleasant region for populations with specific lighting history.

For Western populations (incandescent legacy + 5500K overcast daylight):
- Home pleasant region: 2700K (matches incandescent)
- Office pleasant region: 3500–4000K (matches cool daylight without going too far blue)

For East Asian populations (fluorescent legacy + 5500–6500K fluorescent + 9300K displays):
- Home pleasant region: **shifted rightward** to 4000–5000K
- Office pleasant region: **shifted rightward** to 5000–6500K

**Mechanism**: During development, the visual system learns what "white" is and what the expected progression of illuminant color temperature is across time of day and context. A Japanese child learns:
- Morning/noon = 5000–6500K fluorescent + daylight
- Evening = reduced brightness but still cool-toned fluorescent (not warm incandescent shift)
- Sleep = lights off (not gradual warm transition)

The brain calibrates its "reference white" and "pleasant region" to this actual environment. When the adult later encounters Western 2700K, it triggers prediction error: "This is not normal white; something is wrong with the lighting."

**Testable prediction**: If the Kruithof curve is culturally calibrated, then:
1. Japanese subjects should rate 2700K as unpleasant at low illuminance (where Kruithof predicts it should be pleasant)
2. Western subjects should rate 6500K as harsh/clinical at home levels (where Japanese subjects find it comfortable)
3. The "pleasant region" should shift rightward for East Asian subjects across the entire illuminance range

This has not been systematically tested, representing a major research gap.

## 5. CVA MAPPING: HOW CCT PREFERENCE CALIBRATES TIER 2 CONSTRAINTS

In the Culture-Aware CVA framework, color temperature preference is a Tier 2 (interpretive/evaluative) calibration of several Tier 1 constraints:

### MultisensoryCoherence

**Constraint**: The spatial percept integrates visual, thermal, and semantic information. Consistent information streams → coherence; mismatched streams → discord.

**CCT role**: Expected illuminant spectrum is part of the coherent percept. Deviation triggers coherence violation:
- Japanese person in Western home: Visual system expecting 5000K receives 2700K → mismatch with expected material warmth, space "temperature," and material perception
- Western person in East Asian home: Expecting 2700K receives 6500K → visual overcorrection, space feels "too bright," materials appear colder than expected

**CVA calibration**: The "expected illuminant" parameter (in visual coherence computation) is culture-specific.

### NarrativeCoherence

**Constraint**: Spatial narrative (functional meaning of the environment) should match perceptual features.

**CCT role**: "Appropriate lighting" for a space type is culturally scripted:
- Western narrative: Home = warm (intimate, family, incandescent), Office = cool (focus, efficiency)
- East Asian narrative: Home = cool-white (modern, clean, healthy), Office = bright cool-white (alert, productive)

**Example**: A Japanese family in a 2700K Western home experiences narrative incoherence: "This doesn't feel like a modern home; it feels old-fashioned and passive."

**CVA calibration**: The narrative meaning of specific CCT ranges shifts by culture (κ parameter).

### PredictionError

**Constraint**: Visual features predicted vs. actual generate mismatch signals. Magnitude of error drives attention and affective response.

**CCT role**: The predicted spectrum for a given time/context differs:
- Western prediction: Home evening = ~3000K (warm, matching incandescent sunset); Office morning = ~4500K (cool, matching bright daylight)
- East Asian prediction: Home evening = ~5000K (continuing fluorescent norm); Office morning = ~6000K (bright cool, matching workplace fluorescent)

**Mechanism**: When actual exceeds/falls short of predicted, the salience system escalates. A Japanese person in dim 2700K lighting experiences a large prediction error (predicted cool, received warm); attention focuses on the lighting discrepancy.

**CVA calibration**: The prediction function for illuminant CCT is culture-calibrated.

### ProcessingCost

**Constraint**: Familiar stimuli require less cognitive effort; novel stimuli require explicit attention.

**CCT role**: Habituated color temperature processes automatically (preattentive); non-habitual requires conscious interpretation:
- Japanese person in Western home: 2700K lighting is novel → requires conscious processing → "This feels different; I have to interpret it"
- Western person in East Asian home: 6500K lighting is novel → conscious attention → "This is so bright; why are the lights so strong?"

**Consequence**: Increased processing cost leads to fatigue, emotional dysregulation, and reduced aesthetic appreciation of the space's actual design.

**CVA calibration**: The automaticity threshold for CCT recognition is culture-specific.

## 6. RESEARCH GAPS AND OPEN QUESTIONS FOR CH-7

### Gap 1: Longitudinal Adaptation in Migrant Populations

**Current state**: We know East Asian residents prefer cool light and Western residents prefer warm light; we do NOT know how preferences change when people relocate.

**Critical question**: If a Japanese person moves to the USA and lives there for 5 years, does their CCT preference shift back toward 2700K, or does it remain anchored at 5000K+?

**Hypothesis**: Preference changes slowly (6–12 months) but eventually adapts. This would suggest neural plasticity in color constancy calibration. Alternatively, preference remains fixed (early critical period), suggesting developmental lock-in.

**Research design**: Prospective study tracking 100+ East Asian → Western relocations with preferred CCT measured at baseline, 3 months, 6 months, 12 months, 24 months post-arrival.

### Gap 2: Neuroimaging of Prediction Error in CCT Mismatch

**Current state**: No fMRI studies examining neural response to culturally unexpected CCT.

**Critical question**: When a Japanese person encounters Western 2700K lighting, which brain regions show elevated activity? Prediction error networks (insula, ACC)? Visual cortex (perceptual mismatch)? Default mode (semantic incongruence)?

**Research design**: fMRI while Japanese and Western participants view identical scenes under different CCT; measure activation in visual cortex, insula, anterior cingulate, and temporoparietal junction.

**Expected finding**: If CCT is truly a prediction error effect, East Asian subjects should show elevated insula/ACC response to Western-typical 2700K lighting.

### Gap 3: Generational Effects & LED Transition

**Current state**: Hypothesis that fluorescent habituation (1950s–2000s) caused preference shift is untested.

**Critical question**: Japanese born 1960s (full fluorescent childhood) vs. born 2000s (mixed fluorescent/LED childhood)—do preferences diverge?

**Research design**: Cross-sectional preference study (N=200+) across age cohorts; measure preferred CCT for home/office scenarios.

**Expected finding**: Younger Japanese might show slightly lower CCT preference (LED adoption allows warmer options); older Japanese anchored at fluorescent-era 5000K+.

### Gap 4: Kruithof Curve Replication Across Cultures

**Critical question**: Does the "pleasant illuminance × CCT region" truly shift rightward for East Asian subjects, or is the original Kruithof curve universal?

**Research design**: Replicate Kruithof methodology with Japanese, Chinese, Korean, Western European, and North American subjects; map pleasant region for each group.

**Expected finding**: If CCT is culturally calibrated, Japanese pleasant region should peak at ~4500K (home) and ~6000K (office), while Western subjects peak at ~2700K (home) and ~3500K (office).

### Gap 5: Circadian Adaptation & Health Outcomes

**Current state**: Known that high CCT suppresses melatonin; unknown how habituation affects this.

**Critical question**: Does the circadian system of Japanese individuals (habituated to high CCT) show *different baseline melatonin sensitivity* compared to Western individuals?

**Research design**: Circadian physiology study (salivary melatonin, core temperature); compare Japanese and Western subjects under identical light exposure.

**Hypothesis**: Japanese subjects may show elevated baseline melatonin threshold (require stronger suppression stimulus) due to developmental habituation to high-CCT baseline.

## 7. INTEGRATION WITH ATLAS/CVA SYSTEM

### Tier 1 (Universal Constraints)

CCT preference operates **above** Tier 1. The perceptual apparatus—cone cells, ganglion cells, retinal/cortical color processing—is identical across all humans. Tier 1 constraints (visual acuity, color discrimination, temporal resolution) are unchanged.

### Tier 2 (Culture-Calibrated Interpretation)

CCT preference is fundamentally a **Tier 2 calibration**:
- The same physical stimulus (6500K light) is interpreted differently by Japanese (expected, appropriate, modern) vs. Western (harsh, clinical) subjects
- This difference reflects learned reference standards, not perceptual differences
- The interpretation is automatic and rapid—not conscious deliberation

### CVA Parameters for CH-7

```
κ (culture parameter):
  κ = "JP" (Japanese) → predicted_illuminant_CCT_home = 5000K, office = 6000K
  κ = "CN" (Chinese) → predicted_illuminant_CCT_home = 4500K, office = 5500K
  κ = "WE" (Western Europe) → predicted_illuminant_CCT_home = 2800K, office = 4000K
  κ = "NA" (North America) → predicted_illuminant_CCT_home = 2700K, office = 4000K
  κ = "SK" (Scandinavia) → predicted_illuminant_CCT_home = 2900K, office = 4200K

MultisensoryCoherence:
  - Base constraint: visual_illuminant_mismatch = |actual_CCT - predicted_CCT(κ)|
  - Higher mismatch → lower coherence → increased prediction error signal

NarrativeCoherence:
  - Base constraint: lighting_semantics_alignment(κ)
  - "Warm light in office" = incoherent for Japanese (κ="JP")
  - "Cool light in bedroom" = incoherent for Western (κ="WE")

PredictionError:
  - Magnitude calibrated by |actual_CCT - predicted_CCT(κ)| / habituation_strength(κ, age)
  - Younger = stronger habituation = larger error signal for mismatches

ProcessingCost:
  - Familiar CCT = preattentive processing (cost ≈ 0)
  - Unfamiliar CCT = explicit attention (cost > 0)
  - Cost function: 1 / (1 + similarity(actual_CCT, familiar_CCT_history))
```

### Architectural Design Application

When designing for multicultural occupancy:
1. Recognize that "neutral" 4000K is actually NOT neutral—it's culture-specific
2. For East Asian primary users: 5000–6000K residential, 5500–6500K office
3. For Western primary users: 2700–3000K residential, 3500–4000K office
4. For mixed-use: Tunable/adjustable CCT allows users to self-calibrate
5. Acknowledge CVA tension: "Appropriate lighting" cannot simultaneously satisfy both cultures; design choice reflects prioritization

## 8. CONCLUSION: CCT AS TIER 2 HABITUATION

Color temperature preference is a paradigmatic example of **Tier 2 cultural calibration**—a case where universal perceptual mechanisms are locally adapted to match environmental statistics encountered during development.

The phenomenon is:
- **Traceable to specific historical events** (post-WWII Japanese fluorescent adoption)
- **Reinforced through standardization** (NTSC-J displays, architectural practice)
- **Neurobiologically grounded** in color constancy calibration and prediction error mechanisms
- **Functionally important for CVA** in that it affects multiple constraints (MultisensoryCoherence, NarrativeCoherence, PredictionError, ProcessingCost)
- **Practically relevant** for architectural design in multicultural contexts

CH-7 extends the cultural habituation framework (CH-1 through CH-6) by documenting a domain (lighting) where regional preference is particularly stark, historically recent, and traceable to technological adoption patterns. This makes color temperature an exemplary case for studying how technology shapes culture, and how culture shapes perception.
