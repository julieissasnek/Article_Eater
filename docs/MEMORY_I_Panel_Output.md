# PANEL TRANSCRIPT

## Panel Charge

Ten templates spanning hippocampal memory mechanisms in architectural context require calibration from structural scaffolds into full CMR JSON. The domain covers the complete arc from initial encoding (relational binding, prediction error encoding, schema-dependent encoding, pattern separation/completion) through consolidation (systems consolidation, sharp-wave ripple replay, DMN-mediated restoration) to architectural modulation of memory (context-dependent encoding, reconsolidation, and episodic boundary encoding at architectural thresholds).

Three critical cross-panel dependencies constrain this panel's work:

1. **SPATIAL-I SC1 (Spatial Integration)**: SN_CONTEXT_MEMORY_002 inherits the spatial integration parameters from SPATIAL-I. The hippocampal context representation IS the spatial cognitive map — the same place cell ensembles that encode spatial configuration also encode episodic context. The panel must reference SC1's integration-valence parameters and avoid double-counting the spatial navigation contribution.

2. **SOCIAL-I**: Social memory interactions (encoding of social encounters, context-dependent retrieval of social experiences) connect MEMORY-I templates to SOCIAL-I's encounter frequency and co-presence templates.

3. **STRESS-I T6/T7**: Cortisol impairs hippocampal encoding and retrieval (T6 mechanism chain step 5). Elevated allostatic load degrades pattern separation capacity and context encoding fidelity.

Five specific calibration disputes are expected to generate Crucible friction:

1. **Pattern separation threshold**: What environmental input similarity level triggers pattern separation vs. completion in DG-CA3? This determines when architectural distinctiveness matters for memory.

2. **Reconsolidation architectural bridge**: Can reconsolidation windows be exploited architecturally (e.g., spatial redesign during active reconsolidation of a learned route)?

3. **Schema acceleration vs. novelty encoding trade-off**: Familiar architectural schemas accelerate encoding but reduce distinctiveness. Novel architecture produces stronger encoding but higher cognitive load.

4. **Ripple replay conditions**: What architectural features constitute "quiet rest" sufficient for SWR-mediated consolidation?

5. **Doorway effect magnitude and dose-response**: How do specific threshold properties (contrast, compression, transition duration) modulate episodic segmentation strength?

## ROUND TABLE PHASE

### Opening Statement: Lila Davachi — Relational Binding and the Hippocampal Encoding Signal

The hippocampus does not encode experiences as undifferentiated snapshots. It binds discrete elements — objects, locations, temporal positions, emotional states — into relational structures. My fMRI work (Davachi, 2006; Davachi & DuBrow, 2015) demonstrates that the strength of this binding depends on two factors: the distinctiveness of the elements being bound, and the presence of prediction errors at the moment of encoding.

For ED_HIPPOCAMPAL_ENCODING_001, the critical architectural parameter is environmental distinctiveness: spaces with unique combinations of spatial, acoustic, luminance, and material features produce stronger hippocampal encoding than generic spaces. The mechanism is hippocampal pattern separation in DG/CA3 — distinctive inputs activate non-overlapping neural populations, creating orthogonal memory traces that are less susceptible to interference. Generic spaces produce overlapping inputs that are harder to separate.

For ED_PE_ENCODING_PRINCIPLE_001, the mechanism is different but complementary: prediction errors at encoding — surprises, novelties, expectation violations — trigger dopaminergic input to the hippocampus from the ventral tegmental area (VTA), strengthening the synaptic plasticity that underlies memory formation (Lisman & Grace, 2005). Architecturally, this means that moments of spatial surprise (unexpected views, threshold transitions, scale changes) produce stronger memory encoding than predictable sequences.

### Opening Statement: Charan Ranganath — Environmental Context as Memory Scaffold

I must emphasize a finding that has profound architectural implications: the context-dependent memory advantage is one of the most robust effects in memory research, with a meta-analytic effect size of approximately 20–40% better recall in same vs. different context (Smith & Vela, 2001). The hippocampus represents environmental context as a stable pattern of place cell activity — what Nadel and I have called the "context representation" (Ranganath & Ritchey, 2012). Returning to the encoding environment reactivates this pattern, and because the encoded experience is bound to the context representation, the experience is retrieved along with it.

The architectural implication is immediate: spatial stability supports memory. Schools that maintain consistent classroom assignments produce better learning outcomes than those that rotate students between rooms. Hospitals where patients stay in one room throughout their stay show better medication adherence and health knowledge retention than those with frequent room transfers. The mechanism is not comfort or familiarity per se — it is that the stable context representation serves as a retrieval scaffold for all experiences encoded in that space.

### Opening Statement: Karim Nader — Reconsolidation and the Malleability of Spatial Memory

My 2000 *Nature* paper demonstrated something that was heretical at the time: memories are not fixed after initial consolidation. When a consolidated memory is reactivated — retrieved into working memory — it enters a labile state requiring protein synthesis for restabilization. During this reconsolidation window (approximately 6 hours in rodent fear conditioning), the memory can be modified, strengthened, or weakened.

The architectural relevance is speculative but intriguing: if an occupant navigates a familiar building and encounters a spatial change (a new corridor, a redesigned lobby), the act of navigating the familiar route reactivates the spatial memory, potentially opening a reconsolidation window during which the modified spatial information can be integrated into the existing cognitive map. This would make architectural renovations less disorienting than complete rebuilds — the old spatial memory is updated rather than replaced.

I must be candid about the bridge to architecture: reconsolidation has been demonstrated in fear conditioning, motor learning, and declarative memory in laboratory settings. The translation to naturalistic spatial memory in architectural contexts has not been tested. I will advocate for CAPACITY warrant at best, ANALOGICAL for specific dose-response claims.

### Opening Statement: György Buzsáki — Sharp-Wave Ripples and the Architecture of Rest

I will make a claim that may surprise architects: rest spaces are not amenities — they are memory infrastructure. The hippocampus consolidates spatial experiences through sharp-wave ripple complexes (SWRs) that occur during quiet wakefulness and sleep. SWRs are brief (50–120 ms), high-frequency (150–250 Hz) events that compress and replay sequences of place cell activity from recent navigation (Lee & Wilson, 2002; Foster & Wilson, 2006). Disrupting SWRs impairs spatial learning (Girardeau et al., 2009).

The critical condition is quiet wakefulness — a behavioral state characterized by immobility, low visual complexity, reduced arousal, and absence of demanding cognitive tasks. Architecturally, this corresponds to spaces with low visual complexity, acoustic shielding, comfortable postural support, and absence of attention-demanding stimuli. These features define what I call "consolidation-permissive environments."

For MS_RIPPLE_REPLAY_002, I will calibrate the environmental conditions that support vs. suppress SWR generation. For ED_SYSTEMS_CONSOLIDATION_001, I will calibrate the longer-term process by which SWR-mediated replay transfers spatial knowledge from hippocampus to neocortex over days to weeks.

### Opening Statement: Richard Morris — Schemas and the Speed of Learning

The schema-dependent encoding phenomenon that my laboratory demonstrated (Tse et al., 2007) has a direct architectural corollary: buildings that conform to familiar spatial schemas are learned faster than buildings that violate them. A hospital with a standard double-loaded corridor layout activates the "hospital schema" in neocortical memory, and new spatial information (location of specific rooms, services) is encoded rapidly because it maps onto the pre-existing framework.

But there is a trade-off that the template must capture: schema-conforming environments are learned faster but encoded less distinctively. Schema-violating environments require more encoding effort but produce more distinctive, less interference-prone memory traces. The optimal design depends on the use case: hospitals should maximize schema conformity (fast learning, low navigational stress) while creative workplaces might benefit from moderate schema violation (distinctive encoding, memorable spatial experiences).

### Opening Statement: Michael Yassa — Pattern Separation, Aging, and Environmental Distinctiveness

I bring the most directly relevant human data for ED_PATTERN_SEP_COMP_001. Using high-resolution fMRI at 7T, my laboratory has demonstrated that the dentate gyrus (DG) performs pattern separation — orthogonalizing similar inputs into distinct representations — while CA3 performs pattern completion — retrieving a stored pattern from a partial or degraded cue (Yassa & Stark, 2011; Bakker et al., 2008).

The critical parameter for architectural calibration: the similarity threshold at which DG switches from separation to completion. In our mnemonic similarity task, inputs that are ~60–70% similar to stored patterns engage pattern separation in young adults; inputs that are >70% similar engage pattern completion. In older adults (65+), this threshold shifts downward: ~50–60% similarity already triggers pattern completion rather than separation, meaning older adults are more likely to confuse similar environments.

The architectural implication: buildings for elderly populations must be MORE distinctive between spaces than buildings for young adults, because the aging DG has a degraded capacity for separating similar inputs. Identical corridors in eldercare facilities are not just aesthetically monotonous — they are neurologically confusing because the degraded DG cannot separate them into distinct memory traces.

### Opening Statement: Marc Howard — Temporal Context and the Drift of Memory

My Temporal Context Model (Howard & Kahana, 2002) formalizes how temporal context changes during experience. As you move through a building, your internal temporal context drifts — each new experience slightly shifts the context representation. Events that occur close together in time share similar context states and are therefore more easily associated in memory.

For ED_PE_ENCODING_PRINCIPLE_001, the TCM provides the computational framework: prediction errors create discontinuities in the temporal context signal, producing stronger context shifts at encoding. These stronger shifts create more distinctive memory traces — but also segment the ongoing experience into separate episodes. This connects directly to Radvansky's doorway effect: a physical threshold creates both a spatial boundary and a temporal context shift, and the combination produces episodic segmentation.

### Opening Statement: Gabriel Radvansky — The Doorway Effect and Episodic Boundary Encoding

The doorway effect (Radvansky & Copeland, 2006; Radvansky et al., 2011) is a well-replicated phenomenon: walking through a doorway produces transient forgetting for information associated with the previous room. The effect is typically d ≈ 0.30–0.50 for recall decrement, present in both physical and virtual environments, and persists even when the person returns to the original room.

The mechanism is event model updating: when sensory and motoric cues signal a boundary between environments (a door frame, a threshold, a significant spatial transition), the event model is updated — the current event representation is closed and a new one is opened. Information from the closed event becomes less accessible, while the new event model begins accumulating new associations.

For THRESHOLD_EPISODIC_BOUNDARY_001, the architectural question is: what physical properties of a threshold determine the strength of the episodic boundary? My research suggests three key features: (a) spatial contrast — the magnitude of change in spatial properties across the boundary (room size, ceiling height, lighting, materials); (b) enclosure change — the degree to which the transition changes the sense of enclosure; (c) transition duration — abrupt transitions (door) produce stronger boundaries than gradual transitions (open archway).

### Opening Statement: Margaret Schlichting — Waking Rest and Memory Integration

My work on offline memory integration (Schlichting & Preston, 2015; Schlichting et al., 2015) demonstrates that rest periods following encoding are not merely pauses — they are active integration windows during which the hippocampus replays recent experiences and the medial prefrontal cortex integrates them with prior knowledge.

For MS_CONSOLIDATION_RESTORATION_001, the critical finding is that even brief rest periods (10–15 minutes) of quiet wakefulness following spatial learning improve subsequent navigation performance. The mechanism involves DMN re-engagement: during rest, the default mode network — particularly the hippocampal-medial prefrontal cortex axis — becomes active and performs memory integration. Architecturally, this means that buildings with restorative rest spaces (nature views, low cognitive demand, comfortable seating) positioned after learning-intensive zones (classrooms, orientation areas, training rooms) will support better memory consolidation than buildings without such transitional rest spaces.

### Opening Statement: Colin Ellard — Translating Memory Parameters to Built Environment Design

I bring the ambulatory physiological monitoring perspective. My laboratory measures EDA, HRV, and subjective experience as participants navigate real architectural environments. The memory parameters my colleagues are calibrating — pattern separation thresholds, context encoding strength, episodic boundary magnitude — all have downstream physiological signatures that we can measure in situ.

My calibration contribution is translation: mapping the laboratory memory parameters to architectural feature specifications that a builder AI can use. For example, Yassa's pattern separation threshold of ~60–70% similarity translates to architectural distinctiveness metrics — the minimum number of distinguishing features (color, material, spatial proportion, wayfinding landmark) required to ensure DG pattern separation between adjacent spaces. Radvansky's doorway effect translates to threshold design specifications — the minimum spatial contrast ratio across a boundary to produce measurable episodic segmentation.

## CRUCIBLE DEBATES

### Debate 1: Pattern Separation Threshold and Architectural Distinctiveness

**Yassa**: The mnemonic similarity task gives us a precise threshold: young adults reliably pattern-separate inputs at ~60–70% similarity; completion dominates above ~70%. But "similarity" in my task is visual similarity of objects. For architectural spaces, similarity is multi-dimensional — spatial proportion, lighting, materials, color, acoustic signature, olfactory cues. We need to define architectural similarity operationally.

**Ellard**: I propose a Distinctiveness Index: count the number of feature dimensions on which two adjacent spaces differ. If spaces share the same ceiling height, floor material, wall color, lighting type, and acoustic character — but differ only in layout — they are ~80% similar. If they differ on 3+ dimensions, they drop below the ~60% threshold and DG should separate them.

**Davachi**: I support Ellard's approach but with a caveat: not all feature dimensions are equally weighted in hippocampal encoding. Spatial proportion and lighting are more heavily weighted than wall color or floor material, because the hippocampus preferentially encodes spatial and luminance information via the parahippocampal place area (Epstein & Kanwisher, 1998). A distinctiveness index must weight spatial and lighting features more heavily.

**Yassa**: For elderly populations, the threshold is more conservative. We need ~50% similarity maximum — meaning at least 5 out of 8 feature dimensions must differ between adjacent spaces. This is a strong design constraint for eldercare.

**Panel consensus**: ED_PATTERN_SEP_COMP_001 uses a weighted Distinctiveness Index. Minimum 3 feature dimensions differing (with spatial proportion or lighting as mandatory 1 of the 3) for young adults; minimum 5 for elderly. Confidence on the threshold: 0.55 (lab-validated for visual objects; architectural translation is a plausible extension). Bridge warrant: EMPIRICAL_COVARIANCE for the threshold; CAPACITY for the architectural distinctiveness mapping.

### Debate 2: Schema Conformity vs. Novelty — The Encoding Trade-Off

**Morris**: Schema-dependent encoding is faster — approximately 50% faster for schema-conforming information in our rodent studies (Tse et al., 2007). The mechanism is neocortical pre-activation: when the schema is activated, neocortical neurons that represent the schema become active and provide a framework onto which new hippocampal bindings can attach.

**Davachi**: But schema-conforming encoding is less distinctive. My prediction error encoding principle implies that schema-violating features are encoded more strongly because they generate prediction errors. There is a genuine trade-off: schema conformity → fast but generic encoding; schema violation → slow but distinctive encoding.

**Radvansky**: The doorway effect illustrates this trade-off. A standard doorway (schema-conforming threshold) produces a moderate episodic boundary. An unexpected threshold (a waterfall curtain, a narrow squeeze, a dramatic scale change) produces a stronger boundary — more forgetting for the prior event, but also stronger encoding of the novel threshold experience itself.

**Morris**: I propose a two-regime model. For utilitarian spaces where fast learning is paramount (hospitals, airports, emergency routes), optimize for schema conformity. For experiential spaces where memorable distinctiveness matters (museums, hotels, creative workplaces), optimize for moderate schema violation. The modifier is a binary design choice, not a continuous parameter.

**Panel consensus**: ED_SCHEMA_ENCODING_001 encodes a two-regime parameter: schema_conformity_priority (boolean) that selects between "fast encoding" mode (schema alignment → ~50% faster learning, lower distinctiveness) and "deep encoding" mode (moderate schema violation → ~30% slower learning, higher distinctiveness). The trade-off is real and design-actionable. Confidence: 0.60 for the schema acceleration; 0.50 for the quantitative trade-off ratio.

### Debate 3: Ripple Replay Environmental Conditions

**Buzsáki**: I must be precise about what "quiet rest" means neurally. SWRs occur when the hippocampus transitions from theta state (active exploration, engaged attention) to large irregular activity (LIA). This transition requires: (a) cessation of locomotion; (b) reduction of visual scene processing load; (c) absence of salient or threatening stimuli. In rodents, SWRs are maximal during immobile quiet wakefulness in a familiar, low-complexity environment.

**Schlichting**: In humans, the closest analogue is the DMN-re-engagement state measured by fMRI. DMN activation during rest periods correlates with subsequent memory performance (Tambini et al., 2010). The environmental features that promote DMN re-engagement: low visual complexity, nature views (which promote soft fascination without directed attention), acoustic shielding, and postural comfort.

**Ellard**: I can translate these to architectural specifications: (a) Visual complexity below VF2's "boring" threshold (SRV < 0.05) but above zero (not sensory deprivation); (b) Nature view present (VIEW1 channels 1–3 activated but not demanding); (c) LAeq < 45 dBA with low variability; (d) Seating that supports ~15° recline. These four conditions define a "consolidation-permissive environment."

**Buzsáki**: The constraint from the pre-panel review is correct: SWR data are from rodent hippocampus. Human scalp EEG cannot reliably detect individual SWRs. The environmental conditions I specify are THEORETICAL_DEFAULT for human architectural application until intracranial recording or high-density MEG studies confirm SWR-like events during architectural rest.

**Panel consensus**: MS_RIPPLE_REPLAY_002 environmental conditions are specified as Ellard's four-feature consolidation-permissive environment. All parameters carry THEORETICAL_DEFAULT for human architectural translation. Confidence on the animal mechanism: 0.80. Confidence on the human architectural translation: 0.45.

### Debate 4: Doorway Effect Dose-Response

**Radvansky**: The doorway effect is robust (d ≈ 0.30–0.50) but I have not varied threshold properties parametrically. My studies used standard doorways. The question of whether spatial contrast magnitude modulates the effect size is untested.

**Howard**: The TCM predicts a dose-response: larger context shifts produce stronger episodic boundaries. If a doorway produces a context shift of magnitude Δc, and a dramatic threshold (scale change, lighting shift, material transition) produces a context shift of 2Δc, the TCM predicts approximately double the forgetting effect but also stronger encoding of the boundary moment itself.

**Radvansky**: I accept the TCM prediction as theoretically grounded, but I must note that my empirical data do not confirm a dose-response. The effect is present (door vs. no door) or absent (no boundary). Whether bigger doors or more dramatic thresholds produce bigger effects is untested.

**Davachi**: The prediction error encoding principle supports the dose-response: stronger prediction errors at encoding produce stronger memory traces. A dramatic architectural threshold (large spatial contrast) generates a larger prediction error than a standard doorway. The boundary is encoded more strongly, but information from the prior event is also more strongly segmented.

**Panel consensus**: THRESHOLD_EPISODIC_BOUNDARY_001 encodes a dose-response prediction based on TCM and PE encoding: spatial contrast ratio → episodic boundary strength (monotonic, predicted linear). This is a THEORETICAL_DEFAULT prediction at confidence 0.45. The binary doorway effect (present/absent) is EMPIRICAL_COVARIANCE at confidence 0.60.

### Debate 5: Systems Consolidation Timescale and Architectural Claims

**Buzsáki**: Systems consolidation — the gradual transfer of memory from hippocampus to neocortex — operates on a timescale of weeks to months to years. I must be very clear: no single architectural experience produces systems consolidation. What architecture can do is support the CONDITIONS for consolidation: SWR replay during rest, DMN re-engagement, and repeated exposure to the same spatial context.

**Morris**: I agree on timescale. Schema-dependent encoding can accelerate the rate at which new memories are transferred to neocortex, but the process still takes days at minimum (Tse et al., 2007 — rapid schema-dependent encoding was observed 48 hours post-training). Any architectural claim about same-day systems consolidation is inconsistent with the mechanism.

**Schlichting**: The distinction from the pre-panel review is critical: (a) acute environmental effects on consolidation-SUPPORTING neural activity (SWR replay, DMN re-engagement) are real and session-scale; (b) systems consolidation ITSELF is weeks-to-years. Architecture supports (a), which feeds (b) over time.

**Panel consensus**: ED_SYSTEMS_CONSOLIDATION_001 distinguishes two confidence levels: acute consolidation-supporting activity (session-scale, confidence 0.55 for mechanism, 0.45 for architectural conditions) and systems consolidation outcome (weeks-to-years, confidence 0.40 for architectural claims because the causal chain is too long and slow to attribute to any single architectural exposure).

## RESIDUAL GAPS

### ED_HIPPOCAMPAL_ENCODING_001

**Gap 1**: The feature dimension weights (spatial proportion 0.25, lighting 0.20, etc.) are panel-estimated, not empirically derived from parametric architectural studies. No study has independently varied architectural feature dimensions and measured hippocampal encoding strength. The weights are extrapolated from PPA response properties to isolated feature categories (Epstein & Kanwisher, 1998) rather than from multi-feature architectural configurations.

**Gap 2**: The encoding strength boost of 1.35 for distinctive environments is a panel estimate extrapolated from laboratory paired-associate learning differences. The magnitude of the distinctiveness effect in real architectural navigation has not been measured.

### ED_RECONSOLIDATION_001

**Gap 1**: The reconsolidation window duration (4–6 hours) is from rodent fear conditioning. Human spatial reconsolidation has not been studied with architectural stimuli. The window may be shorter, longer, or absent for complex spatial memories as opposed to simple associative fears.

**Gap 2**: The modification integration limit (≤30% spatial layout change) is a panel estimate with no empirical basis. This is the most speculative parameter in the entire MEMORY-I panel.

**Gap 3**: The interaction between reconsolidation and wayfinding behavior is entirely untested. Whether occupants who encounter renovated spaces during active navigation (which would reactivate their spatial memory) actually undergo reconsolidation of the cognitive map is an open empirical question.

### ED_SCHEMA_ENCODING_001

**Gap 1**: The schema acceleration factor (1.5×) is from rodent paired-associate learning (Tse et al., 2007). Whether human architectural spatial learning shows the same magnitude of schema acceleration is untested. The human literature on schema effects in memory (van Kesteren et al., 2012) supports the direction but not the specific magnitude.

**Gap 2**: The "schema availability threshold" (≥3 prior exposures to building type) is a panel estimate. Individual differences in schema acquisition rate are likely substantial and modulated by architectural expertise, spatial ability, and cultural familiarity with building types.

### ED_SYSTEMS_CONSOLIDATION_001

**Gap 1**: The distinction between acute consolidation-supporting activity and systems consolidation outcome is well-motivated but the boundary between them is fuzzy. At what point does "repeated SWR replay across sessions" become "systems consolidation"? The template does not specify a threshold for this transition.

**Gap 2**: The schema acceleration of consolidation (2–5× with existing schema) is from Tse et al. (2011) in rodents. The human evidence for schema-accelerated consolidation is indirect (van Kesteren et al., 2012) and the magnitude of acceleration in architectural contexts is unknown.

### ED_PATTERN_SEP_COMP_001

**Gap 1**: The mnemonic similarity task uses 2D visual objects. The extension to multi-dimensional architectural similarity (spatial proportion + lighting + materials + acoustics) is a significant extrapolation. Whether the DG performs pattern separation over architectural feature spaces in the same way it separates similar objects is assumed but not tested.

**Gap 2**: The elderly threshold shift (from 0.35 to 0.50) is robust for visual objects (Yassa et al., 2011) but the architectural translation — elderly adults need 50% dissimilarity between adjacent spaces — has not been validated in real buildings.

### ED_PE_ENCODING_PRINCIPLE_001

**Gap 1**: The PE encoding boost (1.40×) is extrapolated from laboratory surprise-encoding studies. Whether architectural spatial surprises produce the same magnitude of encoding enhancement is untested.

**Gap 2**: The PE habituation rate (3–5 exposures) is a panel estimate. Architectural prediction errors in a building navigated daily would habituate rapidly; the initial encoding boost would disappear after the first week. The template does not model the long-term dynamics of PE habituation in repeated architectural exposure.

**Gap 3**: Overlap with NEUROMOD-I. The dopaminergic PE mechanism (VTA → hippocampus) is shared with reward prediction error circuits. When NEUROMOD-I calibrates Schultz's reward PE parameters, the panel must ensure no double-counting of the hippocampal PE encoding boost.

### SN_CONTEXT_MEMORY_002

**Gap 1**: The context stability threshold (<15% feature change) is a panel estimate. The actual tolerance of hippocampal context representations for environmental change during the interval between encoding and retrieval is not known for architectural contexts.

**Gap 2**: The cross-panel dependency on SPATIAL-I SC1 is correctly specified but creates a tight coupling: any change to SC1 spatial integration parameters propagates to SN_CONTEXT_MEMORY_002. The direction and magnitude of this propagation are not formally specified.

### MS_RIPPLE_REPLAY_002

**Gap 1**: The entire "consolidation-permissive environment" specification is THEORETICAL_DEFAULT for humans. No study has measured SWR-like events or their analogues in humans resting in varied architectural conditions. The environmental conditions are extrapolated from rodent behavioral state requirements and human DMN research, which are two different levels of analysis joined by analogy.

**Gap 2**: The interaction between SWR replay and sleep architecture is important but out of scope for this template (waking rest only). Buildings that impair sleep quality (hospital noise, unfamiliar bed) also impair SWR-mediated consolidation during sleep, but this is a separate mechanism.

### MS_CONSOLIDATION_RESTORATION_001

**Gap 1**: The causal link from Attention Restoration Theory (ART) environmental features to DMN re-engagement is plausible but not directly tested. ART and DMN research developed independently; the assumption that ART's "soft fascination" maps onto DMN re-engagement is a theoretical bridge, not an empirical finding.

**Gap 2**: The rest-period memory benefit (15–25%) is from Dewar et al. (2012), which used quiet rest in a controlled laboratory. Whether the same benefit obtains in real restorative architectural environments (with nature views, comfortable seating, etc.) vs. stark laboratory rooms has not been tested parametrically.

### THRESHOLD_EPISODIC_BOUNDARY_001

**Gap 1**: The dose-response prediction (spatial contrast ratio → boundary strength, monotonic linear) is the most important untested prediction in this template. Radvansky's paradigm uses binary boundaries (door vs. no door); no study has parametrically varied threshold properties (contrast magnitude, compression ratio, sensory discontinuity) and measured graded effects on episodic boundary strength.

**Gap 2**: The interaction between threshold design and information retention is architecturally important but double-edged: stronger episodic boundaries improve the distinctiveness and memorability of the boundary experience itself but impair access to information from the prior event. The design trade-off (memorable thresholds vs. information continuity) needs explicit guidance for the builder AI, which this template does not provide.

## CMR INTEGRATION NOTE

### How MEMORY-I Templates Compose

The ten MEMORY-I templates form a connected system that covers the full temporal arc of architectural memory, from initial encoding through consolidation to long-term storage. The composition logic follows the temporal sequence of memory processing:

**Encoding phase** (seconds to minutes): ED_HIPPOCAMPAL_ENCODING_001 provides the base encoding strength, modulated by ED_PATTERN_SEP_COMP_001 (distinctiveness gates separation vs. completion), ED_PE_ENCODING_PRINCIPLE_001 (surprise boosts encoding), and ED_SCHEMA_ENCODING_001 (schema conformity trades speed for distinctiveness). SN_CONTEXT_MEMORY_002 encodes the spatial context that binds the episode.

**Boundary encoding** (milliseconds at transitions): THRESHOLD_EPISODIC_BOUNDARY_001 segments the ongoing stream into discrete episodes at architectural thresholds, interacting with ED_PE_ENCODING_PRINCIPLE_001 (threshold surprises generate PEs).

**Consolidation phase** (minutes to hours): MS_RIPPLE_REPLAY_002 and MS_CONSOLIDATION_RESTORATION_001 operate during post-encoding rest. SWR replay compresses and reactivates recent spatial experiences; DMN re-engagement integrates them with existing schemas. These templates share environmental conditions (quiet, restorative, low demand) and can be co-satisfied by a single "consolidation-permissive" space design.

**Long-term storage** (weeks to years): ED_SYSTEMS_CONSOLIDATION_001 describes the gradual hippocampal-to-neocortical transfer driven by repeated replay across sessions. ED_RECONSOLIDATION_001 handles the updating of stored spatial memories when the environment changes.

### Key Cross-Panel Dependencies

**SPATIAL-I SC1** → SN_CONTEXT_MEMORY_002: Spatial integration quality determines the richness of context encoding. The builder AI must not re-parameterize spatial navigation in MEMORY-I; it should reference SC1 values.

**STRESS-I T6** → ED_HIPPOCAMPAL_ENCODING_001, ED_PATTERN_SEP_COMP_001, MS_RIPPLE_REPLAY_002: Cortisol is the primary environmental stressor pathway that impairs hippocampal encoding, pattern separation, and SWR generation. Chronic stress is the enemy of architectural memory at every level.

**SOCIAL-I** → SN_CONTEXT_MEMORY_002, THRESHOLD_EPISODIC_BOUNDARY_001: Social encounters are encoded as part of spatial context; threshold crossings that change social density produce both episodic and proxemic prediction errors.

**VISUAL-I VIEW1** → MS_RIPPLE_REPLAY_002, MS_CONSOLIDATION_RESTORATION_001: Nature view channels support the consolidation-permissive state.

### Partial-Out Protocol for Shared Mechanisms

**PE encoding and schema violation**: ED_PE_ENCODING_PRINCIPLE_001 and ED_SCHEMA_ENCODING_001 share the prediction error mechanism (schema violations generate PEs). The builder AI should not sum the PE encoding boost AND the schema violation encoding boost independently; when a schema violation produces a PE, the encoding enhancement is computed once at the higher of the two values, not as an additive combination.

**SWR replay and DMN re-engagement**: MS_RIPPLE_REPLAY_002 and MS_CONSOLIDATION_RESTORATION_001 share environmental conditions and co-occur during rest. The builder AI should model a single "consolidation rest" design requirement that satisfies both templates, not two independent rest space requirements.

**Pattern separation and context encoding**: ED_PATTERN_SEP_COMP_001 and SN_CONTEXT_MEMORY_002 share the spatial distinctiveness pathway. Distinctive spaces that promote pattern separation also produce stronger context encoding. The builder AI should compute distinctiveness once and propagate to both templates.
