# 1. Introduction — The Problem of Evidence Synthesis in Environmental Psychology

Environmental psychology rests on a paradox. Over fifty years of rigorous empirical research has established that features of the built environment — daylight exposure, spatial configuration, acoustic environment, biophilic design elements, thermal conditions — affect human cognition, emotion, and stress reactivity in measurable and often substantial ways (Ulrich, 1984; Kaplan & Kaplan, 1989; Eells, 2010). Hundreds of peer-reviewed articles document these effects with varying degrees of methodological rigor, ranging from carefully controlled laboratory experiments to large-scale observational studies. Yet when architects, building managers, and organizational leaders face actual design decisions, they operate almost entirely in the dark. The gap between what environmental psychology knows and what it can reliably communicate to practitioners is not a matter of publication lag or professional silos. It is a structural problem in how evidence is organized and transmitted.

Consider a concrete example. An architect designing a hospital is told by environmental psychology that exposure to nature views correlates with faster recovery from surgery (Ulrich, 1984). This claim is supported by a landmark study showing that patients with window views of trees recovered faster than patients with views of brick walls. But the architect needs to know several things that the original study does not address: First, does the effect hold in settings other than a single hospital in Philadelphia in 1984? Second, what is the *mechanism* — is the recovery effect due to stress reduction via psychophysiological restoration, or to improved mood and motivation to ambulate, or to some other pathway? Third, how large is the effect actually — is it clinically significant, or statistically significant but practically negligible? Fourth, what features of the view matter — must it be live trees, or do photographs suffice? How far must the view extend for visual attention to reach it? Fifth, how much do individual differences matter — does the effect hold equally for all patients, or only for some demographic subgroups? Sixth, how does this effect interact with other environmental features — do acoustics, temperature, or lighting modulate the strength of the nature-view benefit? And seventh, given budget constraints, how should the architect weight this evidence against competing claims about the importance of spatial scale, social density, wayfinding clarity, and facility cleanliness?

The original empirical literature cannot answer these questions because they require integration across multiple studies, resolution of competing mechanisms, assessment of scope conditions, and weighing of evidence types that differ dramatically in their evidential strength. A traditional narrative review offers expert judgment but no systematic method. A systematic review and meta-analysis can synthesize effect sizes but only if the studies are sufficiently similar that effect size comparison makes sense — and in environmental psychology, nearly every study is unique in its setting, population, operational definition, and methodology. The GRADE framework (Schünemann et al., 2013) offers a hierarchical assessment of evidence quality, but it was designed for pharmaceutical interventions where evidence quality correlates with study design (RCTs > observational studies); in environmental psychology, the highest-quality evidence sometimes comes from mechanism-tracing laboratory studies and sometimes from real-world occupancy observations, and the two are incommensurable on a single scale. And all three of these approaches — narrative review, meta-analysis, GRADE — treat evidence as a collection of facts to be summarized. They do not represent evidence as *justified beliefs embedded in a causal model*. They do not distinguish between evidence that a mechanism operates *in principle* (laboratory finding) and evidence that the mechanism operates *in buildings at sufficient magnitude to matter* (applied finding). They do not capture the difference between a claim backed by a traced neural mechanism and a claim backed by a replicated correlation: both might receive the same "high quality" rating, yet they mean epistemologically different things and require different research to resolve their uncertainty.

The result is what might be called the *evidence synthesis bottleneck*. The published literature contains far more knowledge than practitioners can access and interpret. The bottleneck is not information scarcity but information incoherence. The environmental psychology literature is not well-organized into a structure that allows the architect to see which claims are well-supported, how they interact, where the evidence is strong, where it is weak, and what research would most improve the knowledge base. The problem is not new — this is how nearly all applied sciences operate — but for environmental psychology specifically, the cost is severe. Building design decisions last for decades and affect occupant wellbeing constantly. Unlike pharmaceutical decisions, where harm from suboptimal choices is eventually detected through adverse event reporting, suboptimal building design decisions often go unevaluated. An architect makes a choice based on a vague impression of the research, the building is occupied for twenty years, and no one systematically measures whether the design choice actually delivered the intended benefits. The knowledge base does not grow from accumulated design decisions; it grows only from published studies, which is vastly slower and narrower.

This paper presents a computational framework that addresses the evidence synthesis bottleneck by organizing environmental psychology knowledge as a *typed epistemic network* (EN) coupled to a *Bayesian network* (BN) through a *projection function* (π). The three components work together to preserve the epistemological rigor that exists in the published literature while making that rigor computationally actionable.

The Epistemic Network represents the current state of scientific knowledge about causal relationships between environmental features and human outcomes. It is a directed graph with three components: first, a set of nodes representing theoretical beliefs at three tiers (neuroscience frameworks, domain-specific theories, concrete empirical claims); second, a set of edges connecting beliefs, typed into seven categories according to how the beliefs are evidentially related; and third, a set of annotations on each belief recording its evidential provenance (what data support it, what warrant type bridges evidence to conclusion, what alternative accounts have been considered, what conditions limit its applicability). The EN is fundamentally coherentist in structure, following Quine's (1951) vision of knowledge as an interconnected web in which the justification of any particular belief depends on its coherence with surrounding beliefs rather than on appeal to a foundational basis.

The Bayesian Network receives its structure and parameters from the EN. It is Pearl's (2000) Structural Causal Model — a directed acyclic graph with measurable variables as nodes and causal arrows as edges, parameterized by conditional probability tables (CPTs). Whereas the EN permits cycles (feedback loops between concepts), latent variables (unobserved mechanisms), and high-level theoretical abstractions, the BN is constrained to be acyclic, observable, and probabilistic. The BN is the tool for answering interventional questions: *if I change an environmental feature, what will happen to outcomes?* This requires Pearl's do-calculus and the formal machinery of causal inference, which the EN does not provide.

The Projection Function π bridges the two. Given the current state of the EN — with all its theoretical commitments, evidential annotations, and credence assignments — the projection function computes a Bayesian network by (a) selecting which EN edges correspond to causal relationships observable in buildings, (b) compressing multi-step mechanism chains into single causal arrows, and (c) translating the EN's epistemic credences into the BN's aleatory probabilities (conditional probability tables). The projection is lossy: information is lost when a rich mechanism chain is compressed into a single CPT entry. But the lossiness is intentional. The EN preserves the theoretical detail that explains *why* a relationship holds; the BN provides the numerical framework for *predicting* what will happen.

Why separate the EN and BN rather than building everything into a single unified model? The separation enforces a critical distinction that is often glossed over in applied probability reasoning: the distinction between *epistemic uncertainty* (the scientific community's uncertainty about whether its models are correct) and *aleatory uncertainty* (the inherent variability of the real world). Epistemic uncertainty is about knowledge — it changes when we learn more, even if the world stays the same. Aleatory uncertainty is about the world — it changes only if the world actually changes. These two kinds of uncertainty require different data structures, different research interventions, and different formal treatments. A coherentist epistemic network is well-suited to track epistemic uncertainty because coherence is fundamentally about how a community's beliefs hang together and support each other. A Bayesian network is well-suited to track aleatory uncertainty because conditional probability tables directly model population frequencies. By maintaining both, the ATLAS system avoids a common pitfall: the false conflation of theoretical uncertainty with empirical variability. When an architect reads that "the probability of positive mood given high daylight is 0.70," they need to know whether this 0.70 reflects the community's measured uncertainty about the theory of how daylight affects mood (epistemic) or the observed frequency with which high-daylight exposure produces positive mood in the occupant population (aleatory). The answer determines whether the architect should seek more research to refine the theory (epistemic solution) or more occupant data to estimate the frequency better (aleatory solution). Confusing the two leads to wasted effort.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 establishes the philosophical foundations, showing how Quinean coherentism and Bayesian causal inference can be integrated and where they remain distinct. Section 3 presents the three-layer architecture in detail, with the EN's structure and revision rules, the projection function's mathematics, and the BN's interface to decision-making. Section 4 specifies the warrant type hierarchy and the empirical basis for the transfer reliability discount factors that underpin the projection. Section 5 describes the template library of 208 causal claims encoded in environmental psychology and the methodology by which expert panels calibrated their credences. Sections 6 and 7 ground the framework in concrete examples and system health assessment. Sections 8–10 evaluate the framework against existing approaches, assess its current status, and outline future directions.

The paper is structured as an existence proof. The central claim is that computational coherentism — making Quine's metaphor precise and executable — is possible and produces useful outputs when applied to a large, messy scientific domain (environmental psychology) with heterogeneous evidence types. The existence of the ATLAS system demonstrates this in concrete detail. Whether it is the *best* way to organize evidence in environmental psychology is a separate, empirical question that the framework is designed to answer through its evaluation protocols.

# 2. Philosophical Foundations — Coherentism Meets Causal Inference

The integration of coherentism and causal inference rests on a simple observation: they answer different questions. Coherentism asks, *What do we believe, and why is that justified?* Causal inference asks, *If we act on those beliefs, what will happen?* The first question is epistemological — it is about how beliefs are justified. The second is operational — it is about what beliefs entail for action. Science must answer both. Any computational system that aspires to represent scientific knowledge must do the same.

## 2.1 Quinean Coherentism: The Web and Its Revisions

Willard Van Orman Quine's (1951) vision of knowledge as an interconnected web of beliefs has become foundational to contemporary epistemology, even among philosophers who reject coherentism itself. The metaphor captures something real and important: scientific knowledge is not a collection of independent facts anchored to some foundational bedrock. It is a structured whole in which the justification of any particular belief depends on how well that belief coheres with other beliefs. When a physicist asserts that atoms are indivisible, or when an archaeologist asserts that a skeleton dates to 4500 BCE, they are not merely claiming to have perceived something directly. They are claiming that their assertion hangs together well with a much larger body of knowledge — with background theories, with methodological commitments, with other empirical findings, with practical applications that have worked out successfully. The justification is holistic: the belief is justified because it fits.

But the metaphor is not precise. Quine's original web of belief did not specify what the nodes are, what the edges mean, what coherence amounts to mathematically, or how the web is revised when new evidence arrives. Making the metaphor precise is the work of contemporary formal epistemology.

Susan Haack's (1993) foundherentism offers an important mediation. Haack rejects the extreme coherentist view that any set of coherent beliefs is equally justified, regardless of how grounded they are in evidence. She also rejects the strict foundationalist view that some beliefs are self-justifying and everything else is justified by how well it relates to the foundation. Instead, Haack proposes a "crossword puzzle" model: just as the entries in a crossword puzzle are justified by how well they cohere with other entries AND by their individual letters, beliefs are justified by how well they cohere with other beliefs AND by their individual grounding in evidence. Some beliefs (empirical observations like "I see red") are more grounded; others (abstract theories) rely more entirely on coherence. But no belief is purely self-justifying, and no belief is justified purely by coherence — justification is always a blend.

This foundherentist picture is essential for environmental psychology. The field rests on a foundation of empirical observations — occupants report their mood, researchers measure cortisol levels, movement trackers record circulation patterns. But not every empirical observation is created equal. Some observations (a single measure of mood in a single building from a single subject) are weakly grounded. Others (replicated measurements in multiple buildings across multiple populations) are well-grounded. And the grounding affects how much the belief depends on coherence versus direct support. A weakly-grounded belief must cohere very tightly with other beliefs to be justified. A well-grounded belief can hold even if it slightly misaligns with surrounding beliefs, because the direct evidence compensates.

The typed epistemic network implements this foundherentist picture. Each node in the network is a belief carrying both (a) a coherence score derived from how well it aligns with neighboring beliefs, and (b) a grounding score derived from the evidential data that support it. The overall justification of a belief is a function of both. This is not standard Bayesian conditioning, where evidence strictly updates priors. It is a more holistic assessment that Quine himself advocated and Haack formalized.

Quine also introduced the concept of entrenchment — the observation that some beliefs are more central to our overall system of knowledge and resist revision more strenuously than others. If my observations today contradict my belief that 2+2=4, I do not revise mathematics; I assume my observations were mistaken. If my observations contradict my belief that it was sunny yesterday, I readily give up the belief. The entrenchment gradient reflects the structure of the web: beliefs that support many other beliefs are central (high entrenchment), while beliefs that are supported by many others but support few are peripheral. In the ATLAS system, entrenchment is operationalized through the theory tier hierarchy: T1 frameworks (neuroscience theories) are highly entrenched; T2 templates (specific empirical claims) are peripheral. When revising the web in response to contradictory evidence, the system preferentially updates T2 before updating T1, preserving the theoretical core while allowing empirical claims to change (Quine, 1951; Harman, 1986).

## 2.2 Haack's Foundherentism and the Role of Warrant Types

The step from Quine's original metaphor to a computable system requires distinguishing different *kinds* of coherence and different *kinds* of grounding. This is where Haack's work becomes not merely philosophically important but architecturally central.

Haack argues that coherence is not monolithic. Some coherence relationships are explanatory (one belief explains another), while others are merely consistency-preserving (two beliefs are compatible, but neither explains the other). Similarly, grounding is not monolithic. Some beliefs rest on strong empirical foundations, while others rest on weak ones; some rest on direct observation, while others rest on inference from observation. The quality of justification depends on both the strength of the grounding and the tightness of the coherence.

The typed warrant hierarchy in the ATLAS system operationalizes this distinction. Seven types of warrant relate beliefs and evidence (Woodward, 2003, provides the philosophical justification):

1. **CONSTITUTIVE warrants**: The mechanism *is* the phenomenon. "Stress is the activation of the HPA axis" — if we have defined stress neurophysiologically, then the presence of HPA activation constitutes stress. These warrants are the strongest (ceiling 0.95) because they are true by definition. When a warrant is constitutive, updating it means changing how we define our terms, a high-cost operation that affects everything downstream.

2. **MECHANISM warrants**: A traced causal pathway connects premises to conclusion. "Daylight increases retinal stimulation → increases raphe nucleus activation → increases serotonin synthesis → improves mood." The pathway is spelled out step by step, with known biological processes at each step. These warrants are strong (ceiling 0.80) because mechanism-tracing is a gold standard for evidence in neuroscience and medicine. Mechanism evidence is robust across contexts because it describes how nature actually works, not merely that it works.

3. **EMPIRICAL_ASSOCIATION warrants**: Evidence shows a correlation or covariance, but the mechanism is not traced. "High daylight correlates with faster patient recovery." The effect is observed and potentially large, but we have not traced the pathway from observation to mechanism. These warrants are moderate-to-strong (ceiling 0.80), but the ceiling drops as a function of replication diversity. A single correlation study has ceiling 0.55; multiple studies with diverse populations and designs have ceiling 0.80. The ATLAS system tiers EMPIRICAL_ASSOCIATION into four grades based on replication diversity (Cartwright, 2007, motivates this distinction).

4. **FUNCTIONAL warrants**: The belief describes a functional role that something plays without tracing the mechanism. "Views provide cognitive restoration" — we do not necessarily know how or why, but occupants are restored by looking. Ceiling is 0.65. These warrants are useful in applied contexts where the mechanism is inaccessible but the functional consequence is observable.

5. **CAPACITY warrants**: The belief describes a disposition or capacity. "High ceilings afford spatial thinking" — the environment has the capacity to elicit this cognitive state, even though we do not have direct evidence and the mechanism is obscure. Ceiling is 0.55. These warrants are common in architectural writing and can be useful for bootstrapping research, but they are epistemically weaker because capacity claims often go untested.

6. **ANALOGICAL warrants**: The belief is derived by analogy to another domain. "Since color has been shown to affect mood in merchandise displays, color in buildings should affect mood similarly." The reasoning from the source domain to the target domain carries evidential weight only if the analogy is sound. Ceiling is 0.40. These warrants are weak because analogies often break down in unanticipated ways, but they can be useful for hypothesis generation and in domains where direct evidence is sparse.

7. **THEORY_DERIVED warrants**: The belief is derived from a background theory without direct evidence. "If Predictive Processing theory is correct, then environmental complexity should modulate attention in the following way…" Ceiling is 0.25. These warrants are weakest because they are entirely theory-dependent and have no empirical grounding. But they are valuable for identifying where direct evidence is most needed.

The ceilings listed above are not discovered facts about epistemology. They are *calibrated values* — initial estimates based on expert judgment about the typical strength of different warrant types, which are refined empirically through the system's evaluation protocols. The ordering (CONSTITUTIVE > MECHANISM > EMPIRICAL_ASSOCIATION > … > THEORY_DERIVED) reflects genuine epistemological distinctions that any reasonable philosophy of evidence would endorse. The numbers are calibration parameters.

Why distinguish warrant types at all? Why not simply assign a single credence number to each belief? The reason is that the warrant type affects what research is needed to improve confidence. If a belief has an ANALOGICAL warrant, the research needed is not more data from the analogous domain but better understanding of whether the analogy actually holds in the target domain. If a belief has a MECHANISM warrant, the research needed is not more mechanism-tracing but evidence that the mechanism actually operates in applied conditions at sufficient magnitude. If a belief has an EMPIRICAL_ASSOCIATION warrant with low replication diversity, the research needed is more independent replications. By encoding the warrant type, the system encodes what *kind* of evidence would most improve the knowledge base.

## 2.3 Epistemic versus Aleatory Probability: The Two Uncertainty Systems

A critical distinction runs throughout the ATLAS framework: the distinction between epistemic and aleatory probability.

**Epistemic probability** is the scientific community's uncertainty about whether its theories, mechanisms, and models are correct. When the ATLAS system assigns a credence of 0.45 to the claim that spatial complexity facilitates creativity, it is describing the state of the field's knowledge. The credence captures how much doubt remains about this claim, given the current evidence, the strength of the warrant type, and coherence with other beliefs. This probability is about ignorance, not about the world.

**Aleatory probability** is the inherent variability of the world. When the BN specifies P(Mood = positive | Daylight = high) = 0.70, it is making a factual claim: in the population of occupants exposed to high daylight, approximately 70% experience positive mood. This is a fact about the world, independent of what we know. If the world changes (e.g., a different population, or different daylight conditions), the probability changes. If we learn more (e.g., more accurate measurements), the probability does not change — the frequency in the world is what it is.

This distinction has a long history in probability theory (Hacking, 1975; Hájek, 2019) and is standard in philosophy of science. What is distinctive about ATLAS is the architectural commitment to maintaining both kinds of uncertainty simultaneously in different data structures, with formal translation protocols between them.

The practical importance of distinguishing them cannot be overstated. Suppose an architect learns that "the probability that occupants will have positive mood given high daylight is 0.70." What research would improve this belief? The answer depends crucially on whether the 0.70 is epistemic or aleatory. If it is epistemic — the community's uncertainty about whether daylight-mood connection is real — then the research needed is better understanding of the mechanism (does it go through serotonin? circadian rhythm?) and better studies establishing the effect's existence and boundary conditions. If it is aleatory — the true population frequency — then the research needed is more occupant data to estimate the frequency more accurately. These are qualitatively different research activities with different costs, timelines, and knowledge outputs. Confusing them is a recipe for misdirected effort (collecting more data when the real problem is theoretical confusion) or worse (conducting elaborate mechanism studies when the practical need is more epidemiological data).

The EN tracks epistemic uncertainty through credences annotated with warrant types and grounding assessments. The BN tracks aleatory uncertainty through conditional probability tables. When the projection function π translates the EN to the BN, information is lost — the rich warrants and mechanisms are compressed into a single number — but this lossiness is by design. The EN is the primary knowledge representation, preserving all theoretical detail. The BN is a derived projection, optimized for prediction and intervention, regenerated whenever the EN revises (Cartwright, 2009, provides the philosophical motivation for keeping causal models derived from richer knowledge representations rather than taking them as primitive).

## 2.4 Woodward's Invariance and the Justification of Warrant Types

A skeptic might ask: why should the ordering CONSTITUTIVE > MECHANISM > EMPIRICAL_ASSOCIATION > ANALOGICAL be taken seriously? Is it not simply an arbitrary choice of how to label epistemically equivalent evidence?

James Woodward's (2003) account of causal invariance provides the answer. Woodward argues that a causal claim is explanatory and justifies inference across contexts (it "transfers") to the extent that it describes an invariant relationship. An invariant is a relationship that holds not just in the original context where it was discovered but also across a range of different conditions, interventions, and initial states.

A CONSTITUTIVE warrant describes the defining relationship between variables — it is invariant by definition. If we have defined stress as the activation of the HPA axis, then the relationship holds invariantly: whenever there is stress, there is HPA activation. The invariance is trivial but absolute.

A MECHANISM warrant describes an invariant causal pathway. The serotonergic pathway from light to mood is an invariant feature of mammalian neurobiology. It operates across different light types (as long as the light is photosynthetically active), different times of year (though with seasonal variation), and different species (mammals share this pathway). The mechanism was discovered in laboratory rats, but it transfers to humans because the underlying biological process is invariant across species.

An EMPIRICAL_ASSOCIATION warrant describes a regularity that has been observed and replicated but where the mechanism is unknown. "Patients with views of nature recover faster from surgery" is an empirical invariant that has been replicated in one laboratory setting (Ulrich's hospital) but it is not yet known whether the invariance extends to other hospitals, other types of surgery, other populations, or other types of views. Each successful replication across different conditions increases confidence that the invariance holds more broadly.

An ANALOGICAL warrant describes a structural correspondence between two domains with no guarantee that the correspondence predicts actual causal invariance. "Plant complexity affects mood through aesthetics, so architectural complexity should affect mood through aesthetics" assumes that the aesthetic pathway is analogous. But the structural similarity may not extend to the causal mechanisms — the emotional response to a plant might depend on biological factors (the biophilic response) that do not generalize to architectural elements. The analogy might break down in unanticipated ways.

A THEORY_DERIVED warrant describes a prediction from a general theory without empirical confirmation that the predicted invariance actually holds. "If Predictive Processing theory is correct, then visual complexity should modulate early visual processing" makes a causal claim derived from theory, but whether the theoretically-predicted relationship actually invariates across populations and conditions has not been tested.

Woodward's framework justifies the warrant ordering without appeal to convention. The ordering reflects genuine differences in how invariant (robust, transferable, replicable) the relationships are. This is not relativism — it is a discovery that some causal relationships are more invariant than others, and scientific evidence is stronger to the extent that it documents invariant relationships. The CONSTITUTIVE/MECHANISM distinction is the strongest because true definition and traced mechanisms are maximally invariant. The THEORY_DERIVED warrant is weakest because theoretical predictions often fail to invariate to the target domain. The EMPIRICAL_ASSOCIATION warrants are intermediate and tiered by replication diversity: a single observation is less invariant (applies only to the observed context) than a finding that has been replicated across diverse populations, designs, and conditions.

# 3. The Three-Layer Architecture: EN → π → BN

The ATLAS system rests on three formal structures that together constitute a computational epistemology: an Epistemic Network (EN), a projection function (π), and a Bayesian Network (BN). Each serves a distinct representational function, and the three are integrated such that no component could function as a standalone knowledge representation.

## 3.1 The Epistemic Network: Structure and Belief Representation

The Epistemic Network is a directed multigraph G = (N, E, τ_E, Θ) with four components:

**Nodes (N)** represent beliefs in environmental psychology. These beliefs are organized into three tiers reflecting their theoretical depth:

- **Tier 1 (T1) — Neuroscience Frameworks**: Eight foundational theories imported from cognitive neuroscience that apply across domains. These include Predictive Processing (Clark, 2013), describing perception and action as Bayesian inference; Stress Recovery Theory and Neuromodulation Systems (McEwen & Seeman, 1999), describing how environmental stressors activate the HPA axis and autonomic nervous system; Spatial Cognition and Navigation (O'Keefe & Nadel, 1978), describing how the brain constructs and uses spatial maps; Interoception and Affect Construction (Barrett, 2017), describing how the brain builds emotional states from bodily signals; and others. These theories are general across domains. Their nodes in the EN represent commitments to these general frameworks as they apply to architecture.

- **Tier 1.5 (T1.5) — Domain-Reduced Theories**: Fourteen theories specific to architecture and environmental psychology that have been formally reduced to T1 frameworks. Examples include Attention Restoration Theory (Kaplan & Kaplan, 1989), which has been reduced to Predictive Processing + Spatial Navigation; Stress Recovery Theory specific to nature (Ulrich, 1983), which reduces to Neuromodulation + HPA dynamics; Biophilia (Wilson, 1984), which reduces to multiple T1 frameworks; Prospect-Refuge Theory (Appleton, 1975), which reduces to Spatial Cognition + Default Mode Network. The reduction relationship is a causal claim: the T1.5 theory is implemented by mechanisms described in the T1 frameworks.

- **Tier 2 (T2) — Concrete Templates**: 208 specific causal claims about how environmental features affect occupant outcomes. "High daylight improves mood via serotonin pathway" is a T2 template. "Complex visual environments facilitate creative cognition" is another. "Prospect-refuge balance reduces psychological stress" is a third. Each T2 template is a concrete instantiation of one or more T1 or T1.5 theories, combined with empirical evidence from environmental psychology or neuroscience about the specific claim.

These three tiers form a hierarchy with deep epistemological structure. T1 beliefs are the most entrenched and change least frequently — they represent the field's commitment to broad theoretical frameworks. T1.5 beliefs represent the field's commitment that these general frameworks apply in the domain of architecture. T2 beliefs are most peripheral — they represent specific empirical claims that can be revised based on new evidence. When the EN must be revised in response to contradictory evidence (a core operation described in Section 3.4), the system preferentially revises T2 before T1.5 before T1, preserving the theoretical core while allowing specific claims to change.

**Edges (E)** represent relationships between beliefs. The system recognizes eight distinct edge types, each encoding a different kind of logical or evidential relationship:

1. **Reduction edges** connect lower-tier theories to higher-tier theories, representing the claim that a higher-tier theory is implemented by lower-tier mechanisms. "Attention Restoration reduces to Predictive Processing + Spatial Navigation" is a reduction edge.

2. **Bridge warrant edges** (seven subtypes, described fully in Section 4) connect theoretical claims to evidence. These are the epistemically critical edges encoding the quality and type of evidential support for a claim.

3. **Competition edges** connect alternative hypotheses that attempt to explain the same phenomenon. "Daylight affects mood via serotonin" and "Daylight affects mood via circadian entrainment" are competitors — both explain daylight-mood covariation, but through different mechanisms. Competition edges carry a resolution status (UNRESOLVED, VICTORY for one account, or COMPROMISE dividing the domain).

4. **Cross-template interaction edges** connect beliefs that share mechanisms or environmental inputs. If two templates both depend on HPA axis activation, the interaction edge records this shared dependency, preventing double-counting of effects and enabling synergistic effects to be modeled.

5. **Inheritance edges** represent parameter sharing. Multiple templates might use the same underlying parameter (e.g., HPA responsiveness), and inheritance edges ensure consistency and prevent contradictions when the parameter is updated.

6. **Working model edges** represent temporary theoretical commitments that constrain multiple beliefs. The Barrett-Craig two-stage interoceptive model, for instance, is a working model that defines how interoceptive predictions are generated and updated — it constrains multiple templates about emotion and stress without being a full T1 or T1.5 theory.

7. **Axiom edges** represent cross-cutting meta-parameters that modify all related beliefs. A dose-response axiom, for instance, specifies how effects scale with exposure magnitude — it applies to multiple templates without being theory-specific.

8. **Partial-out edges** represent scope restrictions preventing double-counting. When two beliefs would each claim a portion of an overall effect, partial-out edges ensure that the two portions do not overlap, summing to the whole but not exceeding it.

**Annotation function (Θ)** assigns to each node and edge structured metadata capturing its epistemological status. For each node, Θ records:

- **Credence** (c): A value in [0, 1] or an interval [c_lo, c_hi] representing the field's confidence in the belief. 0.7 means the field is fairly confident; 0.3 means the field is quite uncertain; 0.5 means genuinely divided opinion.

- **Bridge warrant ceiling** (w): The maximum credence that the current evidence type can justify for this belief, given its warrant type. A MECHANISM warrant ceiling is 0.80; an ANALOGICAL warrant ceiling is 0.40. The ceiling reflects the inherent strength of the evidence type (see Section 4).

- **Grounding assessment**: A tuple (evidence_quality, replication_diversity, methodological_rigor) summarizing how well the belief is grounded in empirical data. High grounding justifies credence; low grounding requires coherence to be strong to justify credence.

- **Toulmin structure** (Toulmin, 1958): A record of the data supporting the belief, the inferential warrant (bridge warrant type) connecting data to claim, the backing of the warrant (what justifies the warrant type), explicit qualifiers (scope conditions under which the warrant applies), rebuttals (conditions under which the warrant fails), and competing accounts (alternative hypotheses considered and their resolution status).

For each edge, Θ records:

- **Edge type** (τ_E): One of the eight types listed above.

- **Strength** (s): For credence-bearing edges (bridge warrants, interactions, inheritance), a weight in [0, 1] indicating the magnitude of the relationship.

- **Direction** (if applicable): Some edges have intrinsic asymmetry (reduction edges always point from T2 toward T1).

- **Valence** (if applicable): For interaction edges, whether the interaction is synergistic, antagonistic, or conditional.

The Toulmin structure is not decorative metadata. It is the epistemological substance that makes the EN fundamentally different from a knowledge graph or a probabilistic program. A knowledge graph stores facts — it cannot explain *why* it believes what it believes. A probabilistic program computes posterior probabilities — it takes its model structure as given and estimates parameters. The typed EN stores *justified beliefs* with explicit provenance: why the field believes what it believes, what evidence supports it, what alternative accounts have been considered, and what would change minds. This provenance is what enables the three core operations of the EN: (a) coherence assessment (evaluating how well beliefs fit together), (b) reflective equilibrium (updating beliefs to maximize overall coherence), and (c) value-of-information analysis (identifying which beliefs, if better understood, would most improve downstream predictions).

## 3.2 The Projection Function π: From Epistemic to Aleatory Probability

The projection function π translates the EN's epistemic credences into the BN's aleatory probabilities. It is the bridge across the two uncertainty systems.

The projection operates via a four-factor multiplicative formula:

$$\text{logit}(p_{\text{target}}) = d(\tau) \cdot \omega \cdot \delta(\text{pop}_{\text{source}}, \text{pop}_{\text{target}}) \cdot \text{logit}(p_{\text{lab}})$$

Where:
- **p_lab** is the credence assigned to the belief in the lab/theoretical context where it was developed (the EN node's credence value).
- **logit(p_lab)** converts this credence to log-odds space: logit(p) = log(p / (1-p)).
- **d(τ)** is the transfer reliability discount factor, which depends on the warrant type τ (CONSTITUTIVE = 0.95, MECHANISM = 0.80, …, THEORY_DERIVED = 0.25). This factor captures the degree to which the relationship is invariant across contexts.
- **ω** is the warrant strength, a judgment about how strong the evidence is for this particular instance of this warrant type. An EMPIRICAL_ASSOCIATION warrant might have canonical strength 0.80, but an EMPIRICAL_ASSOCIATION warrant with low replication diversity might have reduced strength ω = 0.60. This allows fine-grained calibration within warrant types.
- **δ(pop_source, pop_target)** is the population transfer factor, a measure of demographic and contextual similarity between the source population (where the evidence was gathered) and the target population (the occupants for whom the prediction is being made). If the source population is WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) and the target population is WEIRD, δ ≈ 0.90. If the source is WEIRD and the target is subsaharan African urban, δ might be ≈ 0.50, reflecting uncertainty about whether the relationship holds across very different populations.

The product d · ω · δ is a compound attenuation factor. Each component reduces the lab credence, capturing a different source of uncertainty in transferring knowledge from where it was developed to where it will be applied.

**Why log-odds space?** The formula operates in log-odds space rather than direct probability space because log-odds addition is the natural metric for combining independent evidence and because multiplication in log-odds space corresponds to simple multiplication of scaling factors (BonJour, 1985, motivates log-odds representation in epistemology; Pearl, 1988, provides the causal inference justification). In direct probability space, combining two pieces of evidence p1 and p2 requires the noisy-OR formula 1 - (1-p1)(1-p2), which is nonlinear and harder to interpret. In log-odds space, combining independent evidence is simply addition: logit(p_combined) = logit(p1) + logit(p2) + … This linearity is computationally and conceptually cleaner.

**Worked example**: Consider the claim "exposure to daylight improves occupant mood." In the lab, this has been established through both mechanism evidence (serotonergic pathway) and correlational evidence (patient recovery study). Suppose the EN assigns this claim:

- Credence p_lab = 0.72 (fairly confident, but not certain)
- Warrant type = MECHANISM (transfer reliability d = 0.80)
- Warrant strength ω = 0.85 (high-quality mechanism evidence with multiple pathway confirmations)
- Population transfer δ = 0.90 (source population is US office workers, target population is hospital patients, similar enough in demographics but different in stress context)

The projection computes:
- logit(0.72) = log(0.72 / 0.28) = 0.944
- Projected logit = 0.80 × 0.85 × 0.90 × 0.944 = 0.646
- p_target = 1 / (1 + e^(-0.646)) = 0.66

The projected credence of 0.66 is lower than the lab credence of 0.72, reflecting the uncertainties in transfer. The reduction is modest (0.72 → 0.66) because the mechanism is well-established and the populations are similar. If the warrant type had been ANALOGICAL instead (d = 0.40), the projected credence would be approximately 0.36, a much larger reduction reflecting the weak evidence type.

The projection function also handles *chains* of evidence, where a belief depends on multiple other beliefs. Consider a template claiming that "high-ceiling environments facilitate creative cognition via the mechanism of spatial representation freedom." This template depends on several supporting beliefs:

1. "High ceilings activate spatial representation mechanisms" (premise 1, credence c1)
2. "Spatial representation freedom facilitates creative cognition" (premise 2, credence c2)
3. "The mechanism linking (1) and (2) operates in buildings" (premise 3, credence c3)

In a chain, the credentials multiply. If all three premises are independent, the projected credence of the composite claim is:

$$c_{\text{composite}} = c_1 \cdot c_2 \cdot c_3$$

This captures the intuition that a chain is only as strong as its weakest link. If any premise has low credence, the composite credence drops substantially. In practice, environmental psychology claims often have several-step chains. Consider the following:

Window → Daylight exposure → Retinal stimulation → Raphe nucleus activation → Serotonin synthesis → Prefrontal cortex modulation → Mood improvement

Each step is a premise that must be true for the chain to hold. If we assign credences:
- Retinal stimulation / Daylight exposure: 0.95 (high mechanism confidence)
- Raphe activation / Retinal stimulation: 0.90
- Serotonin synthesis / Raphe activation: 0.85
- Prefrontal modulation / Serotonin synthesis: 0.75
- Mood improvement / Prefrontal modulation: 0.80

The composite chain credence is 0.95 × 0.90 × 0.85 × 0.75 × 0.80 = 0.49. A seven-step chain with these individual credences yields a composite credence around 0.50. This is the origin of the term *bottleneck effect*: long chains of reasoning can have high individual steps but low overall credence because multiplicative decrease compounds.

However, the EN can also represent *parallel evidence*, where multiple independent chains support the same conclusion. If "daylight affects mood" has evidence via both the serotonin pathway and the circadian rhythm pathway, these can be combined using log-odds addition (corresponding to noisy-OR in probability space) rather than multiplication:

$$\text{logit}(c_{\text{combined}}) = \text{logit}(c_1) + \text{logit}(c_2) + \text{logit}(c_3) - 2 \cdot \text{logit}(0.5)$$

The constants adjust to keep the formula in probability scale. With two independent chains, each with logit = 0.50 (equivalent to credence 0.62), the combined logit = 0.50 + 0.50 - 0 = 1.00 (equivalent to credence 0.73). Parallel evidence substantially increases confidence.

## 3.3 The Bayesian Network: Causal DAG with Projected Credences

The Bayesian Network (BN) is the system's interface to causal inference and prediction. It is a directed acyclic graph (DAG) where nodes represent measurable variables and edges represent direct causal relationships (Pearl, 2000). Each edge in the BN is parameterized by a conditional probability table (CPT) specifying P(child | parents).

In the ATLAS system, the BN's structure and parameters are derived entirely from the EN via the projection function π. The BN does not stand alone; it is always a projection of the current EN state. The relationship is asymmetric: the EN generates the BN, but not vice versa. If new evidence arrives and the EN is revised, the BN is regenerated from scratch.

The structure of the ATLAS BN reflects a compression. The EN contains approximately 130 nodes and 300-400 edges representing theoretical beliefs and their relationships. The BN contains approximately 25 environmental input variables (ceiling height, daylight level, acoustic condition, spatial configuration, biophilic elements, color, thermal comfort, and others) and 15 outcome variables (mood, stress, creativity, attentional restoration, productivity, place attachment, wellbeing, and others). The BN has approximately 80 edges.

Each BN edge represents a *collapsed chain* from the EN. Consider a complex mechanism chain in the EN like "Window → Daylight → Retinal stimulation → Raphe nucleus activation → Serotonin synthesis → Mood improvement." This chain, with all its intermediate steps and nuances, is compressed into a single BN edge from "Daylight level" node to "Mood" node. The CPT entry for this edge is computed by projecting through π, accounting for the chain's intermediate steps, warrant type, and population transfer factor. The compression is lossy — detailed information about the serotonin pathway is lost — but the loss is intentional. The compressed edge is optimized for prediction and intervention, not for explanation. For explanation, one consults the original EN chain.

The BN is the tool for answering interventional questions. Suppose an architect asks: "If I increase the ceiling height in this office from 8 feet to 10 feet, how much will productivity improve?" The BN can answer this via Pearl's do-calculus (Pearl, 2000). The do(ceiling_height = 10) intervention severs all incoming edges to ceiling_height and sets the variable to the specified value. The inference engine then propagates the intervention through the DAG using the CPTs, computing the posterior distribution over productivity. The answer integrates across all pathways by which ceiling height affects productivity (direct effects, indirect effects through creativity and mood, interactions with other variables) and produces a predicted change in productivity.

The EN cannot directly answer this question. It contains information about the relationship between ceiling height and spatial representation, and about spatial representation and creativity, but it does not provide the numerical machinery for do-calculus. The BN does. This is why the architectural separation is crucial: the EN provides understanding and can guide the design of new studies to resolve uncertainties; the BN provides prediction and can guide design decisions.

## 3.4 Coherence and Reflective Equilibrium: The EN's Revision Dynamics

The EN is not static. It must accommodate new evidence. The question is how.

Quine's original vision was that the web of belief should be revised holistically in response to contradictory evidence, guided by the principle of "minimal mutation" — the field should modify as few beliefs as possible and should preferentially modify peripheral beliefs rather than central ones. This holistic revision process is called *reflective equilibrium* by contemporary philosophers (Rawls, 1971; BonJour, 1985). The idea is that the field adjusts its web of beliefs until (a) no internal contradictions remain, (b) new evidence is accommodated, and (c) the overall system achieves coherence — a state where each belief is supported by the others, no substantial evidence remains unintegrated, and no competing accounts remain unresolved.

The ATLAS system implements reflective equilibrium algorithmically through a three-stage process:

1. **Incoming integration**: New evidence arrives (e.g., a newly published study). The system assigns it to a T2 template or creates a new T2 template, assigning it provisional credence and warrant type.

2. **Coherence assessment**: The system computes the overall coherence of the web, looking for: (a) internal contradictions (two beliefs that logically cannot both be true at credence above some threshold), (b) unintegrated evidence (evidence in the system that is not yet part of any theoretical chain), (c) unresolved competitions (two alternative accounts of the same phenomenon with neither clearly winning), and (d) anomalies (beliefs whose credence is much lower than expected given their warrant type and grounding).

3. **Targeted revision**: The system identifies the smallest set of revisions (preference: lower credences of T2 templates; second preference: change warrant type assignments; third preference: revise T1.5 theories; last preference: revise T1 frameworks) that would restore coherence. It proposes these revisions and requires expert judgment before implementing them.

The revision process is not automatic. The ATLAS system identifies incoherence but defers to human experts on how to resolve it. This is a design choice reflecting the recognition that coherence optimization, while important, is not the sole epistemic goal. Sometimes an apparently incoherent system is actually correct and the seemingly coherent alternatives are wrong. Sometimes maintaining modest incoherence preserves important contested terrain rather than forcing premature consensus. The role of the system is to *highlight* incoherence and *propose* revisions, not to *enforce* coherence.

Coherence itself is computed as a weighted combination of four factors:

$$\text{Coherence} = \alpha_1 \cdot \text{Consistency} + \alpha_2 \cdot \text{Integration} + \alpha_3 \cdot \text{Support} + \alpha_4 \cdot \text{Resolution}$$

where:
- **Consistency**: Absence of contradiction. Computed as 1 - (fraction of belief pairs that logically contradict at their assigned credence levels).
- **Integration**: Extent to which evidence is incorporated. Computed as the fraction of external evidence (from papers) that appears in the system.
- **Support**: Extent to which beliefs cohere (each belief is supported by neighboring beliefs). Computed using a spreading-activation algorithm where support flows from high-credence beliefs to adjacent low-credence beliefs.
- **Resolution**: Extent to which competitions are resolved rather than unresolved. Computed as the fraction of competition edges that have VICTORY or COMPROMISE status (rather than UNRESOLVED).

The weights α1, α2, α3, α4 are calibration parameters that reflect the field's priorities. In a fast-moving field with continual new evidence, α2 (integration) might be high; in a field prioritizing foundational understanding, α4 (resolution) might be high.

# 4. Warrant Types and Transfer Reliability

The seven warrant types are the connective tissue between the EN and the BN. They determine both how beliefs cohere in the EN and how evidence transfers when moving from the EN to the BN. This section specifies the warrant types in detail, explains the empirical and philosophical basis for the transfer reliability discount factors (the d values), and describes how warrant strength (ω) and population transfer factors (δ) are assigned.

## 4.1 The Seven Warrant Types: Hierarchy and Hierarchy Justification

The seven warrant types form a hierarchy from strongest to weakest on the basis of *causal invariance* — the degree to which the relationship holds across different conditions, populations, and interventions. The hierarchy is:

| Rank | Warrant Type | d Value | Justification | Typical Credence Range |
|------|--------------|---------|---------------|------------------------|
| 1 | CONSTITUTIVE | 0.95 | True by definition; invariant by stipulation | 0.70–1.00 |
| 2 | MECHANISM | 0.80 | Traced causal pathway; biologically implemented | 0.50–0.85 |
| 3 | EMPIRICAL_ASSOCIATION | 0.80–0.55 | Observed correlation; tiered by replication | 0.40–0.75 |
| 4 | FUNCTIONAL | 0.65 | Functional role without mechanism specification | 0.35–0.70 |
| 5 | CAPACITY | 0.55 | Dispositional property; often untested | 0.25–0.65 |
| 6 | ANALOGICAL | 0.40 | Cross-domain structural similarity | 0.15–0.50 |
| 7 | THEORY_DERIVED | 0.25 | Prediction from background theory; no direct evidence | 0.05–0.40 |

The transfer reliability values (d) represent empirical and philosophical judgments about how well each warrant type's relationships persist across contexts. The remainder of this section justifies each assignment.

**CONSTITUTIVE warrants (d = 0.95)** assert that the mechanism *is* the phenomenon. "Stress *is* HPA axis activation" (if we have defined stress neurophysiologically) means the relationship holds by definition. The d value is 0.95 rather than 1.00 to acknowledge that definitions can be revised and the constitutive relationship might not be the *complete* specification of the phenomenon (e.g., stress might include components beyond HPA activation). But the core relationship is as invariant as any relationship can be.

**MECHANISM warrants (d = 0.80)** assert that a causal pathway from antecedent to consequent has been traced through known biological or psychological processes. "Daylight stimulates the retinohypothalamic pathway → SCN entrainment → melatonin suppression → circadian phase shift" is a mechanism warrant. The d value of 0.80 reflects two considerations: (a) mechanism pathways are highly invariant — they describe how nature actually works and persist across most contexts; (b) but mechanism pathways discovered in laboratory conditions sometimes fail at full magnitude in applied conditions, and individual differences can modulate the pathway's strength. A mechanism that operates perfectly in vitro might operate with reduced effect in vivo. The 0.80 factor captures this modest uncertainty while preserving the strong warrant status.

**EMPIRICAL_ASSOCIATION warrants (d = 0.80 down to 0.55, tiered by replication)** assert that a statistical correlation or covariance has been observed. The d value is tiered based on replication diversity (Cartwright, 2007):
- Single study, no replication: d = 0.55. A single study showing that high daylight correlates with mood improvement is a weak basis for generalization.
- Two to three studies with similar populations: d = 0.60. Some replication increases confidence, but limited diversity is concerning.
- Four to eight studies across different populations/methodologies: d = 0.70. Substantial diversity across replications.
- Nine or more studies with diverse populations, designs, and settings: d = 0.80. Highly replicated relationships.

The rationale is that empirical associations that have been observed in multiple populations and study designs are more likely to reflect invariant relationships (as opposed to population-specific artifacts, publication bias, or methodological idiosyncracies). The tiering recognizes that more diverse replication provides stronger grounds for confidence that the relationship will transfer to new contexts.

**FUNCTIONAL warrants (d = 0.65)** assert that something plays a functional role in a system without the mechanism being specified. "Nature views provide psychological restoration" asserts the functional consequence (restoration) without tracing the mechanism (is it stress reduction? attention restoration? biophilic response?). The d value of 0.65 reflects that functional claims, when well-supported by observations, are fairly invariant — if something *does* restore people, it likely will continue to do so — but without mechanism understanding, scope conditions are unclear. Might functional role depend on context in unexpected ways? The 0.65 value expresses modest uncertainty about whether the functional role will transfer to different populations or settings.

**CAPACITY warrants (d = 0.55)** assert that something has a capacity or disposition to produce an effect, often without direct evidence that the effect actually manifests. "High ceilings afford spatial thinking" is a capacity claim — the environment has the capacity to elicit this cognitive state. The warrant is weak (d = 0.55) because capacity claims are often made without empirical testing and rest on intuition about what an environment makes possible. Possibilities often remain unrealized in practice.

**ANALOGICAL warrants (d = 0.40)** argue that because relationship A holds in domain X, a structurally similar relationship B should hold in domain Y. "Since color has been shown to affect mood in merchandise displays (domain X: retail), color in buildings should affect mood similarly (domain Y: architecture)." Analogies can be insightful but are unreliable guides to causal invariance. The structural similarity might not correspond to causal similarity. The emotional response to color in a retail setting (brief exposure, deliberate marketplace framing) might operate through different mechanisms than color response in a building (sustained exposure, different contextual framing). The d value of 0.40 reflects deep skepticism about transfer — the relationship must be re-established empirically in the target domain; analogy from the source domain provides at best an educated guess.

**THEORY_DERIVED warrants (d = 0.25)** rest entirely on prediction from background theory. "If Predictive Processing theory is correct, then environmental complexity should modulate early visual processing" makes a causal claim derived from theory alone, without direct evidence that the theoretically-predicted relationship actually manifests. The d value of 0.25 is very weak. Theoretical predictions often fail to transfer to real-world conditions because (a) the theory is incomplete or incorrect, (b) the theory-to-application mapping requires assumptions that do not hold in practice, or (c) confounding factors in the real world overwhelm the predicted effect. Theory-derived warrants are most useful in nascent domains where empirical evidence is scarce and need to be promoted to stronger warranties through targeted empirical research.

## 4.2 Warrant Strength: Fine-Grained Calibration Within Warrant Types

The d value specifies the canonical discount factor for each warrant type. But a particular instance of a warrant type might be stronger or weaker than the canonical value based on the quality of the evidence. This is where warrant strength (ω) comes in.

Warrant strength is assigned by expert judgment, typically during panel calibration. An expert panel evaluating a claim such as "high daylight improves mood" might assign:
- Warrant type: EMPIRICAL_ASSOCIATION (canonical d = 0.80 for highly replicated associations)
- Warrant strength ω: 0.75

The ω value of 0.75 (rather than the canonical 0.80) might reflect that while the association is well-replicated, the studies vary substantially in how they operationalize "mood" and "daylight," or there is publication bias toward positive findings, or the effect size is smaller than originally claimed. The reduced ω captures these nuances within the warrant type.

Conversely, an ANALOGICAL warrant (canonical d = 0.40) for a claim such as "circadian rhythm disruption affects mood via melatonin suppression" might be assigned ω = 0.60 because (a) the analogy between circadian systems in animals and humans is very strong, (b) melatonin's role in mood is well-established in human neuroscience, and (c) the mechanism is understood in detail. The higher ω reflects that this particular analogical warrant is stronger than the canonical 0.40 would suggest.

Warrant strength is thus a calibration knob that allows the system to express fine-grained uncertainty within warrant types. The formula d · ω produces the effective discount factor for a particular claim:

$$d_{\text{effective}} = d(\tau) \cdot \omega$$

For the daylight-mood claim: d_eff = 0.80 × 0.75 = 0.60
For the circadian claim: d_eff = 0.40 × 0.60 = 0.24

## 4.3 Population Transfer Factors: Cross-Context Generalization

The final component of the projection function is the population transfer factor δ(pop_source, pop_target), which captures demographic and contextual similarity between the source population (where evidence was gathered) and the target population (where the evidence will be applied).

Population transfer is a complex question in philosophy of science and statistics (Cartwright, 1989; Pearl & Bareinboim, 2014). The question is: if a causal relationship holds in population X (college students in a US laboratory), will it also hold in population Y (hospital patients in rural India)? The answer depends on the *causal mechanism*. If the mechanism is universal (e.g., basic neurophysiology), transfer probability is high. If the mechanism depends on context (e.g., cultural attitudes toward nature), transfer probability is lower.

In the ATLAS system, δ is estimated by expert judgment, considering:

1. **Demographic similarity**: Age, gender, SES, education, cultural background, neurodiversity status. Greater similarity → higher δ.

2. **Contextual similarity**: Built environment characteristics, climate, social norms, technological context. Greater similarity → higher δ.

3. **Mechanism universality**: If the mechanism is understood and known to be universal (e.g., photobiological response to light), δ can be high even with large demographic differences. If the mechanism is unknown or potentially context-dependent, δ is lower.

**Canonical δ values** for common comparisons:

| Source → Target | δ | Justification |
|-----------------|---|---------------|
| US college students → US general population | 0.90 | Similar age/education, but broader SES/health |
| US college students → Western general population | 0.85 | Similar development level, different culture |
| US college students → Global population | 0.60 | Large demographic/contextual differences |
| Laboratory (controlled) → Real building (uncontrolled) | 0.75 | More variability, confounds in real building |
| Single building → Other buildings of same type | 0.80 | Architectural variability |
| Single climate → Different climate | 0.70 | Potential mechanism modulation by climate |

These are initial estimates to be refined empirically. The ATLAS system includes a module for *transportability analysis* (Pearl & Bareinboim, 2014) that can adjust δ when causal mechanisms are well-understood. For instance, if a mechanism is fully specified (e.g., "daylight → retinal stimulation → raphe nucleus"), then δ can be high even across large demographic gaps, because the biological mechanism is universal. If a mechanism is under-specified (e.g., "nature → psychological benefit"), then δ must be conservative, because contextual and cultural factors might modulate the effect.

## 4.4 Combining the Four Factors: The Projection Formula in Full

Bringing together all four factors, the projection formula becomes:

$$\text{logit}(p_{\text{target}}) = d(\tau) \cdot \omega \cdot \delta(\text{pop}_{\text{source}}, \text{pop}_{\text{target}}) \cdot \text{logit}(p_{\text{lab}})$$

**Example: Projecting the Daylight-Mood Relationship**

A panel assigns:
- Lab credence p_lab = 0.72 (fairly confident based on observational and mechanism evidence)
- Warrant type τ = MECHANISM (d = 0.80 for well-traced biological mechanisms)
- Warrant strength ω = 0.85 (high-quality mechanism evidence with multiple pathway confirmations)
- Population transfer δ = 0.90 (source is light-exposure studies in normal populations; target is hospital patients, broadly similar enough)

Calculation:
- logit(0.72) = log(0.72/0.28) = 0.944
- d · ω · δ = 0.80 × 0.85 × 0.90 = 0.612
- Projected logit = 0.612 × 0.944 = 0.578
- p_target = 1 / (1 + e^(-0.578)) = 0.64

The projected credence is 0.64, lower than the lab credence of 0.72, reflecting uncertainty in transfer.

**Bottleneck Effect in Chains**

When a claim depends on multiple premises, each with its own warrant, the discounts multiply. Consider ceiling height → spatial representation → creative cognition:

Premise 1: "Ceiling height affects spatial representation"
- d = 0.80 (MECHANISM warrant), ω = 0.75, δ = 0.85
- d_eff = 0.80 × 0.75 × 0.85 = 0.51

Premise 2: "Spatial representation affects creativity"
- d = 0.65 (FUNCTIONAL warrant), ω = 0.70, δ = 0.90
- d_eff = 0.65 × 0.70 × 0.90 = 0.41

Composite: p_target = p_premise1 × p_premise2 (in probability space) or logit_target = logit_premise1 + logit_premise2 (in log-odds space, for independent evidence).

In log-odds space with logit(p) = 1.0 for each premise:
Projected logit = 0.51 × 1.0 + 0.41 × 1.0 = 1.51
p_target = 1 / (1 + e^(-1.51)) = 0.82

But in chain (multiplicative) rather than parallel (additive) space:
p_target = 0.70 × 0.63 = 0.44 (assuming logit(0.70) ≈ 0.80, logit(0.63) ≈ 0.53)

The bottleneck effect is substantial: a three-step chain with moderate-strength evidence at each step yields much lower overall credence than parallel evidence for the same conclusion.

### Section 5: The Template Library and Three-Tier Theory Architecture

#### 5.1 Overview of the Template Library

The empirical foundation of ATLAS rests upon a curated library of 208 causal templates distributed across twelve environmental psychology domains: daylighting, thermal comfort, acoustics, spatial configuration, biophilic design, color, wayfinding, spatial density, flexibility, materiality, social infrastructure, and cross-cutting effects. This library represents a comprehensive synthesis of environmental psychology research conducted over the past four decades, organized according to a principled schema that preserves both the specificity of empirical findings and the generalizability necessary for design application. Each template encodes a localized causal relationship between an environmental variable (e.g., illuminance level, correlated color temperature, reverberation time) and an outcome of interest (e.g., circadian entrainment, stress recovery, wayfinding efficiency). Rather than treating these templates as isolated correlations, ATLAS situates each within a structured theoretical hierarchy that explicitly connects empirical associations to underlying mechanisms and, ultimately, to first-principles neuroscientific frameworks. This architecture accomplishes what has long been elusive in environmental psychology: a systematic method for translating laboratory findings into design guidance while maintaining epistemic transparency about the theoretical grounds supporting each translation.

The construction of the template library proceeded through a systematic literature synthesis conducted by teams of domain specialists in collaboration with methodologically trained reviewers. For each domain, the process began with comprehensive searches across PubMed, PsycINFO, and Web of Science, using domain-specific terminology and appropriate date ranges. Retrieved studies were screened for inclusion according to explicit criteria: published in peer-reviewed venues, reporting quantitative effect sizes or sufficient statistical information for reconstruction, describing clear environmental interventions, and measuring psychological or health outcomes with validated instruments. For studies meeting these criteria, teams extracted effect sizes, study designs, sample characteristics, environmental specifications, and measured outcomes. Multiple effect sizes from the same study were retained when they addressed different outcome variables. This process yielded the initial set of approximately 1,200 empirical associations, which were then consolidated into 208 distinct causal templates through clustering and deduplication. Templates representing equivalent causal pathways were merged; templates addressing substantially different mechanisms or population groups were retained as separate entries. The resulting library represents a compression of the literature without a corresponding loss of causal precision—multiple empirical findings contribute to confidence in a single template, but distinct causal relationships remain properly delineated.

#### 5.2 The Three-Tier Theory Organization: From Neuroscience to Empirics

ATLAS organizes its theoretical commitments across three tiers, each serving a distinct epistemic function. The first tier (T1) comprises ten foundational neuroscience frameworks that represent current consensus-level understanding of basic neural organization and function. These frameworks—Predictive Processing (Friston, 2010), the Default Mode Network (Raichle & Snyder, 2007), Homeostatic Regulation (Sterling & Eyer, 1988), Interoception (Craig, 2009), Salience Detection (Seeley, 2019), Approach-Avoidance (Elliot, 2006), Cognitive Load Theory (Sweller, 1988), Reward Prediction (O'Doherty, 2004), Social Cognition (Mitchell, 2011), and Temporal Dynamics (Buonomano & Maass, 2009)—are not novel theoretical claims but rather well-established findings from cognitive neuroscience that the system adopts as background assumptions. The role of T1 is not to advance neuroscience but to provide a unified conceptual vocabulary and mechanistic foundation for environmental psychology claims. Each T1 framework is treated as a relatively stable background theory, updated incrementally when major new findings warrant revision but maintained across versions to ensure causal stability.

The second tier (T1.5) contains fourteen formally reduced domain theories that bridge between T1 neuroscience and empirical psychology. These theories—including Affective Regulation Theory (ART), Biophilia Hypothesis, Prospect-Refuge Theory, Attention Restoration Theory, Environmental Stress Model, Ambient Affect Theory, Wayfinding Cognitive Load, Density Effects Model, Flexibility-Adaptation Framework, Materiality Perception, Social Infrastructure Effects, Cross-Environment Transfer, Domain-Specific Learning, and Individual Differences Modulation—represent genuine theoretical contributions to environmental psychology. Critically, these T1.5 theories are not independent of T1; rather, they are partially reduced to T1 mechanisms while retaining irreducible residuals that cannot yet be fully explained from first principles. This partial reduction is the key innovation distinguishing ATLAS from purely bottom-up or purely top-down approaches. Consider Affective Regulation Theory: this theory is partially grounded in T1 frameworks for homeostatic regulation, interoception, and the default mode network, allowing ATLAS to connect environmental design features to specific neural mechanisms of affect modulation. However, ART also contains domain-specific claims about how particular environmental features (e.g., visual complexity, color cues, thermal gradients) interact with affective processes in ways that cannot yet be fully derived from neuroscience alone. This residual domain knowledge is preserved and probabilistically weighted rather than dismissed as merely phenomenological.

The process of formal reduction that connects T1.5 to T1 proceeds according to principles articulated in the mechanistic philosophy of science (Craver, 2007; Machamer, Darden & Craver, 2000). When a T1.5 theory makes a causal claim—for instance, that "salient natural elements reduce attentional fatigue"—ATLAS decomposes this claim into two components: (1) a mechanistic path through T1 frameworks (salience detection via superior temporal sulcus and anterior insula, attention restoration via anterior cingulate and prefrontal cortex); and (2) a domain-specific residual (why natural elements specifically trigger these mechanisms more effectively than artificial stimuli with equivalent salience). The mechanistic portion receives a high warrant type (typically MECHANISM, discount factor 0.80) in the epistemic network, while the residual receives a lower warrant type (EMPIRICAL_ASSOCIATION tiered at 0.55-0.80 depending on replication count and study quality). This dual grounding structure means that T1.5 theories benefit from the explanatory power of neuroscience while maintaining appropriate epistemic humility about domain-specific claims. Importantly, this structure also creates clear points at which future neuroscience advances can update T1.5 theories: if mechanisms currently treated as domain-specific residuals become explicable from T1, the warrant type and discount factor automatically shift upward, reflecting improved mechanistic grounding.

The third tier (T2) comprises 208 empirical templates, each representing a specific, testable causal relationship extracted from the literature. These templates are the leaves of the theory tree: they inherit mechanistic grounding from T1.5 theories and, transitively, from T1 frameworks, but they also incorporate empirical specificity regarding dose-response relationships, population effects, boundary conditions, and interaction patterns that cannot be predicted from higher-tier theories alone. Unlike a naive empiricist approach that treats each finding as isolated, ATLAS uses T2 templates to accumulate evidence that either confirms or revises T1.5 theories. When multiple T2 templates converge on a common mechanistic narrative, this convergence increases confidence in the relevant T1.5 theory. Conversely, when T2 templates diverge in ways not predicted by T1.5, this signals either that the T1.5 theory requires revision or that important contextual moderators have been overlooked. The bidirectional relationship between T2 and T1.5 ensures that the system evolves in response to empirical accumulation while maintaining theoretical coherence.

#### 5.3 Mechanism Chains: Environmental Inputs to Neural Substrates to Outcomes

ATLAS represents the causal pathway from environmental design to psychological outcome as an explicit mechanism chain, following the levels-of-organization framework developed in mechanistic philosophy of science (Bechtel & Abrahamsen, 2005; Craver, 2007). A mechanism chain for a given template specifies the sequence of entities and activities connecting environmental variable to measured outcome, with each step localized to a particular level of organization. Consider the mechanism chain for "daylight and hospital recovery," one of the most extensively studied relationships in environmental psychology. The chain begins at the physical level: photons at specific wavelengths (primarily 460-480 nanometers for circadian effects) enter the retina and activate intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs). This describes an entity (ipRGC) and an activity (photon capture and transduction). The mechanism then steps to the neural level: ipRGC firing projects to the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), which is the master circadian pacemaker. The SCN activity constitutes an entity-activity pair (neural oscillator engaged in circadian phase adjustment). The mechanism steps again to the physiological level: the SCN coordinates the secretion of melatonin from the pineal gland, establishing a circadian rhythm in cortisol, core body temperature, and other homeostatic variables. Each of these variables constitutes an entity and its dynamics constitute an activity. The mechanism steps finally to the psychological level: circadian alignment facilitates sleep consolidation, reduces delirium incidence, improves immune function, and accelerates wound healing in hospitalized patients. The outcome measures—sleep quality, delirium rating scale, discharge date—are psychological and behavioral phenomena dependent on these physiological rhythms.

This single mechanism chain, however, does not exhaust the causal pathways by which daylight affects recovery. Daylight also activates the suprageniculatehypothalamic tract, which connects to the ventral tegmental area and basal forebrain, affecting dopamine-mediated arousal and salience detection. This alternative pathway may influence attentional engagement, motivation for rehabilitation efforts, and mood—all of which independently affect recovery trajectories. The complete causal picture for daylight and recovery requires specifying multiple mechanism chains in parallel, each grounded in distinct T1 frameworks. ATLAS handles this by encoding competing mechanism chains within the epistemic network, each with its own warrant type and credence value. The parallel contribution model (detailed in Section 6) then combines these mechanisms into a single estimate of the total effect size. This approach preserves the causal specificity necessary for mechanistic understanding while incorporating the empirical reality that environmental features typically affect outcomes through multiple pathways simultaneously.

The precision with which mechanism chains must be specified has implications for the discount factors applied in the projection function. A template grounded in a fully articulated mechanism chain—one tracing a clear path from environmental variable through identified neural substrates to measured outcome—receives a higher warrant type (MECHANISM, d = 0.80) than a template based on empirical association alone. However, the empirical measurement of an outcome variable adds an additional consideration: outcome measures are often psychologically proximal (e.g., self-reported mood) rather than the most distal theoretical variable (e.g., neural activity in nucleus accumbens). ATLAS applies a separate credibility assessment to the relationship between the measured outcome and the theoretical outcome, which moderates the overall warrant type. For instance, in the daylight-recovery example, discharge date is a more distal and behaviorally meaningful outcome than sleep quality (though both are relevant), and this hierarchy is reflected in warrant type assignment. This approach ensures that the system captures both the mechanistic depth provided by neuroscience and the pragmatic constraints of measurement in applied psychological research.

#### 5.4 Calibration Methodology: Expert Panels and Toulmin Argument Structure

The credence values and warrant type assignments in the epistemic network are not determined algorithmically but through structured expert calibration. ATLAS employed twelve expert panels, each comprising four to six researchers with substantial publication records in their respective domains (daylighting, thermal comfort, acoustics, and so forth). The panels were convened over eighteen months, with quarterly meetings for each domain. Prior to each panel meeting, domain specialists prepared detailed summary dossiers for each template: a structured summary of the empirical literature, identification of competing mechanism chains, quantitative synthesis of effect sizes, specification of population characteristics, and preliminary warrant type recommendations. These dossiers were provided to panelists two weeks prior to meetings, allowing time for independent review and preliminary calibration.

During in-person (or videoconference) panel meetings, template credences were calibrated using a structured argument methodology adapted from Toulmin's (1958) model of practical argument. Each template discussion proceeded according to a standard format: (1) claim statement—the core causal assertion encoded in the template; (2) data presentation—quantitative summaries of supporting empirical evidence; (3) backing statement—articulation of the T1.5 theoretical rationale and T1 mechanistic grounding; (4) qualifier specification—explicit statement of boundary conditions and scope constraints; (5) rebuttal consideration—identification of competing explanations, negative findings, or methodological limitations; and (6) competing accounts reconciliation—explicit discussion of how the proposed mechanism relates to and differs from alternative theoretical models. This structure ensures that credence assignments are not merely consensus votes but are explicitly grounded in evidence, theory, and acknowledged uncertainty.

The calibration process resulted in credence distributions rather than point estimates. For each template, panelists indicated not only their central credence but also the range of uncertainty (typically specified as a 90-percent credible interval). These distributions were then fitted to beta distributions, which are natural priors for binary events (the template is causal vs. not causal) and provide computational convenience for Bayesian updating. Warrant type assignments followed a parallel process: panelists indicated which warrant type best characterized the evidential basis for each template, and secondary discussion addressed whether this warrant type was justified given the available evidence and theoretical grounding. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Krippendorff's alpha (Krippendorff, 2011), with a threshold of κ > 0.75 required for final approval. Templates with lower inter-rater reliability (indicating genuine disagreement among panelists) were either revised for clarity or retained with a note indicating substantial disagreement, which moderates the credence value downward.

This calibration approach honors the distinction between epistemic confidence and actual causal probability. A template might have high empirical support (many well-designed studies) but be grounded in a mechanism chain that seems probabilistically unlikely given other biological constraints. The structured argument format allows panelists to articulate this intuition and adjusts credences accordingly. Conversely, a template with modest empirical support but very strong theoretical grounding might receive relatively higher credence if panelists judge the mechanistic argument compelling. This flexibility reflects the reality that expert judgment integrates multiple sources of evidence, and the structured format simply makes that integration explicit and auditable.

#### 5.5 Template Metadata and the Specification of Causal Boundaries

Each template in the library carries an extensive metadata structure that specifies the conditions under which the causal relationship holds and the manner in which it varies across contexts. This metadata serves multiple functions: (1) it enables the projection function to apply appropriate population transfer factors; (2) it supports the Bayesian network in encoding moderation and interaction effects; and (3) it provides transparency regarding the limitations of each template's applicability. The metadata dimensions are as follows.

Credence and ceiling specify the probability that the template correctly encodes the causal relationship and the maximum probable effect size observed in the literature, respectively. Warrant type and discount factor specify the primary evidential warrant supporting the template (e.g., EMPIRICAL_ASSOCIATION, MECHANISM, CAPACITY) and the associated reduction in confidence when applying the template to new populations. Scope conditions enumerate the specific populations, contexts, and measurement domains for which the template has been empirically validated. For instance, a template concerning "thermal comfort and cognitive performance" might be scoped to adult office workers in sedentary tasks with normal BMI, acute exposure windows of two to four hours, and outcome measures of sustained attention or reasoning tasks. Templates are scoped conservatively to avoid overgeneralization, and templates addressing related but distinct scope conditions are maintained as separate entries. Effect size and replication count provide quantitative information about the magnitude of the relationship and the number of independent replications in the literature. Replication count particularly influences the tiered discount factors within EMPIRICAL_ASSOCIATION warrant type: templates with fewer than three independent replications receive a discount factor of 0.55, while those with five or more replications receive 0.80. This approach acknowledges that replication is among the strongest signals of causal robustness.

Time horizon specifies the temporal window during which the effect is expected to manifest. Some environmental effects emerge immediately upon exposure (e.g., acoustic startle), while others require sustained exposure or recovery periods (e.g., circadian adaptation to light exposure). Dose-response relationships articulate how the magnitude of effect scales with the intensity or duration of the environmental variable, allowing the projection function to interpolate or extrapolate beyond the empirically measured range. Individual differences note known moderators such as age, circadian chronotype, personality traits, or prior environmental history. Cross-template interactions identify cases where the effect of one template is modified by the presence of another—for instance, the effect of color on mood may be reduced when thermal comfort is compromised. This metadata structure transforms the template library from a simple database of correlations into a rich specification of causal relationships, complete with boundary conditions and interaction patterns.

### Section 6: Worked Examples—From Lab Finding to Design Decision

#### 6.1 Daylight and Hospital Recovery: The Complete Projection Pathway

The relationship between daylighting and patient recovery in hospital settings provides an exemplary case study of the ATLAS projection pipeline. This relationship has been the subject of extensive research, most prominently Ulrich's (1984) classic study demonstrating that patients with views of natural scenes had significantly shorter hospital stays and required fewer analgesic medications compared to patients viewing walls. Subsequent research by Boubekri, Lee, MacNaughton, Whaley, Miwa, and Kawakubo (2014), Huisman, Morales, van Hoof, and Kort (2012), and others has confirmed and extended these findings, though effect sizes vary substantially across studies and populations. The ATLAS treatment of this relationship begins by identifying the distinct mechanism chains that explain why daylight affects recovery.

The primary mechanism chain connects daylighting to recovery through circadian rhythm entrainment. Daylight at illuminance levels of 500-2000 lux activates intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells, which project to the suprachiasmatic nucleus and entrain the endogenous circadian oscillator to the 24-hour light-dark cycle. This entrainment stabilizes circadian rhythms in core body temperature, cortisol secretion, melatonin production, and sleep-wake behavior. The stabilized circadian rhythm, in turn, improves sleep consolidation, reduces delirium incidence (of particular importance in hospitalized elderly patients), enhances immune function, and accelerates wound healing. The mechanism chain progresses from photoreception (retinal level) to circadian pacemaking (hypothalamic level) to physiological rhythmicity (endocrine and immune levels) to recovery outcomes (clinical level). Each step in this chain corresponds to a distinct level of organization and is grounded in T1 frameworks: the photoreception step relies on the Homeostatic Regulation T1 framework, the circadian pacemaking step on Temporal Dynamics, and the immune enhancement step on Homeostatic Regulation. This mechanistic grounding elevates the primary daylight mechanism to MECHANISM warrant type with a baseline discount factor of 0.80.

A secondary mechanism chain operates through affective and attentional pathways. Natural views, in addition to providing circadian photons, are visually salient and emotionally positive. This salience activates the suprageniculatehypothalamic tract and ventral tegmental area, increasing dopamine-mediated arousal and engagement. Emotional positivity activates the nucleus accumbens reward system, reducing stress responsivity and supporting mood. Engagement and mood independently predict better adherence to medical recommendations, more active participation in physical rehabilitation, and faster psychological adjustment to hospitalization (Herrera-Perez & Callanan, 2020). This secondary mechanism is grounded in T1 frameworks for Salience Detection, Approach-Avoidance, and Reward Prediction. The pathway, however, does not explain circadian effects per se but rather operates through motivational and emotional channels. This secondary mechanism receives EMPIRICAL_ASSOCIATION warrant type with a tiered discount factor of 0.70, reflecting substantial empirical support (six independent replications) but lesser mechanistic specificity compared to the circadian pathway.

The expert panel for daylighting calibrated the primary circadian mechanism as having a credence of 0.68 in the epistemic network. This credence reflects high confidence in the basic neuroscience of circadian entrainment, strong empirical evidence from sleep labs and chronobiology research, but acknowledged uncertainty about the sufficiency of this mechanism for explaining all daylight-recovery relationships. The secondary affective mechanism was calibrated at 0.52 credence, indicating less consensus and greater residual uncertainty about its contribution. The two mechanisms are encoded in the epistemic network as distinct causal claims, each with associated warrant types and credences. The parallel contribution model (detailed below) combines these mechanisms into a single template credence for the overall daylight-recovery relationship. The combined template credence for daylight and recovery emerges at 0.72, calculated as P(circadian mechanism) + P(affective mechanism) - P(both) = 0.68 + 0.52 - (0.68 × 0.52) ≈ 0.72.

Now consider the application of this template to a design decision: a hospital renovating a patient ward seeks to maximize recovery-supporting design features within budget constraints. The ATLAS system is queried with this scenario, specifying the target population (mixed acute care patients, age 18-85, mixed diagnoses), the design intervention (installing south-facing windows with daylight controls), and the outcome of interest (length of stay). The projection function implements the pipeline: π(p_lab, d, ω, δ) = logit^{-1}[d · ω · δ(pop, pop_target) · logit(p_lab)], where p_lab is the laboratory credence (0.72 for daylight-recovery), d is the discount factor (average of 0.75 across the two mechanisms), ω is the warrant strength (0.72), and δ is the population transfer factor.

The population transfer factor δ reflects differences between the laboratory research context (which has involved diverse settings but primarily patient populations in temperate climates with high-quality hospital infrastructure) and the target context (the specific hospital, patient mix, and climate). The expert panel assigned δ = 0.85 to this transfer, reflecting moderate confidence that the documented daylight effects will generalize. The reduction from 1.0 reflects uncertainty about specific factors: variation in individual chronotypes and sleep disorders in the target population, potential confounding with nighttime light exposure (artificial lighting practices vary across hospitals), and variation in window placement and physical access in the specific building. The projection function thus yields logit(p_target) = 0.75 × 0.72 × 0.85 × logit(0.72) ≈ 0.58, suggesting that the projected probability of meaningful recovery improvement from daylighting in this target context is approximately 0.58. This estimate can then be incorporated into the Bayesian network alongside other design recommendations (thermal comfort, acoustic treatment, spatial configuration) to identify the portfolio of design changes most likely to improve recovery outcomes given the hospital's constraints.

#### 6.2 Noise and Productivity: Competing Mechanisms and Parallel Contribution

Noise effects on productivity provide a more complex case study in which multiple competing mechanisms contribute to a single outcome, and the ATLAS system must integrate these mechanisms while maintaining clarity about their distinct causal pathways. The relevant empirical literature is extensive: Banbury and Berry (2005) found that open-office noise reduced proofreading accuracy; Jahncke, Hygge, and Halin (2005) demonstrated cognitive impairment in complex reasoning tasks exposed to traffic noise; Hygge, Evans, and Bullinger (2002) showed combined effects of noise and air quality on cognitive performance. However, these studies often do not specify which mechanism(s) explain the noise effect, and different noise types (speech, mechanical, traffic) appear to affect performance through different pathways.

The primary mechanism chain connecting noise to reduced productivity operates through stress-induced cognitive load. Unexpected or unpredictable noise activates the amygdala's threat detection system, triggering a stress response mediated by the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and sympathetic nervous system. This stress response consumes prefrontal cortical resources necessary for executive function and working memory maintenance. The T1 framework most relevant here is Predictive Processing (Friston, 2010): noise violates predictions about the auditory environment, creating prediction errors that the brain must resolve, diverting attention from primary tasks. The result is reduced performance on cognitively demanding tasks, particularly those requiring sustained attention or working memory. This mechanism is empirically grounded in studies combining noise exposure with neuroimaging, cortisol assays, and cognitive testing (e.g., Banbury & Berry, 2005). The warrant type is MECHANISM, grounded explicitly in HPA axis biology and predictive processing, with a discount factor of 0.80. The expert panel's credence for this mechanism was 0.75, reflecting strong supporting evidence.

A secondary mechanism operates through attention fragmentation. Speech-containing noise provides linguistic content that intrinsically captures attention, particularly speech in one's native language or containing semantically relevant information (the "cocktail party problem" and its pathological inverse). Attentional capture by speech-containing noise interrupts ongoing tasks, and recovery from these interruptions requires reinstatement of task context and working memory contents, creating performance decrements even when overall noise level (in decibels) is moderate. The T1 framework is Salience Detection and Attention: the anterior insula and temporoparietal junction detect behaviorally relevant sounds, particularly speech (Mitchell, 2011). This mechanism receives EMPIRICAL_ASSOCIATION warrant type (evidence from attention studies, but less direct mechanistic grounding than the stress pathway) with a tiered discount factor of 0.70 and a credence of 0.62 from the expert panel.

A tertiary mechanism involves motivation reduction. Chronically noisy environments create a perception of low environmental control and predictability, which, in turn, reduce intrinsic motivation and effort allocation to primary tasks. This pathway is grounded in T1 frameworks for Homeostatic Regulation (the stress of unpredictability) and Approach-Avoidance (disengagement from aversive environments). Empirical support exists but is more indirect, typically measured through engagement questionnaires rather than primary task performance. This mechanism receives CAPACITY warrant type (the capacity for noisy environments to undermine motivation) with a tiered discount factor of 0.55 and a credence of 0.48.

The parallel contribution model integrates these three mechanisms. Let P_s denote the probability of the stress mechanism, P_a the attention fragmentation mechanism, and P_m the motivation reduction mechanism. The overall probability that noise reduces productivity is the probability that at least one mechanism operates:

P_overall = 1 - (1 - P_s)(1 - P_a)(1 - P_m) = 1 - (1 - 0.75)(1 - 0.62)(1 - 0.48) ≈ 0.96

This calculation reflects the fact that noise is likely to reduce productivity through multiple pathways simultaneously, and the system's confidence in the overall relationship (0.96) exceeds the credence in any single mechanism. However, the parallel contribution model also identifies which mechanisms are dominant: the stress mechanism contributes most to overall credence, followed by attention fragmentation, with motivation reduction playing a smaller role. This decomposition is crucial for design decision-making: if cognitive load is the primary driver, then acoustic isolation or noise masking would be most effective. If attention fragmentation dominates, speech-filtering headphones or structured silence periods might be more targeted. If motivation is the concern, design interventions addressing perceived environmental control and autonomy become more salient. The ATLAS system reports both the overall credence (0.96) and the mechanism-specific contributions, enabling designers to select interventions aligned with the hypothesized causal pathway.

The projection to a specific context (e.g., a software development open office with target ambient noise levels of 55 dBA) proceeds by specifying δ for each mechanism separately, then recalculating the parallel contribution. The stress mechanism may generalize well (δ_s = 0.80) to the target context, as HPA axis stress responses are relatively universal. Attention fragmentation effects may be weaker in the specific context (δ_a = 0.65) if the target organization has strong norms around communication protocols that reduce ambient speech. Motivation reduction may be significant (δ_m = 0.75) if the target organization has high turnover and low engagement. The projection function yields π = 1 - (1 - 0.80 × 0.75)(1 - 0.65 × 0.62)(1 - 0.75 × 0.48) ≈ 0.88, suggesting an 88-percent probability that noise reduction interventions will improve productivity in this specific context. This estimate reflects both the strong empirical evidence for noise effects and the specific contextual factors that moderate their magnitude.

#### 6.3 Nature Views and Attention Restoration: Dual-BN Diagnostic

The third worked example introduces the system's approach to theory inflation detection and diagnostic Bayesian network architecture. The relationship between nature views and attention restoration has been the subject of substantial theorizing: Attention Restoration Theory (Kaplan & Kaplan, 1989) proposes that natural environments facilitate recovery from directed attention fatigue by engaging involuntary attention (fascination) while reducing cognitive load and stress. Stress Reduction Theory (Ulrich, 1983) offers an alternative mechanistic account emphasizing rapid biophilic responses and threat-level assessment. Empirical evidence from Berman, Jonides, and Kaplan (2008), Hartig, Evans, Jamner, Davis, and Gärling (2003), and others demonstrates that nature exposure improves attention-dependent performance and reduces cognitive fatigue markers. However, the empirical effects are moderate in size (typically d ≈ 0.4-0.6), and the mechanisms remain incompletely specified.

The ATLAS system addresses this ambiguity by maintaining two distinct Bayesian networks for the nature-views relationship: a "Full Projection" network that assumes Attention Restoration Theory's mechanism is valid, and an "Empirical Floor" network that treats the relationship as an association without assuming a specific mechanism. In the Full Projection network, nature views are causally connected to reduced directed attention demand (through the system's categorization as engaging involuntary attention), which causally leads to attention restoration and improved performance on subsequent attention-dependent tasks. The mediating variable (reduced directed attention demand) is estimated from the theory, and its magnitude is set by the AESHI-calibrated credence for ART (a T1.5 theory with credence 0.68). The Bayesian network calculates the probability that a nature view intervention will improve attention-dependent outcomes through the specified mechanistic chain. This projection yields p_full ≈ 0.62, reflecting theoretical confidence moderated by empirical uncertainty.

In the Empirical Floor network, nature views are treated as causally related to attention restoration only to the extent empirically demonstrated in studies with explicit attention measures. The network encodes no assumption about mechanism: nature views are simply a cause node with a probabilistic connection to attention restoration outcomes. The projection relies solely on the empirical effect sizes documented in the literature, without multiplication by mechanism-specific credences or discount factors. This network yields p_empirical ≈ 0.48, representing the conservative estimate based on documented empirical associations alone. The difference between 0.62 and 0.48 represents the theory inflation—the increase in confidence resulting from assuming ART's mechanism is valid rather than treating the relationship as a pure association.

The diagnostic power of maintaining both networks lies in their divergence. When both networks yield similar probabilities (difference < 0.05), the system concludes that empirical evidence strongly supports the theoretical mechanism, and theory inflation is minimal. When the Full Projection network substantially exceeds the Empirical Floor (difference > 0.15), the system flags this as a "theory inflation risk" and recommends either (1) seeking empirical evidence specifically testing the mechanism (e.g., measuring directed attention fatigue before and after nature exposure), or (2) treating the design recommendation with appropriate caution, acknowledging that the empirical basis is weaker than the theory suggests. In this case, the 0.14-point difference is moderate, prompting a medium-confidence recommendation: nature views are likely beneficial (the empirical floor of 0.48 is already moderate), and theoretical understanding suggests the mechanism (ART) has merit, but designers should not assume the mechanism is definitely operative. If budget constraints require prioritization between nature views and other design features, the lower empirical floor (0.48) rather than the theoretical projection (0.62) should drive the decision.

This dual-network diagnostic has implications for system development roadmap: the divergence between Full Projection and Empirical Floor identifies high-priority questions for future research. In this case, the divergence suggests that a targeted empirical study testing whether attention restoration (specifically, recovery from directed attention fatigue) mediates the nature-views effect would substantially improve the system's epistemic grounding. Until such evidence accumulates, the system transparently reports both the theoretical projection and the empirical floor, enabling designers to calibrate their confidence appropriately. This approach avoids both the pitfall of unfounded theory inflation and the opposite error of dismissing plausible theoretical mechanisms simply because their mediation pathways have not yet been empirically confirmed.

### Section 7: System Health and Self-Healing (OVERSEER)

#### 7.1 The AESHI Metric: System Health Assessment

Like any knowledge system that must remain coherent over time while integrating new information and evidence, ATLAS requires mechanisms for monitoring its own integrity and identifying when subcomponents have drifted from appropriate epistemic standards. The OVERSEER system provides this metacognitive function through a composite health metric called AESHI (Attunement, Evidence, Stability, Harmonization, Integrity). The AESHI metric is calculated as a weighted average: AESHI = 0.60 × Q + 0.40 × C, where Q is a quality component and C is a coverage-utilization component.

The quality component (Q) comprises four submetrics, each normalized to a 0-100 scale. Coherence (Q₁) assesses the consistency of credence assignments and warrant types across the epistemic network, implemented through random sampling of template pairs and calculation of coherence violations (cases where credences seem inconsistent given the warrant structure). The coherence metric achieves 100 when violations are less than 1 percent of templates, declines linearly, and hits 0 if violations exceed 20 percent. Cache freshness (Q₂) monitors whether templates have been recently reviewed and updated: templates not reviewed in the past 18 months incur a penalty. Template completeness (Q₃) assesses whether all required metadata fields are populated for each template: missing scope conditions, undefined time horizons, or unspecified dose-response relationships reduce this metric. Bayesian network stability (Q₄) evaluates whether the causal structure of the BN and the conditional probability tables (CPTs) remain stable across optimization iterations: significant drift suggests numerical instability or insufficiently constrained inference. The quality component Q = mean(Q₁, Q₂, Q₃, Q₄) aggregates these four submetrics with equal weight.

The coverage-utilization component (C) comprises two submetrics. Coverage (C₁) assesses the breadth of the template library relative to the documented diversity of environmental psychology research: gaps in coverage relative to peer-reviewed literature on any domain result in penalties. Utilization (C₂) measures the frequency with which templates are accessed and applied by designers and researchers: low-utilization templates may indicate either genuine niche applicability or underdeveloped theoretical grounding. C = 0.5 × C₁ + 0.5 × C₂ balances coverage and utilization, recognizing that a system can be comprehensive without being practically useful or practically engaged with while omitting relevant theoretical domains.

The AESHI metric is calculated automatically on a weekly basis and reported monthly to the expert oversight panel. Target AESHI for system release is 85 (interpreted as 85 percent of optimal), which requires Q > 78 and C > 90. During the ATLAS development and validation phase (August 2024–January 2026), AESHI began at approximately 42 (reflecting the incompleteness of the initial template library and multiple missing metadata fields). Following systematic library expansion, expert panel calibration, and correction of incoherent credence assignments, AESHI improved to 73.9 by February 2026, just prior to pilot implementation. This trajectory indicates that the system is approaching operational readiness and that remaining deficiencies are largely addressable through continued template development and metadata refinement.

#### 7.2 The Thirteen Invariants: Operational and Epistemic Constraints

To maintain system integrity across computational and epistemic domains, ATLAS is defined by thirteen invariants that must hold continuously during operation. These invariants are analogous to the loop invariants and safety invariants that computer scientists use to verify correctness (Dijkstra, 1976), but they extend beyond computational correctness to epistemic integrity. Violations of any invariant trigger automated alerts and temporarily restrict system operations until remediation occurs.

**INV-0: System Operational** — The knowledge base must be accessible, all computational modules must be functional, and the system must respond to queries within 10 seconds. Violations indicate hardware failure, database corruption, or computational overload.

**INV-1: Provenance Integrity** — Every template, every credence value, every warrant type assignment must be traceable to a source document or expert panel decision. Orphan claims (assertions with no documented basis) are impermissible. This invariant ensures that the system's outputs are auditable and that design recommendations can be traced back to evidence sources.

**INV-2: Causal Coherence** — The directed acyclic graph structure of the causal network must remain acyclic. Cyclic dependencies indicate confused or circular causal reasoning and violate basic principles of causal inference.

**INV-3: Schema Conformity** — All template records must conform to the specified schema: credence values must be probabilities (0 ≤ p ≤ 1), warrant types must be members of the defined set, scope conditions must be non-empty, and so forth. This invariant ensures consistency of data representation across the knowledge base.

**INV-4: Coherence Decline Control** — The coherence metric (Q₁) must not decline by more than 5 percentage points in any single month. Larger declines suggest systematic problems (e.g., a corrupt data update, inappropriate credence revisions) that require investigation.

**INV-5: Probability Axioms** — Where the system performs probabilistic inference (within the Bayesian network or the epistemic network's coherence calculations), standard probability axioms must hold: P(A) + P(¬A) = 1, P(A or B) ≤ P(A) + P(B), and conditional probabilities must satisfy Bayes' theorem. Violations indicate errors in inference implementation.

**INV-6: Consistency** — No contradictory claims (claims explicitly incompatible with one another) may be maintained with equal credence. If two claims are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, their credences must sum to 1. This invariant prevents the system from endorsing contradictions and ensures internal logical consistency.

**INV-7: Ceiling Respect** — No inferred or extrapolated probability may exceed the ceiling value documented for that template. If a template's empirical ceiling is 0.70 (the maximum effect size observed in the literature), the system must not project probabilities above 0.70 for that template, even if theoretical reasoning suggests higher values. This prevents theory from overriding empirical constraints.

**INV-8: Discount Factor Bounds** — All discount factors must satisfy 0 < d ≤ 1. A discount factor greater than 1 would indicate that transferring knowledge to a new context increases confidence beyond the original context—a violation of standard epistemology. A discount factor of 0 would indicate complete non-transferability, which would make the template useless for design guidance. Violations suggest errors in calibration or projection.

**INV-9: Theory Attachment** — Every empirical template must be explicitly connected to at least one T1.5 theory. Orphan templates (empirical findings not connected to theoretical frameworks) reduce the system's mechanistic grounding and should trigger a review to identify appropriate theoretical homes. A template may be connected to multiple T1.5 theories (e.g., a nature-views template connected to both ART and Biophilia Hypothesis), reflecting recognized alternative mechanisms.

**INV-10: Cache Consistency** — If the epistemic network has been updated (new templates added, credences revised, warrant types changed) since the Bayesian network was last compiled, the system must recompile the BN before accepting new design queries. Stale cache data (BN parameters derived from older credence values) would produce outdated recommendations.

**INV-11: No Orphaned Nodes** — Every node in the Bayesian network must appear in at least one query template or be reachable from query nodes through directed acyclic paths. Orphaned nodes represent variables that are not used for any design decision and should be removed to reduce computational load and conceptual clutter.

**INV-12: Tier Justification** — Every claim in T1.5 must explicitly reference at least one supporting T1 framework. Every template in T2 must explicitly reference at least one supporting T1.5 theory. This invariant ensures that the three-tier structure is maintained and that claims at lower tiers (more specific to environmental design) are appropriately grounded in higher tiers (more fundamental frameworks).

These invariants are not independently checked but rather are organized into a dependency structure. Checking INV-5 (Probability Axioms) involves verifying INV-6 (Consistency). INV-3 (Schema Conformity) must be satisfied before checking INV-8 (Discount Factor Bounds). This hierarchy of dependencies ensures efficient checking and allows early identification of violations that would trigger cascading failures.

#### 7.3 Nightly Audit Protocol and Expert Panel Reporting

Monitoring of the thirteen invariants occurs through an automated nightly audit protocol implemented as a scheduled background process. The protocol executes weekly (every Tuesday at 02:00 UTC) and involves querying a random sample of the knowledge base to detect violations. Specifically, 10 percent of templates are selected randomly without replacement, and for each sampled template, the system checks all thirteen invariants. Additionally, a verification is performed on the AESHI metric calculation, spot-checking the coherence submetric by sampling template pairs and manually verifying that credence assignments seem consistent. Any invariant violation is logged with a timestamp, the affected template(s), the specific violation, and a flagged status (LOW, MEDIUM, or HIGH depending on the severity). Violations are simultaneously reported to a dedicated email alias (atlas-invariant-violations@ucsd.edu) and to a dashboard visible to authorized administrators.

Violations at the HIGH severity level (those indicating data corruption, causal cycles, or schema violations) trigger an immediate halt of new query processing until manual review confirms the violation is spurious or resolves the underlying issue. MEDIUM-severity violations (e.g., a template with missing metadata) allow continued operation but trigger a defect ticket for scheduled remediation within two business days. LOW-severity violations (e.g., a coherence decline of 3 percentage points) are logged for trend monitoring but do not trigger immediate action. This severity stratification ensures that genuine problems receive prompt attention while preventing alarm fatigue from minor fluctuations.

Monthly expert panel reporting occurs during regularly scheduled expert panels in each domain. During these meetings (conducted virtually or in person, quarterly for each domain), the AESHI metric trajectory over the past month is presented, along with a summary of any HIGH or MEDIUM-severity violations. The panel reviews the violation logs, assesses whether corrections are appropriate, and approves or rejects proposed remediation. For instance, if coherence checking identified that two templates have seemingly inconsistent credence values, the panel discusses whether one or both credences should be revised, whether a new template should be introduced to explain the seeming inconsistency, or whether the pair of templates are actually addressing distinct causal relationships and thus consistency is not required. This human-in-the-loop approach prevents automated checks from making epistemically inappropriate "corrections" while ensuring that violations are taken seriously and addressed systematically.

Quarterly, summary reports are generated for the expert oversight committee, which comprises the original domain experts plus faculty from the Department of Philosophy and the School of Medicine's Clinical Epidemiology division. These reports summarize AESHI trajectory, the nature and frequency of invariant violations, changes to templates or theory assignments, and recommendations for future development priorities. The reports are maintained in a persistent archive (docs/OVERSEER_REPORTS/), creating an institutional history of system performance and calibration.

#### 7.4 Remediation Playbooks: Addressing Common Failure Modes

Despite the specificity of the thirteen invariants, violations sometimes occur, and their remediation requires domain-specific problem-solving. ATLAS is therefore equipped with four remediation playbooks that guide human responders through common failure modes.

**Playbook 1: Coherence Decline** occurs when coherence violations exceed acceptable thresholds. Remediation begins by identifying whether the decline is localized (affecting specific domain templates) or global (affecting the entire network). Localized coherence problems typically result from recent expert panel revisions to credences or warrant types that created inconsistencies with adjacent templates. The playbook directs the responder to (1) identify the templates affected by the recent change, (2) recalculate expected coherence given the revision, (3) assess whether the revision was correctly implemented, and (4) either approve the revision (if it reflects genuine new evidence) or revert it (if it resulted from error). Global coherence decline may indicate a systematic problem in the credence or warrant type assignment process and typically requires escalation to the oversight committee.

**Playbook 2: Cache Staleness** occurs when the Bayesian network parameters are derived from epistemic network credences that have become outdated due to intervening revisions. Remediation involves recompilation of the BN from the current epistemic network state, verification that recompilation was successful (no new schema violations), and notification to users that query results may have changed. The playbook includes provisions for comparing old and new BN parameterizations to assess whether the changes are substantial (requiring notification to design teams who have made decisions based on older results) or marginal.

**Playbook 3: Template Incompleteness** occurs when templates lack required metadata (e.g., missing scope conditions, undefined time horizons). Remediation prioritizes based on template utilization: high-utilization templates receive expedited attention. The playbook directs responders to (1) identify which metadata fields are missing, (2) consult relevant literature or expert judgment to populate missing fields, (3) update the template record, and (4) mark the template for review by the relevant expert panel at the next scheduled meeting. For templates with consistently missing metadata despite multiple attempts to populate, the playbook recommends retiring the template (moving it to archive status) rather than maintaining it in a state that violates schema conformity.

**Playbook 4: Bayesian Network Instability** occurs when the BN's conditional probability tables exhibit numerical instability or when repeated inference runs produce inconsistent results. This is typically a sign of overfitting (the BN structure or parameters have been calibrated too precisely to training data and do not generalize well to new queries). Remediation involves (1) assessing the magnitude of instability, (2) simplifying the BN structure (removing unnecessary nodes or edges), (3) re-sampling the training data to assess whether instability is data-dependent, and (4) if necessary, revalidating the structure and parameters with expert judgment. For severe instability, the playbook recommends rolling back to the most recent stable BN version while initiating a more careful re-calibration process.

These playbooks are themselves living documents: as new failure modes are discovered in operation, the playbooks are updated to address them. The playbook archive is maintained in docs/OVERSEER_PLAYBOOKS/, with version history tracked to enable future review of how problems have been approached and resolved. This documentation ensures that remediation knowledge is preserved institutional memory rather than residing solely in the minds of individual system administrators.

The OVERSEER system thus embodies principles articulated in classical cybernetics and systems theory: Ashby's (1956) principle of requisite variety, which holds that a system monitoring complex phenomena must itself have sufficient variety in its monitoring mechanisms to detect all relevant classes of deviation from expected behavior, and Simon's (1969) concept of "sciences of the artificial," which emphasizes that engineered systems must be actively maintained to preserve their integrity and functionality. By treating ATLAS as a knowledge system requiring continuous health monitoring and human-guided remediation, the OVERSEER framework prevents the system from silently accumulating errors while also preserving human judgment in decisions about when violations reflect genuine problems (requiring correction) versus legitimate scientific uncertainty (requiring no correction).

### Section 8: Evaluation Framework and Current Status

#### 8.1 Internal Consistency: Retrodiction and Warrant Calibration

The ATLAS system underwent retrodictive validation by comparing its credence assignments with historical judgments from our expert panel. In this procedure, the epistemic network was instantiated with all warrant assignments and credence weights in their current calibrated state, and we then queried the system to generate credence distributions across 127 scientifically contentious claims in environmental psychology where the panel had previously issued consensus judgments during the calibration phase. The system's posterior credences were compared against these historical assessments using Spearman's rank correlation, yielding ρ = 0.87 (95% CI: 0.81–0.92), which indicates strong but not perfect alignment between the computational coherentist model and human expert assessment. This result is encouraging, yet the confidence interval bounds suggest room for systematic improvement.

Residual analysis revealed nonrandom patterns in prediction errors. Functional warrants—those derived from mechanistic understanding of how physical environmental variables affect psychological states—were overestimated by approximately 0.08 credence units on average, suggesting that the system grants too much confidence to functional reasoning in the absence of direct empirical measurement. Conversely, analogical warrants, which rely on structural similarity between the target phenomenon and better-understood domains, were systematically underestimated by approximately 0.06 credence units, indicating that the panel's tacit weighting of analogical reasoning exceeds what the current calibration captures. These biases suggest that the warrant weight vector ω requires refinement. We initiated an iterative re-calibration cycle in which panel members reviewed flagged cases of high residual error and adjusted their warrant-type confidence assignments. Preliminary results from this second calibration round indicate that ρ improves to 0.89, and we continue this iterative process with the target threshold of ρ > 0.90 for production use. This internal consistency test, while encouraging, does not constitute validation against new data; it merely confirms that the computational model faithfully represents the panel's own coherence judgments.

#### 8.2 External Validation: Real-Building Studies

To assess whether ATLAS credences actually predict environmental psychology outcomes in situ, we conducted four prospective field studies in diverse real-world settings. These studies were designed to test specific claims from the ATLAS network against measured psychological and physiological responses in contexts where the system had made strong predictions. The hospital study enrolled 180 patients on medical-surgical wards and measured their responses to daylight, thermal comfort, and acoustic environment over a two-week post-operative recovery period. Outcome measures included daily mood surveys (visual analog scale, 0–100), salivary cortisol collection (awakening and evening samples), heart rate variability (collected via wrist accelerometer), and nursing-rated stress indicators. Environmental monitoring was conducted continuously via calibrated sensor arrays measuring illuminance (lux), sound pressure level (dB), temperature (°C), and CO₂ concentration (ppm). The office study enrolled 240 workers across eight open-plan and cellular office spaces, measuring self-reported focus and stress (ecological momentary assessment, four times daily), electrodermal activity (via wearable sensors), and performance metrics (keystroke analysis, task completion time). The residential study included 120 homeowners in climate-controlled dwellings, measuring subjective well-being (validated psychometric scales), sleep quality (actigraphy), and social interaction frequency. The educational study involved 300 students across classroom and open-learning spaces, measuring attention (via computerized vigilance task), cognitive load (via pupil dilation), and learning outcomes (exam performance, course grades).

ATLAS generated prior predictions for each setting by instantiating the Bayesian network with context-specific priors derived from the epistemic network. For instance, the hospital study queried the system for the relationship between daylight access and mood recovery, incorporating uncertainty about whether the mechanism was direct (visual system → hypothalamic regulation) or indirect (circadian rhythm → sleep quality → mood). The system generated a predicted effect size and uncertainty bounds. Across all four settings, initial results showed moderate agreement between predictions and observations, but with systematic discrepancies. For daylight-mood in the hospital setting, ATLAS predicted a standardized effect size of 0.58 (95% credible interval: 0.41–0.73), whereas the observed effect was 0.52 (95% CI: 0.38–0.66), yielding an absolute error of 0.06 on a standardized scale. Pooling across all four settings and all outcome relationships tested (n = 23 pairs), the mean absolute prediction error was 0.07 (SD = 0.09), and the Bayesian network's uncertainty quantification proved well-calibrated: 94% of observed effects fell within the 95% credible intervals, compared to the nominal 95%, indicating slight underdispersion. These results suggest that ATLAS makes reasonable predictions but that additional refinement of the projection formula δ(pop, pop_target) is warranted, particularly for transitions between very different populations (e.g., from healthy volunteers to post-operative patients).

#### 8.3 Template Coverage and Value of Information Analysis

At the time of writing (February 2026), the ATLAS template library contained 385 distinct plausible research questions spanning environmental psychology, of which 208 had undergone full calibration through panel assessment and warrant assignment. This represents 54% coverage. The remaining 177 templates are held in a "scaffold" state, meaning they are mapped to the epistemic network via logical structure but have not yet been quantitatively calibrated. For each scaffold template, we performed a value-of-information (VOI) analysis to identify which calibrations would most reduce decision uncertainty in downstream applications. The VOI calculation followed the standard Bayesian formula: VOI for template i equals the expected reduction in decision entropy at the projection layer if we were to calibrate template i versus the cost of that calibration in expert panel hours. Using this metric, we identified a prioritized list of the ten highest-value templates for the next calibration phase, including effects of view content (nature vs. built vs. abstract art) on sustained attention, the mechanisms of circadian disruption in shift-work environments, and the impact of acoustic masking on cognitive performance in open-plan offices. This prioritized roadmap allows us to continue expanding ATLAS coverage in a strategic fashion, focusing first on domains and questions where additional precision would have the greatest impact on evidence synthesis and subsequent environmental design decisions. The scaffold templates remain available for research planning and hypothesis generation; they simply carry higher uncertainty and are flagged as such in user queries.

#### 8.4 AESHI Trajectory and System Maturation

The ATLAS Evidence and System Health Index (AESHI) is a composite metric that tracks the system's maturity across multiple dimensions: coverage (fraction of templates calibrated), internal consistency (retrodiction ρ), external validity (prediction error), warrant calibration quality (panel agreement on warrant assignments), and network stability (robustness of credences to small changes in warrant weights). The AESHI scale ranges from 0 to 100, with color coding and thresholds. In November 2025, the inaugural deployment achieved an AESHI score of 49 (RED zone), reflecting the system's early state: low coverage, nascent external validation, and uncertain warrant weights. By February 2026, following three months of intensive calibration and field validation, AESHI had risen to 73.9 (YELLOW zone), signaling meaningful progress but continued constraints. The system is currently targeting a GREEN threshold of AESHI ≥ 85, which we project to achieve by Q3 2026 upon completion of the current calibration cycle, extension to the remaining 177 scaffold templates, and maturation of the real-building studies. This trajectory reflects the inherent time-intensive nature of building a coherentist epistemic system: warrant calibration requires expert judgment, field validation requires lengthy field campaigns, and iterative refinement requires careful tracking of residuals and uncertainty sources. The AESHI metric itself serves as a feedback signal, allowing stakeholders to understand the current reliability and limitations of system outputs and to make informed decisions about whether ATLAS predictions should drive design choices or merely inform them.

### Section 9: Comparison with Existing Evidence Synthesis Approaches

#### 9.1 Narrative Review and the Need for Epistemological Structure

Narrative review—the traditional approach to synthesizing research evidence in psychology and design disciplines—remains valuable for interpretive depth and nuance. A skilled reviewer can integrate findings across studies with different designs, populations, and outcome measures, weaving them into a coherent account that acknowledges tensions and raises unanswered questions. However, narrative review is fundamentally non-replicable. Two competent reviewers will produce different syntheses of the same literature, reflecting their different weightings of evidence, their disciplinary backgrounds, and their implicit coherence judgments. More critically, the narrative review tradition offers no systematic method for assigning credence to claims; the reader is left to infer the reviewer's confidence level from linguistic markers ("strongly suggests," "weakly supports") that vary widely in their interpretation. ATLAS does not replace narrative review; rather, it complements and systematizes it. By representing the epistemic network in formal computational terms, ATLAS makes explicit the structure of arguments that a skilled reviewer holds implicitly. It clarifies which warrant types support a claim, how they cohere with related beliefs, and what credence that coherence pattern justifies. When a user encounters an ATLAS credence assignment—for example, "the effect of view restoration on directed attention is supported with 0.72 credence, resting primarily on MECHANISTIC and FUNCTIONAL warrants"—they understand not only what the system believes but why it believes it, with enough precision to audit the reasoning and disagree if they find faults. This transparency is a substantial advance over the hidden reasoning processes of narrative review, and it enables cumulative refinement: when new evidence emerges, a panel can revisit specific warrant assignments rather than starting de novo.

#### 9.2 Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis: Homogeneity Constraints

Systematic review and meta-analysis, as articulated by the Cochrane Collaboration and similar bodies, excel at aggregating evidence when studies are sufficiently similar to justify pooling. The hallmark of systematic review is protocol-driven transparency: researchers specify in advance which studies will be included, what data will be extracted, and what analysis will be performed. This specification eliminates p-hacking and reduces researcher degrees of freedom. Meta-analysis then estimates a pooled effect size and its uncertainty, providing a single, reproducible answer to a well-specified question (Borenstein, Hedges, Higgins, & Rothstein, 2009). However, systematic review and meta-analysis operate under a crucial constraint: they require reasonable homogeneity among studies in design, population, outcome measurement, and theoretical interpretation. When heterogeneity is high—as it often is in environmental psychology, where studies may recruit different populations, manipulate different environmental features, and measure different psychological outcomes—the meta-analytic pooling becomes questionable. A common response is to partition studies into subgroups and perform separate meta-analyses, but this approach multiplies comparisons and risks false discovery. More fundamentally, systematic review and meta-analysis treat all evidence as a pool of effect sizes to be averaged, losing information about the different warrant types underlying those effects. A randomized controlled trial of daylighting in a hospital generates a DIRECT_EMPIRICAL warrant; a mechanistic study of circadian photoreception generates a MECHANISTIC warrant; expert judgment about translation from laboratory to real-world settings generates an ANALOGICAL warrant. These are incommensurable in terms of the information they provide, yet meta-analysis blends them into a single mean effect size, potentially obscuring the sources of disagreement. ATLAS preserves these distinctions. Rather than averaging effects across heterogeneous studies, it represents each empirical result together with its warrant type and sources of uncertainty. When evidence conflicts—say, field studies show smaller effects than laboratory studies—ATLAS can articulate why (differential sampling of populations, demand characteristics, mediating mechanisms) and adjust δ(pop, pop_target) accordingly. This approach is less statistically elegant than meta-analysis but epistemologically more faithful to the actual diversity of evidence in environmental psychology (Littell, Corcoran, & Pillai, 2008).

#### 9.3 GRADE and the Question of Warrant Type versus Study Design

The GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) approach assigns quality levels to evidence based primarily on study design, with randomized controlled trials receiving the highest rating (GRADE 1) and observational studies, expert opinion, and mechanism reasoning receiving lower ratings (GRADE 2–4) (Schünemann, Brozek, Guyatt, & Oxman, 2013). This hierarchy reflects a legitimate epistemological principle: randomized assignment is a powerful method for inferring causation because it eliminates confounding and selection bias. However, the GRADE hierarchy treats all RCTs as equivalent in evidential quality and all mechanism reasoning as equivalent, regardless of context. In environmental psychology, this framework creates two problems. First, an RCT conducted in a small sample of self-selected volunteers in a laboratory may receive GRADE 1 status, while a carefully measured observational study of 300 people in real buildings receives GRADE 2 or 3 status, even though the latter may be more informative about the actual effect in the target population. Second, when the mechanism underlying an environmental effect is well understood—say, the effect of thermal comfort on cognitive performance through metabolic efficiency—mechanism-based reasoning should count as strong evidence, not weak. ATLAS addresses these concerns by assigning warrants not primarily on study design but on the clarity and coherence of the causal pathway being invoked. A DIRECT_EMPIRICAL warrant from an RCT in the target population carries high credence. A MECHANISTIC warrant from studies of the underlying biological pathway carries credence proportional to the coherence of the mechanism with related established knowledge. An ANALOGICAL warrant from a structurally similar domain carries credence depending on whether the analogy is apt. This approach requires more judgment—a panel must assess mechanism clarity and analogy aptness—but it is more aligned with how working scientists actually reason. When a practitioner asks "Should I invest in daylighting for this hospital?" they want to know not the GRADE rating of the evidence but whether the causal chain is well-understood and what uncertainty remains. ATLAS provides that.

#### 9.4 Probabilistic Programming and Bayesian Knowledge Bases

Recent advances in probabilistic programming—languages and tools for specifying Bayesian models, inferring parameters, and computing credible intervals—have made it easier to build and deploy Bayesian networks for decision-making (e.g., PyMC, Stan, Pyro). Some research groups have begun building "Bayesian knowledge bases" in specialized domains, encoding causal graphs, conditional probability distributions, and prior distributions, then updating these models as new data arrive. This approach is powerful for aleatory reasoning: given a fixed causal structure, probabilistic programming efficiently computes what we should believe about uncertain quantities. However, it takes the causal structure—the graph itself—as given, not as subject to epistemic scrutiny. In contrast, ATLAS adds a layer beneath the Bayesian network: the epistemic network (EN). The EN asks, "Are the theories underlying this causal graph correct? Should we believe in this mechanism?" Only when the EN assigns credence to a theory does the BN instantiate it with parameters. For instance, if ATLAS assigns low credence to the theory that "visual preference drives restoration," then the Bayesian network will not include edges representing preference-driven restoration pathways, even if some empirical studies show effects compatible with such pathways. This two-layer structure resolves a long-standing tension between coherentism (which asks whether theories cohere with other beliefs) and Bayesian inference (which takes theories as background and estimates parameters). By implementing both layers, ATLAS makes visible the distinction between theoretical uncertainty and parametric uncertainty, between "Is the mechanism real?" and "If the mechanism is real, how large is the effect?" This is a contribution that probabilistic programming frameworks, oriented primarily toward parametric inference, do not address.

#### 9.5 Complementarity Rather Than Replacement

ATLAS should be understood not as a replacement for existing evidence synthesis methods but as a complementary layer that sits atop them. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses continue to provide essential effect size estimates and quality assessments; ATLAS imports these estimates as DIRECT_EMPIRICAL warrants and incorporates them into the epistemic network. GRADE quality assessments feed into warrant weight calibration; a GRADE 1 RCT may justify higher weighting of DIRECT_EMPIRICAL warrants for that outcome. Probabilistic programming and Bayesian knowledge bases provide the inference machinery at the projection layer and Bayesian network layer; ATLAS uses these tools to propagate credences through the causal graph and generate predictions for new populations. The relationship is one of hierarchical integration. At the foundational layer, traditional experimental and observational studies generate data. Meta-analyses and systematic reviews aggregate these data into effect sizes and quality characterizations. GRADE and related frameworks assess study design and methodological quality. The epistemic network layer, unique to ATLAS, then takes these summaries as inputs and asks whether the theories invoked by the studies are mutually coherent and well-supported. The projection layer translates coherence-derived credences into predictions for specific populations and contexts. Finally, the Bayesian network layer implements these predictions as conditional probability distributions for decision-making. Each layer adds information; none is redundant. A stakeholder using ATLAS will continue to consult the primary literature, but they will encounter it through the structure provided by systematic reviews, quality assessments, and the ATLAS epistemic and projection layers, each of which clarifies a different aspect of what the evidence does and does not support.

### Section 10: Discussion and Future Directions

#### 10.1 Philosophical Limitations of Coherentism and Empirical Feedback

The epistemological foundation of ATLAS rests on Quinean coherentism—the principle that credence in a belief should be determined by how well it coheres with a web of related beliefs (Quine & Ullian, 1970). This approach has substantial advantages for evidence synthesis: it acknowledges that knowledge is holistic, that warrant for a claim depends on how it fits with surrounding beliefs, and that different warrant types (empirical, mechanistic, analogical) can contribute to overall credence. However, coherence alone is not sufficient for truth. A set of beliefs can be mutually coherent but massively false, as in cases of systematic ideological commitment or philosophical skepticism. Olsson (2005) articulated this concern rigorously, demonstrating that coherence can be a misleading guide when the environment contains systematic biases or when background assumptions are wrong. ATLAS addresses this limitation through empirical feedback loops. The external validation studies described in Section 8.2 allow the system to detect when predictions derived from coherence judgments systematically diverge from real-world outcomes. For instance, if ATLAS credences predict a 0.58 effect of daylighting on mood but repeated field studies show effects near 0.40, the discrepancy signals that either (a) the warrant assignments are miscalibrated, (b) the δ(pop, pop_target) projection formula underestimates context dependence, or (c) the theories in the epistemic network are incorrect about how daylighting affects psychology. Through iterative refinement, panels can revise warrant weights, refine δ estimates, or explicitly revise theoretical commitments to improve alignment between predictions and observations. This feedback loop does not eliminate the philosophical problem of coherence-truth correspondence, but it makes it tractable: ATLAS becomes more reliable not because its foundational epistemology is perfect but because empirical reality constrains and corrects the coherence judgments iteratively. The philosophical gap remains, but it is bounded and diminishing.

#### 10.2 Cross-Cultural Validation and Warrant Generalization

The current calibration of ATLAS was conducted predominantly by panels drawn from North American and Western European cognitive scientists, architects, and environmental psychologists. This sampling introduces the risk of cultural bias: the warrant structures, the theories considered plausible, and the credence assignments may reflect Western epistemological traditions and environmental assumptions that do not generalize to other cultural contexts. We are initiating a cross-cultural validation program beginning in Q2 2026, with planned panel convening in India, China, Brazil, and sub-Saharan Africa. The hypothesis guiding this work is that warrant types will generalize differentially across cultures. Mechanistic warrants—those grounded in biology and physics—should be culturally universal: the human circadian system responds to light in predictable ways regardless of cultural context, and acoustic physics does not vary by geography. In contrast, functional warrants, which rest on assumptions about how people use environments and what they value, may vary substantially. An assumption embedded in North American environmental psychology—that visual access to nature is universally restorative—may not hold in cultures with different aesthetic traditions or environmental histories. Analogical warrants, which transfer knowledge from one domain to another, may also vary: the analogy between restoration from environmental noise and restoration from social stressors may be apt in individualist cultures but less compelling in collectivist ones. By assembling panels in diverse cultural contexts and replicating the calibration procedure, we can quantify these differences and either generate culture-specific versions of ATLAS or identify universal principles that transcend cultural variation. This work is essential for ATLAS to be a responsible tool for global environmental design; current limitations should be transparent and documented.

#### 10.3 Extension to the Constraint-Valuation Architecture and Formal Value-of-Information

The ATLAS framework as described in this paper integrates a three-layer model of evidence (EN, π, BN) with empirical feedback loops. In Phase 2, beginning Q3 2026, we plan to extend this architecture to incorporate a fourth layer: the Constraint-Valuation Architecture (CVA), which models how goals, aesthetic preferences, and social values modulate perception and decision-making in environmental contexts. The CVA layer addresses phenomena that the current ATLAS framework treats as outside-scope: the fact that the same physical environment can feel very different depending on whether a person is focused on task completion versus social connection, on cost control versus aesthetic pleasure, or on individual comfort versus collective justice. The CVA will operate as follows. An environmental design is represented as a constraint satisfaction problem: a set of variables (daylight, temperature, acoustics, spatial layout, color) that must satisfy multiple objectives (occupant comfort, energy efficiency, biophilic contact, social cohesion, cost). A person's valuation function—their relative weighting of these objectives and their satisfaction functions for each—is modeled probabilistically, with heterogeneity across individuals and activity frames. The ATLAS Bayesian network then generates predictions not just for average psychological outcomes but for distributions of outcomes conditional on an individual's goal state and valuation. A formal VOI calculation answers: "Which environmental variable should we adjust first to maximize expected reduction in outcome variance for this population, given their constraints and goals?" This extension opens ATLAS to questions about environmental design trade-offs, fairness in design (whose preferences are being satisfied?), and adaptive environments that adjust to occupant states. It also requires methodological innovation: panels will need to assess warrant structures not just for empirical effects but for the causal drivers of aesthetic and value judgments, drawing on research in aesthetics, philosophy of mind, and cultural studies. This extension is still in the planning phase, but preliminary theoretical work suggests it is feasible and would substantially broaden ATLAS's applicability.

#### 10.4 Real-Time Learning and Coherence Maintenance

The current ATLAS system operates in batch mode: calibration is performed in intensive panel sessions, external validation unfolds over months of field study, and warrant weights are updated discontinuously. However, the vision for mature deployment is real-time learning: as buildings are occupied and environmental sensors generate continuous streams of data on physical conditions and occupant responses, the system should update its credences and projections incrementally, learning from actual outcomes. This raises significant technical and philosophical challenges. At the technical level, real-time Bayesian inference in high-dimensional causal graphs with streaming data requires specialized algorithms (particle filters, variational inference) and careful management of computational resources. At the philosophical level, maintaining coherence during continuous updates is nontrivial. If the epistemic network is updated each day with new empirical evidence, old theories may become inconsistent with new findings. ATLAS must then either revise multiple warranted beliefs (costly, potentially destabilizing) or flag the inconsistency and quarantine it, accepting incoherence as a temporary state. Quine's holism suggests that minor inconsistencies can be absorbed by the web of belief, but there are limits; large-scale incoherence undermines the rationale for coherence-based credencing. We anticipate that real-time learning in ATLAS will require a more flexible notion of coherence, one that tolerates local inconsistency while maintaining global structure, and that permits structured forgetting (downweighting old beliefs when environment changes). This is an active research direction, and we expect that the experience of deploying ATLAS in real buildings will illuminate the practical constraints on continuous epistemic updating.

#### 10.5 Domain Extension and Generalization

ATLAS was developed in the context of environmental psychology, motivated by questions about how physical environmental features affect mood, cognition, and social behavior. However, the architecture is fundamentally domain-agnostic: it comprises a general framework for representing heterogeneous evidence, assessing coherence, and projecting to new populations. We are exploring extension to several candidate domains where similar conditions hold: (1) medicine, where clinical guidelines must synthesize mechanistic evidence (from cell biology), experimental evidence (from clinical trials), and observational evidence (from practice), and where population heterogeneity (age, comorbidities, genetic variation) requires context-sensitive projection; (2) agriculture, where crop yield and sustainability depend on complex interactions among soil, climate, genetics, and management, and where warrant types include soil science, agronomy, ecological research, and farmer experience; (3) climate science and climate adaptation, where causal inference about anthropogenic warming and impact projections require integrating paleoclimatic evidence, mechanistic models, observational records, and perturbed-parameter ensembles; (4) public health, where disease prevention strategies rest on epidemiological evidence, mechanistic understanding of transmission and immunity, and behavioral research. Each domain presents different challenges. Medicine and agriculture have well-established quantitative traditions and may be receptive to ATLAS as a framework for codifying decision-making. Climate science involves larger scales and longer timescales, raising questions about whether coherence as defined here is the right epistemological frame. Public health requires bridging epidemiology, behavioral science, and ethics, and raises questions about whose values are encoded in the valuation framework. Despite these differences, all four domains share the key condition that motivated ATLAS: the need to synthesize heterogeneous evidence, perform causal inference under uncertainty, and make recommendations sensitive to specific populations and contexts. We anticipate piloting ATLAS extensions in medicine (pain management and activity promotion in chronic pain populations) and agriculture (sustainable intensification in smallholder farming in East Africa) beginning in 2027.

#### 10.6 Limitations and Honest Accounting

ATLAS is not a solution to all problems of evidence synthesis in applied psychology and design. Several limitations warrant transparent acknowledgment. First, the warrant calibration process is time-intensive, requiring 150–250 expert panel hours per domain. This cost limits how rapidly ATLAS can be extended to new domains and makes it inaccessible to resource-limited research groups. Second, warrant assignment involves substantial human judgment: reasonable experts can disagree about whether a given study instantiates a mechanistic warrant or merely correlational evidence, and about the appropriate weights for different warrant types. These disagreements are tracked in the system—panels report inter-rater reliability and uncertainty—but cannot be eliminated without imposing artificial consensus. Third, generalization of ATLAS beyond the domains in which it has been extensively calibrated remains uncertain. The system has been validated in environmental psychology in North America; whether its success will replicate in other domains, other cultures, or when extended to populations very different from those in calibration studies is an open empirical question. Fourth, scalability is uncertain. The current system manages 208 fully calibrated templates and 177 scaffolded templates; scaling to thousands of questions across many domains while maintaining coherence and internal consistency will require advances in knowledge representation and automated reasoning that are not yet available. Finally, the ATLAS system is only as good as the evidence it synthesizes; if the empirical literature in a domain is biased (e.g., publication bias, replication failures), ATLAS will reflect and potentially amplify these biases. The system is not immune to garbage-in-garbage-out dynamics; it simply makes them more visible by representing evidence structure explicitly.

#### 10.7 Conclusion: Coherence as a Scalable Epistemology

This paper has presented ATLAS as a proof of concept that computational coherentism—the implementation of Quinean principles of belief coherence in a formal system—is feasible and operationally useful for evidence synthesis in applied psychology. By representing theories in an epistemic network, warrant types as abstract properties of studies and judgments, and population-specific projections as transformations of credences, ATLAS makes visible the structure of reasoning that domain experts perform implicitly. By iterating between coherence judgments and empirical feedback from real-world applications, ATLAS refines itself toward greater reliability. By preserving distinctions among warrant types rather than averaging them away, ATLAS respects the diversity of evidence that characterizes contemporary science. The philosophical problem of whether coherence is sufficient for truth remains unresolved—and likely unresolvable in principle. However, empirically bounded coherence, constrained by repeated contact with reality, is a powerful and flexible epistemology. It accommodates heterogeneous warrant types that formal Bayesian modeling treats as incommensurable. It scales across domains because it rests on general principles (coherence, projection, causality) rather than domain-specific assumptions. And it enables transparent, auditable reasoning about evidence and uncertainty, which is essential if scientific synthesis is to serve public interests in environmental design, health, agriculture, and policy.

The integration of coherentist epistemology with Bayesian causal inference resolves a long-standing gap between philosophy of science and applied decision-making. Philosophers of science have argued, since Quine, that knowledge is holistic and that warrant accumulates through coherence with background belief systems. However, practical applications—from medicine to engineering—have operated primarily with frequentist hypothesis testing or ad hoc intuitions, without systematic coherence assessment. ATLAS closes this gap by making coherence computational, auditable, and empirically revisable. As environmental psychology continues to mature as a quantitative science, and as practitioners demand evidence-based design guidance, ATLAS offers a principled alternative to narrative review, and a more epistemologically sensitive complement to meta-analysis. Future work will extend ATLAS across domains, refine its calibration through cross-cultural panels, incorporate goal-dependent valuation, and implement real-time learning. The architecture presented here is a foundation; the full potential of computational coherentism in applied science remains to be realized.

# DRAFT ABSTRACT

The integration of coherentist epistemology and Bayesian causal inference remains an unsolved problem in philosophy of science and computational knowledge representation. This paper demonstrates a working solution through the ATLAS system (Architecture for Typed, Layered Assessment of Science), applied to environmental psychology and architecture. The system organizes scientific knowledge as a typed epistemic network representing current beliefs, warrants, and theoretical commitments, coupled via a projection function to a Bayesian network for causal inference and prediction. Three key innovations enable the integration: (1) a warrant type hierarchy (CONSTITUTIVE, MECHANISM, EMPIRICAL_ASSOCIATION, FUNCTIONAL, CAPACITY, ANALOGICAL, THEORY_DERIVED) that preserves epistemological distinctions invisible in bare numerical credences; (2) a projection function π that translates epistemic credences into aleatory probabilities while accounting for transfer reliability, warrant strength, and population similarity; (3) an OVERSEER governance module that monitors system health, detects violations of core invariants, and proposes remediation. The system represents 208 causal claims in environmental psychology across three theoretical tiers (8 neuroscience frameworks, 14 domain-reduced theories, 208 specific templates), calibrated by expert panels using Toulmin argument mapping. Internal evaluation (retrodiction test, ρ=0.87) shows the system successfully captures expert judgment; external evaluation (building occupancy studies) shows BN predictions match observed outcome frequencies within 0.10. The AESHI health metric has improved from 49/100 (RED) to 73.9/100 (YELLOW) over 12 weeks. We argue that the architectural separation between epistemic and aleatory probability is essential for scientific knowledge representation and that computational coherentism is feasible when grounded in empirical feedback loops. The work has implications for evidence synthesis methodology across sciences.
