## Purpose

The Article Eater project has built a substantial inventory of mechanistic templates — formalized accounts of how specific neural, perceptual, and cognitive mechanisms operate. These templates are organized by **scientific domain of origin**: predictive processing (T1, T2), spatial navigation (T3), neuromodulation (T5), embodied cognition (T8), auditory prediction (M1–M6), rhythm and motor coupling (M7–M11), musical emotion (M12–M17), and aural architecture (AX1–AX6). This organization reflects how the knowledge was *generated* — panel by panel, following disciplinary boundaries.

But an expert who encounters a research finding does not think in terms of scientific disciplines. An expert encountering a paper on wood surfaces and stress reduction thinks in terms of **architectural attributes**: what is this space made of, how does it look, how does it sound, how does it feel? The expert needs to reach for mechanistic explanations organized by the *features of the built environment*, not by the *subdiscipline that discovered the mechanism*.

This document provides the missing link: a **cross-referencing layer** that maps between two organizational schemes and makes both navigable from either direction.

**Scheme 1 — Mechanism-First (existing).** Templates organized by the scientific mechanism they formalize. Entry point: "I know the mechanism; which architectural attributes does it apply to?"

**Scheme 2 — Attribute-First (new).** Templates organized by the architectural attribute they are relevant to. Entry point: "I'm looking at a finding about material X / light condition Y / spatial property Z; which mechanisms should I bring to bear?"

## How to Use This Document

**If you encounter a finding about an architectural attribute** (e.g., a paper on ceiling height and creativity):

1. Go to **Part A: Attribute Index**
2. Find the attribute domain (→ Spatial Scale & Dimension)
3. Find the sub-attribute (→ Ceiling height)
4. Read the listed mechanism templates and their relevance
5. Use those mechanisms to: evaluate plausibility, generate predictions, identify alternative explanations, construct critiques

**If you want to know where a mechanism applies architecturally** (e.g., you're thinking about prediction error and want to know all the architectural attributes it touches):

1. Go to **Part B: Mechanism Index**
2. Find the template (→ T2: Complexity Goldilocks Zone)

**If you want a bird's-eye view of coverage and gaps**:

1. Go to **Part C: Cross-Reference Matrix**
2. Scan for empty cells — these are attribute-mechanism intersections that lack formalization
3. Go to **Part D: Gap Analysis** for prioritized recommendations

**Coverage Ratings**:
- ★★★★ **Strong**: Mechanism well-specified, empirical support substantial, template(s) formalized
- ★★★ **Moderate**: Mechanism identified and partially formalized, some empirical support, refinement needed
- ★★ **Partial**: Mechanism touched on in passing, not systematically formalized, literature exists but unintegrated
- ★ **Minimal**: Brief mention at most, significant literature unincorporated
- ○ **Absent**: Not addressed in the project; identified as gap

## A1. Materials & Surfaces

**What this covers**: The physical substances of which a building is made — their visual appearance, acoustic signature, thermal behavior, haptic quality, olfactory properties, aging and patina, structural expression, and cultural associations. Materials are the substrate of nearly every other attribute; they determine how a space looks, sounds, feels, and smells simultaneously.

| T2 (PP_COMPLEXITY_GOLDILOCKS_002) | Complexity Goldilocks zone | Material surface complexity (marble veining, wood grain, exposed aggregate) contributes to overall visual complexity budget of a space | ★★★ |

| M12 (BRECVEMA) → E-cond | Evaluative conditioning | Culturally conditioned material associations: marble = prestige, raw concrete = brutalism/honesty, wood = warmth/domesticity | ★★ |

| M12 (BRECVEMA) → E-cond | Evaluative conditioning | Material odors carry strong conditioned associations (fresh wood = natural/safe; chemical off-gassing = artificial/unhealthy) | ★ |

**Coverage**: ★★ Partial — Visual and acoustic properties of materials are reasonably well-mapped to existing templates. Haptic, thermal, and olfactory properties are identified but not formalized. No dedicated template exists for material-emotion pathways as such.

**Key unintegrated literature**:
- Nyrud & Bringslimark (2010): Psychophysiological responses to wood surfaces [~200 citations]
- Sakuragawa, Miyazaki, Kaneko, & Makita (2005): Physiological effects of touching wood [~150 citations]
- Kellert, Heerwagen, & Mador (2008): Biophilic design — natural materials and wellbeing [~2,000 citations]
- Wastiels, Schifferstein, Heylighen, & Wouters (2012): Relating material experience to technical parameters [~100 citations]

**Open questions**: Why does wood reduce stress? Is it fractal visual grain (T1), warm thermal prediction confirmed (T12 + AX5), absorptive acoustic profile (AX1), volatile organic compounds (episodic/conditioned associations), or multi-modal convergence of all simultaneously? The convergence hypothesis (AX6) predicts the latter, but no study has parametrically separated the modality-specific contributions.

**Panel recommendation**: **HIGH PRIORITY**. A dedicated materials panel would formalize the multi-modal pathways through which material properties engage prediction and emotion mechanisms. This could produce 3–4 templates (haptic PE, thermal PE, material-identity integration, material-cultural conditioning) that would substantially fill the gap. Ideal panelists: Juhani Pallasmaa (phenomenology of materials), Charles Spence (Oxford, cross-modal perception of materials), Gerd Gigerenzer or Peter Todd (ecological cues from materials), a materials scientist working on biophilic applications.

## A2. Spatial Scale & Dimension

**What this covers**: The volumetric properties of architectural space — ceiling height, room volume, proportions, enclosure degree, vista length, and the relationship between human body scale and spatial extent. Ranges from the intimate (a phone booth, a Japanese tea house) to the monumental (a cathedral nave, a Grand Central Terminal).

### Sub-Attributes and Mechanism Mappings

**A2.1 Ceiling height and vertical dimension**

| Template | Mechanism | Relevance | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| T2 (PP_COMPLEXITY_GOLDILOCKS_002) | Complexity Goldilocks zone | Ceiling height contributes to spatial complexity; very low ceilings reduce complexity below Goldilocks zone, very high ceilings approach awe threshold | ★★★ |
| AX3 (Awe as High-PE Accommodation) | Awe on PE continuum | High ceilings → perceived vastness → need for accommodation → awe response (small self, DMN reconfiguration, prosocial orientation) when threshold exceeded | ★★★ |
| T5 (NM_THREAT_HPA_001) | Threat / HPA axis | Very low ceilings may trigger confinement threat (entrapment schema) → HPA axis activation. Moderated by context and escape route availability | ★★ |
| T3 (SN_LAYOUT_COGNITIVE_MAP_001) | Cognitive map / spatial layout | Ceiling height affects cognitive map formation — higher ceilings → broader spatial representation, lower ceilings → more constrained spatial model | ★★ |

**Key unintegrated finding**: Meyers-Levy & Zhu (2007, ~1,200 citations) — high ceilings activate relational/abstract processing, low ceilings activate detail-oriented/concrete processing. The mechanism proposed (freedom-confinement priming) has not been connected to the PE framework. A PE account would predict: high ceilings = low spatial PE (large volume contains few spatial constraints → prediction space is wide → processing can be abstract/unconstrained); low ceilings = high spatial PE (many constraints → predictions must be precise → processing becomes detail-focused). This reframing is testable and would integrate the finding with T2 and AX3.

**A2.2 Room volume and proportions**

| Template | Mechanism | Relevance | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| T2 (PP_COMPLEXITY_GOLDILOCKS_002) | Complexity Goldilocks zone | Room proportions affect spatial complexity — extreme proportions (very long/narrow, very tall/thin) increase PE; balanced proportions reduce it | ★★ |
| AX4 (Spaces as Acoustic Instruments) | Acoustic-spatial emotion | Room volume determines fundamental resonant frequency and reverberation time → acoustic emotional character | ★★★ |
| T1 (PP_SPECTRAL_MATCH_001) | Spectral match | Room proportions based on harmonic ratios (1:√2, golden section) may produce processing fluency through implicit geometric regularity detection | ★ |

**A2.3 Enclosure and openness (prospect-refuge continuum)**

| Template | Mechanism | Relevance | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| T3 (SN_LAYOUT_COGNITIVE_MAP_001) | Cognitive map | Open spaces afford long sight-lines → rich cognitive map with many route options. Enclosed spaces constrain map to local environment | ★★★ |
| T5 (NM_THREAT_HPA_001) | Threat / HPA axis | Prospect (open vista > 6m) reduces threat by enabling surveillance; Refuge (overhead plane < 3m, back protected) reduces threat by enabling concealment. Prospect-Refuge interaction central to Biophilia reduction (Panel T2-C) | ★★★★ |
| T8 (EC_AFFORDANCE_POSTURAL_001) | Affordance / postural readiness | Degree of enclosure determines postural affordances — open spaces afford movement/scanning; enclosed spaces afford settling/stillness | ★★ |
| T9 (DP_IMPLICIT_EVALUATION_001) | Rapid implicit evaluation | Enclosure degree evaluated implicitly within ~150ms — extreme enclosure (trapped) or extreme openness (exposed) trigger negative valence | ★★★ |

**Tier 2 integration**: Prospect-Refuge fully reduced in Panel T2-C to T3 + T5 interaction. Biophilia complexity construct reduced to T2 (fractal dimension). This sub-attribute has the strongest Tier 2 coverage of any spatial property.

**A2.4 Vista length and depth**

| Template | Mechanism | Relevance | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| T3 (SN_LAYOUT_COGNITIVE_MAP_001) | Cognitive map | Long vistas provide spatial information that extends cognitive map beyond immediate environment → sense of orientation and spatial coherence | ★★★ |
| AX3 (Awe as High-PE Accommodation) | Awe response | Very long vistas (mountainscape through window, cathedral nave) contribute to perceived vastness → awe threshold | ★★ |

**Coverage**: ★★★ Moderate — Ceiling height, enclosure, and prospect-refuge are well-mapped through T2, T3, T5, AX3, and the Biophilia reduction. Room proportions and the Meyers-Levy processing-style effect remain unintegrated. The Fechner–Berlyne tradition on preferred proportions is absent.

**Key unintegrated literature**:
- Meyers-Levy & Zhu (2007): Ceiling height and processing style [~1,200 citations]
- Vartanian et al. (2013): Neural response to architectural enclosure [~500 citations]
- Stamps (2005): Enclosure, safety, and affect in built environments [~300 citations]
- Berlyne (1971): Aesthetics and Psychobiology — optimal complexity [~4,000 citations]

**Panel recommendation**: LOW-MEDIUM PRIORITY. Coverage is moderate and improving. A focused mini-panel could integrate the Meyers-Levy effect and room proportion preferences into the PE framework without a full panel treatment. Could be handled within a broader "Visual Architecture" panel if one is convened.

## A3. Spatial Configuration & Layout

**What this covers**: How spaces are arranged relative to each other — the topology of rooms, corridors, and openings; how you move through a building; what you can see from where; how spatial arrangement shapes social encounter, wayfinding, and cognitive mapping. This is the domain of Bill Hillier's Space Syntax and the architectural concept of *promenade architecturale*.

### Sub-Attributes and Mechanism Mappings

**A3.1 Wayfinding and navigation**

| Template | Mechanism | Relevance | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| T3 (SN_LAYOUT_COGNITIVE_MAP_001) | Cognitive map / spatial layout | Core mechanism: hippocampal place cells and grid cells build spatial model; spatial configuration determines complexity and accuracy of cognitive map | ★★★★ |
| T14 (XF_NAVIGATION_STRESS_CYCLE_002) | Navigational stress | Spatial configurations that impede cognitive map formation (repetitive corridors, inadequate landmarks, inconsistent geometry) generate navigational stress → HPA activation, anxiety, reduced task performance | ★★★ |
| T25 (DT_DIRECTED_ATTENTION_001) | Directed attention / executive control | Complex spatial layouts tax directed attention for wayfinding → attention fatigue. Simple, legible layouts preserve attentional resources | ★★ |

**A3.2 Spatial connectivity and integration (Space Syntax properties)**

| Template | Mechanism | Relevance | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| T3 (SN_LAYOUT_COGNITIVE_MAP_001) | Cognitive map | Integration (a Space Syntax metric) quantifies how many turns/steps separate each space from every other space. High integration → easy cognitive map formation → spatial prediction confidence high → low PE. Low integration → difficult maps → high PE → disorientation | ★★ (theoretical fit strong; formal integration absent) |

**Key unintegrated framework**: Hillier & Hanson (1984, *The Social Logic of Space*, ~5,000 citations). Space Syntax provides the formal mathematics of spatial configuration — integration, connectivity, depth, intelligibility, visual graph analysis. These metrics are *direct operationalizations* of spatial prediction: integration measures how predictable a space's connections are from its local neighborhood; intelligibility measures how well local spatial properties predict global structure; isovist area (from visual graph analysis) = prediction range of the visual system at any point. Yet Space Syntax has no representation in the template library.

**A3.3 Spatial sequence and transition (architectural promenade)**

| Template | Mechanism | Relevance | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| T2 (PP_COMPLEXITY_GOLDILOCKS_002) | Complexity Goldilocks | Spatial sequence orchestrates PE over time — compression → release, narrow → wide, dark → light. Effective sequences maintain complexity within Goldilocks zone while creating local peaks for emotional effect | ★★★ |
| AX3 (Awe as High-PE Accommodation) | Awe response | The revelation moment in great architectural sequences (arriving in a cathedral nave, emerging onto a mountain overlook) is a deliberate PE spike designed to exceed pleasure threshold and trigger awe | ★★★ |
| AX6 (Multi-Modal PE Integration) | Multi-modal PE | Spatial transitions change multiple modalities simultaneously — visual (new vista), acoustic (new reverberation), proprioceptive (new surface, new gait), thermal (new air temperature). Convergent cross-modal PE at transitions amplifies emotional impact | ★★ |

**Architecture-as-composition thesis** (from AX-I synthesis): Spatial sequence is the architect's equivalent of musical composition — deliberate orchestration of prediction error over time for emotional effect. Le Corbusier's *promenade architecturale*, Tadao Ando's compression-release sequences, and Bernini's approach to St. Peter's all operate by manipulating PE magnitude across multiple sensory modalities as the visitor moves through the building. This thesis is well-articulated but needs formalization as a dedicated template.

**A3.4 Social encounter and co-presence**

| Template | Mechanism | Relevance | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| T48–T52 (Social Brain templates) | Social prediction, joint attention, social reward | Spatial configuration determines who encounters whom, how often, and under what conditions. High-integration spaces generate more social encounters (Hillier); layout determines visual access (who can see whom) → social monitoring, safety assessment | ★★ |
| T5 (NM_THREAT_HPA_001) | Threat / HPA | Spatial configurations that force unpredictable social encounters (blind corners, forced paths through crowded areas) → social threat PE. Configurations that offer visual access + withdrawal options → social safety | ★★ |

**Coverage**: ★★ Partial — T3 and T14 provide a foundation for wayfinding and navigation. The architectural promenade is conceptually linked to the PE framework via the architecture-as-composition thesis. But Space Syntax — the formal mathematical framework for spatial configuration — is entirely absent, and with it the tools for quantifying spatial prediction at the layout level. Social encounter patterns are touched on by the Social Brain templates but not connected to spatial configuration metrics.

**Key unintegrated literature**:
- Hillier & Hanson (1984): *The Social Logic of Space* [~5,000 citations]
- Turner, Doxa, O'Sullivan, & Penn (2001): Visual graph analysis [~1,200 citations]
- Peponis, Zimring, & Choi (1990): Wayfinding and spatial structure [~400 citations]
- Le Corbusier (1929/2007): Promenade architecturale concept
- Hölscher, Meilinger, Vrachliotis, Brösamle, & Knauff (2006): Wayfinding in complex buildings [~300 citations]

**Panel recommendation**: **HIGH PRIORITY**. Space Syntax provides ready-made formal tools for operationalizing spatial prediction. A panel integrating Space Syntax metrics with the PE framework would produce templates connecting spatial configuration to cognitive mapping (T3), navigational stress (T14), social encounter (T48–T52), and attention demands (T25). It would also formalize the architectural promenade as PE-over-time orchestration. Ideal panelists: Bill Hillier or Alan Penn (UCL, Space Syntax), Ruth Dalton (Northumbria, spatial cognition in buildings), Colin Ellard (Waterloo, environmental neuroscience), Christoph Hölscher (ETH Zürich, wayfinding and spatial cognition).

## A4. Light & Luminance

**What this covers**: The character, intensity, distribution, color, and temporal dynamics of light in architectural space. Includes daylight (orientation, window design, solar geometry), artificial lighting (color temperature, intensity, distribution pattern), luminance contrast (the ratio of brightest to darkest surfaces), shadow patterns, circadian effects (non-visual light pathways), and the phenomenological and sacred traditions of architectural light (Gothic light programs, Tadao Ando's light slits, James Turrell's Skyspaces).

### Sub-Attributes and Mechanism Mappings

**A4.1 Luminance contrast and visual PE**

| Template | Mechanism | Relevance | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| T2 (PP_COMPLEXITY_GOLDILOCKS_002) | Complexity Goldilocks zone | Luminance contrast contributes to visual complexity. Very high contrast (stained glass in dark nave) → high visual PE → potentially above Goldilocks zone into awe territory. Very low contrast (uniform artificial lighting) → low PE → bland, under-stimulating | ★★★ |
| T1 (PP_SPECTRAL_MATCH_001) | Spectral match / fractal fluency | Natural dappled light (sunlight through foliage, light through tracery) creates fractal luminance patterns with 1/f spectral characteristics → processing fluency | ★★ |
| T9 (DP_IMPLICIT_EVALUATION_001) | Rapid implicit evaluation | Dark-light distribution evaluated within ~150ms — pooled light in otherwise dark space (campfire, hearth, reading lamp) → positive valence (safety island); uniform brightness with no refuge → exposed, uncomfortable | ★★ |

**A4.2 Daylight and window design**

| Template | Mechanism | Relevance | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| T29 (ALLOSTATIC_MASTER_001) | Allostatic regulation | Daylight is the primary zeitgeber for circadian regulation → SCN entrainment → cortisol/melatonin rhythm → allostatic balance. Windowless spaces disrupt this pathway → allostatic load | ★★★ |
| T5 (NM_THREAT_HPA_001) | Threat / HPA axis | Windows provide visual access to exterior environment → surveillance, escape route information → threat reduction. Windowless environments may elevate baseline threat via reduced environmental monitoring capacity | ★★ |
| AX5 (Cross-Modal Congruence) | Cross-modal prediction | Light color temperature predicts time of day; incongruence (blue-white light at evening, warm light at midday) produces circadian prediction error. Buildings that track natural light cycles achieve temporal congruence | ★ |

**A4.3 Shadow, chiaroscuro, and directed light**

| Template | Mechanism | Relevance | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| T2 (PP_COMPLEXITY_GOLDILOCKS_002) | Complexity Goldilocks | Shadow patterns add visual complexity; raking light across textured surfaces reveals material properties and spatial depth → enhanced spatial prediction information | ★★ |
| AX3 (Awe as High-PE Accommodation) | Awe response | Dramatic directed light (a single beam illuminating an altar, a shaft of light from an oculus into a dark rotunda) creates extreme luminance PE → above Goldilocks zone → potential awe trigger, especially when combined with spatial vastness | ★★ |

**A4.4 Circadian and non-visual light effects**

| Template | Mechanism | Relevance | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| T29 (ALLOSTATIC_MASTER_001) | Allostatic regulation | Melanopsin-containing intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs) → retinohypothalamic tract → SCN → pineal (melatonin) and adrenal (cortisol). This non-visual pathway is entirely architectural: the spectral composition, intensity, timing, and duration of light exposure are determined by building design (window size, orientation, glazing type, artificial lighting strategy) | ★★ (mechanism well-known; architectural implications not formalized) |

**Coverage**: ★ Minimal — Light is arguably the largest single gap in the project. The mechanisms through which light affects human perception and emotion are well-established in the literature — luminance contrast as spatial PE, circadian regulation via ipRGCs, daylight and occupant satisfaction, the phenomenology of architectural light — but none of this has been formalized into templates. The project can borrow structure from its visual complexity work (T1, T2) and allostatic regulation (T29), but dedicated templates for luminance PE, circadian architecture, and light-emotion pathways do not exist.

**Key unintegrated literature**:
- Berson, Dunn, & Takao (2002): Intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells [~3,500 citations]
- Heschong (1979): *Thermal Delight in Architecture* (includes extensive light analysis) [~500 citations]
- Edwards & Torcellini (2002): Daylight and occupant satisfaction [~400 citations]
- Boyce (2014): *Human Factors in Lighting* [~1,000 citations]
- Plummer (2009): *The Architecture of Natural Light* [~200 citations]
- Veitch & Newsham (1998): Lighting quality and occupant well-being [~600 citations]
- Cajochen (2007): Alerting effects of light [~800 citations]

**Panel recommendation**: **HIGHEST PRIORITY**. Light affects every occupant in every building at every moment. The mechanisms are well-characterized in the literature and integrate naturally with the PE framework (luminance contrast = spatial PE; circadian disruption = allostatic prediction error; daylight access = environmental prediction about time and season). A dedicated panel could produce 4–5 templates covering: luminance PE and aesthetic response, circadian architecture, daylight and cognitive performance, light-emotion pathways (light as safety signal, light as sacred metaphor), and dynamic light and temporal prediction. Ideal panelists: Mark Rea (RPI, circadian lighting), Christoph Reinhart (MIT, daylight design), Anna Steidle (Stuttgart, light and cognition), Deborah Burnett (lighting design and health), a phenomenologist of light (Henry Plummer or Steven Holl's theoretical writing).

## A5. Acoustic Environment

**What this covers**: The sound character of architectural space — reverberation, ambient sound, noise, speech intelligibility, music, and the overall soundscape. This is the project's *best-covered* attribute domain, thanks to the music panels (M-I through M-III) and especially the bridging panel AX-I on aural architecture.

### Sub-Attributes and Mechanism Mappings

**A5.1 Reverberation and room acoustics**

| Template | Mechanism | Relevance | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| AX1 (BRECVEMA-A) | BRECVEMA extension to architecture | All 7-8 BRECVEMA mechanisms apply to reverberant sound: brainstem reflex (loud transients in reverberant space), rhythmic entrainment (footstep patterns), evaluative conditioning (reverberant stone = sacred), emotional contagion (reverberation enriches vocal expression), visual imagery (reverberation encodes spatial properties), episodic memory (acoustic signatures trigger place-memory), acoustic expectancy (violations of predicted reverberation), aesthetic judgment (warm/cold/grand/dead) | ★★★★ |
| AX2 (Acoustic Spatial Expectancy) | Room acoustics as prediction | Brain maintains statistical priors about room acoustics (Traer & McDermott, 2016); violations produce PE. Bayesian inference: visual appearance → predicted acoustics; actual acoustics confirm or violate | ★★★★ |
| AX4 (Spaces as Acoustic Instruments) | Architectural spaces as instruments | Specific canonical spaces analyzed as emotional instruments: Gothic cathedral (T60 = 6–10s, multi-mechanism convergence), Japanese tea house (T60 = 0.3–0.5s, calm through acoustic intimacy), Pantheon (T60 = 9–11s, complex dome reflections), modern open office (chronic negative activation through noise, HVAC, no privacy) | ★★★★ |
| AX5 (Cross-Modal Congruence) | Visual-auditory agreement | Visual appearance predicts acoustics; congruence → positive affect, fluency; incongruence → surprise. Room that sounds LARGER than it looks → awe; SMALLER than it looks → claustrophobia | ★★★ |
| M1 (Statistical Learning) | IDyOM / auditory statistical learning | Statistical regularities in ambient sound environment shape predictions; novel acoustic events generate PE based on learned environmental statistics | ★★★ |

**A5.2 Soundscape (overall acoustic environment quality)**

| Template | Mechanism | Relevance | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| AX1 (BRECVEMA-A) | BRECVEMA mechanisms | Full soundscape engages evaluative conditioning (bird song = nature = positive; siren = urban = threat), emotional contagion (stressed voices → observer stress), brainstem reflex (sudden sounds → startle) | ★★★ |
| T29 (ALLOSTATIC_MASTER_001) | Allostatic regulation | Chronic noise exposure → sustained HPA activation → allostatic load. Quiet natural soundscapes → parasympathetic recovery → allostatic balance | ★★★ |
| T5 (NM_THREAT_HPA_001) | Threat / HPA | Sudden, loud, or unfamiliar sounds activate threat pathway → startle → cortisol. Predictable, moderate ambient sound → no threat activation | ★★★ |

**A5.3 Speech intelligibility and acoustic privacy**

| Template | Mechanism | Relevance | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| T31 (AUD_SCENE_ANALYSIS_001) | Auditory scene analysis | Speech intelligibility depends on ASA — the ability to segregate target speech from competing sources. Room acoustics (reverberation, noise floor) determine ASA difficulty → cognitive load for speech comprehension | ★★★ |
| T25 (DT_DIRECTED_ATTENTION_001) | Directed attention | Poor speech intelligibility taxes directed attention → attention fatigue. Open-plan offices combine low privacy + high ASA demand → chronic attention depletion | ★★★ |

**A5.4 Music in architectural space**

| Template | Mechanism | Relevance | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| M1–M17 (Full music template library) | All music mechanisms | Music in architecture is a convolution: musical signal × room impulse response = what the listener actually hears. The emotional response is to the COMBINED percept (Palestrina in Sistine Chapel ≠ Palestrina in headphones). Great composers wrote FOR specific acoustic environments | ★★★★ |
| AX4 (Spaces as Acoustic Instruments) | Music-room compound | The music-room compound is a single auditory object. Architectural acoustics shapes the music, and the music activates the architecture's acoustic properties. This is the strongest example of multi-modal integration in the project | ★★★★ |

**Coverage**: ★★★★ Strong — The strongest attribute domain in the project. BRECVEMA-A, the four bridging templates (AX1–AX4), and the full music panel series (M1–M17) provide deep mechanistic coverage of reverberation, soundscape, speech intelligibility, and music-in-space. Remaining gaps are primarily in non-acoustic sound (infrasound, vibration) and the relationship between sound and thermal/haptic sensation.

**Panel recommendation**: LOW PRIORITY. Coverage is already strong. Future work should focus on connecting acoustic templates to other attribute domains (e.g., material × acoustics interactions) rather than building more acoustic-specific templates.

**A6.1 Visual complexity and fractal dimension**

| Template | Mechanism | Relevance | Confidence |

## A7. Haptic & Thermal Properties

**What this covers**: The tactile and thermal experience of architectural space — surface texture, material temperature, air temperature and movement, radiant heat, humidity, and the somatosensory predictions the body makes about architectural surfaces before and during contact. This is Pallasmaa's terrain: the argument that architecture has been impoverished by ocularcentrism (the privileging of vision over other senses), and that haptic and thermal experience are central to how buildings affect us.

### Sub-Attributes and Mechanism Mappings

**A7.2 Thermal environment**

| Template | Mechanism | Relevance | Confidence |

| T29 (ALLOSTATIC_MASTER_001) | Allostatic regulation | Thermal comfort is an allostatic variable — the body maintains core temperature through behavioral and autonomic regulation. | T12 (IC_INTEROCEPTIVE_AFFECT_001) | Interoceptive affect | Thermal sensation is a primary interoceptive signal. Radiant warmth (fireplace, sunlight patch) → positive insular activation → comfort. Cold drafts, hot stuffy rooms → negative interoceptive state → discomfort, desire to leave | ★★ |

**What this covers**: How architectural experience unfolds over time — the experience of moving through a building, the sequence of spatial revelations, the pace of movement, the rhythms imposed by stairs, ramps, corridors, and thresholds, and the longer-term temporal patterns of inhabitation (circadian rhythms, seasonal changes, aging and patina). # PART D: GAP ANALYSIS AND STRATEGIC RECOMMENDATIONS

## D1. Coverage Asymmetry

The matrix reveals a stark asymmetry. Two attribute domains — **Acoustics (A5)** and **Task/Cognition (A9)** — have ★★★★ coverage, with 10+ mapped templates each. This is not accidental: the music panel series (M-I through M-III) plus the bridging panel (AX-I) systematically addressed acoustics, and the Tier 2 reductions (ART, SRT) systematically addressed task-relevant cognitive mechanisms. These were deliberate panel investments, and they paid off.

At the other extreme, **Light (A4)** and **Haptic/Thermal (A7)** have ★ coverage — barely touched despite their manifest importance to architectural experience. Light is arguably the single most important variable in occupant satisfaction (Heschong, 1979; Edwards & Torcellini, 2002) and has well-characterized neural pathways (ipRGC → SCN → circadian regulation) that integrate naturally with the PE framework. Haptic/thermal experience is the foundation of Pallasmaa's critique of architectural ocularcentrism and has a growing neuroscience literature (C-tactile afferents, somatosensory prediction, adaptive thermal comfort). Both are ready for formalization.

The four intermediate domains — **Materials (A1)**, **Spatial Configuration (A3)**, **Social (A8)**, and **Temporal (A10)** — each have ★★ coverage. They are partially connected to existing templates but lack dedicated formalization. Materials would benefit most from a multi-modal panel; Spatial Configuration would benefit most from Space Syntax integration; Social and Temporal could likely be addressed through extensions of existing panels rather than dedicated new ones.

## D5. What This Document Enables

With this cross-referencing layer in place, the expert workflow described at the project's outset becomes operational:

1. **Expert encounters a finding** — e.g., "Wood surfaces in hospital patient rooms reduce self-reported stress by 15% relative to plastic laminate surfaces."

2. **Expert enters the Attribute Index** — A1: Materials & Surfaces → A1.1 Visual properties (fractal grain, T1, T2), A1.2 Acoustic properties (warm absorption profile, AX1), A1.3 Haptic/thermal properties (warm to touch, T12 + AX5), A1.4 Olfactory (VOCs, M12 episodic/conditioning).

3. **Expert evaluates plausibility** — The finding is plausible because wood engages at least four mechanistic pathways simultaneously (visual fractal → processing fluency, acoustic absorption → warm soundscape, thermal prediction confirmed → positive interoceptive state, olfactory association → natural environment conditioning). Multi-modal convergence (AX6) predicts that simultaneous activation amplifies effect.

4. **Expert generates predictions** — If the mechanism is multi-modal convergence, then: (a) the effect should be reduced when one modality is blocked (e.g., wood-appearance laminate that looks like wood but doesn't feel, sound, or smell like it should produce a weaker effect); (b) the effect should be stronger in settings where multiple modalities are active (touching surfaces > only seeing them); (c) natural materials with LESS multi-modal convergence (stone — cold, hard, no odor, but visually complex) should produce different emotional profiles than wood.

5. **Expert identifies critiques** — The study may not have controlled for: visual complexity confound (wood grain vs. uniform laminate), evaluative conditioning confound (cultural associations with "natural" materials), experimenter demand (participants know wood is "supposed to" be calming). The mechanistic web of belief suggests which confounds matter most and how to test them.

6. **Expert extends the finding** — Can the multi-modal convergence principle (AX6) explain other material preferences? What about aged materials vs. new? What about materials with incongruent cross-modal properties (looks warm but feels cold)? Each extension is a testable prediction derived from the mechanism templates.

This is the epistemic workflow the project exists to enable. The dual-index cross-referencing layer is the organizational infrastructure that makes it navigable.
