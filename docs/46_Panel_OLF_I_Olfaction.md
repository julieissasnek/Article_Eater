# PANEL OLF-I: ARCHITECTURAL OLFACTION AND CHEMOSENSORY PREDICTION
## Mechanistic Templates for How the Smell of Buildings Affects Memory, Emotion, and Place Identity
## February 16, 2026 — Document 46

---

## Panel Charge

Olfaction is the only major sensory modality without a dedicated template in the Article Eater inventory. Smell has appeared repeatedly as a minor note in other panels — MAT4 mentions terpene volatiles from wood, MAT5 mentions cultural odor associations, M12 references the olfactory-hippocampal pathway (Proust effect), TP3 notes that aged materials carry characteristic scents. But no template formalizes how architectural olfaction operates as a PE mechanism, despite olfaction having unique properties that make it particularly powerful for architectural experience: (a) the olfactory-hippocampal pathway bypasses thalamic gating, giving smell privileged access to episodic memory, (b) olfactory adaptation is rapid (minutes), making smell a transition-sensitive modality that encodes arrival and change rather than steady-state, (c) olfactory hedonics are strongly conditioned by experience, making smell a powerful carrier of place identity and cultural meaning.

The panel is charged with producing 1–2 templates covering: (a) olfactory PE in architectural transitions — how the brain predicts the smell of a space and how violations produce PE that is rapidly encoded in episodic memory, and (b) the olfactory identity of place — how characteristic scent profiles become associated with buildings and settings and how these associations shape emotional responses. These must connect to T23 (context memory/hippocampal remapping), TP2 (episodic boundaries at thresholds), MAT4 (natural material convergence), MAT5 (cultural conditioning), and T12 (interoceptive affect).

## Panel Composition

### Olfactory Neuroscience

**1. Rachel Herz** (Brown University, Department of Psychiatry and Human Behavior)
The leading researcher on olfactory-emotional memory — the Proust effect. Her work demonstrates that odor-evoked memories are more emotional, more vivid, and more specifically contextual than memories evoked by other sensory modalities (Herz, 2004, ~600 citations; Herz & Cupchik, 1995, ~400 citations). The mechanism: the olfactory bulb projects directly to the amygdala and hippocampus without thalamic relay — the only sensory system with this direct limbic access. This anatomical privilege means that olfactory stimuli are processed by emotion and memory systems BEFORE reaching conscious awareness.
*Role*: Establishes the olfactory-memory mechanism. When you walk into a building and smell old wood, stone, incense, fresh paint, or floor wax, the olfactory signal reaches the hippocampus and amygdala within milliseconds — before you are consciously aware of the smell. If a previous episode is associated with that odor, it is retrieved automatically: the smell of cedar evokes the memory of a grandmother's closet; the smell of chlorine evokes the swimming pool; the smell of old books evokes the library. This involuntary, emotion-laden memory retrieval is architecture's most direct access to the occupant's personal history.

**2. Jay Gottfried** (University of Pennsylvania, Department of Neuroscience)
Neuroscientist who has demonstrated that the olfactory system generates PREDICTIONS about incoming odors based on context (Gottfried, 2010, ~300 citations; Zelano et al., 2011, ~200 citations). The orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) maintains an olfactory prediction: when visual, spatial, and contextual cues signal a specific environment (a bakery, a hospital, a forest), the brain pre-activates the expected olfactory pattern. Confirmation of the prediction (the bakery smells like bread) produces fluent processing. Violation (the bakery smells like disinfectant) produces olfactory PE.
*Role*: Provides the predictive processing framework for architectural olfaction. The brain does not passively receive building odors — it PREDICTS them. The prediction is generated from visual cues (I see wood; I predict wood smell), spatial context (I am entering a church; I predict incense/stone/candle wax), and memory (this building smelled like this last time). Olfactory PE occurs at the intersection of prediction and actual scent. This connects olfaction to the same PE framework that governs all other modalities.

**3. Noam Sobel** (Weizmann Institute of Science, Department of Neurobiology)
Research on olfactory perception including the discovery that humans can discriminate over 1 trillion olfactory stimuli (Bushdid et al., 2014, ~600 citations, though this number is debated) and that olfactory sampling (sniffing) is an active, motor-controlled process analogous to visual saccades (Sobel et al., 1998, ~400 citations). His key contribution: olfaction is not passive — it is actively sampled through sniff modulation, and the first sniff in a new environment is the olfactory equivalent of an initial visual fixation — a sampling event that captures the olfactory scene.
*Role*: Provides the active-sampling perspective. When an occupant enters a new space, the first inhalation is an olfactory sampling event that captures the entire olfactory scene — the characteristic scent profile of that space. This is the olfactory equivalent of the first visual impression. Subsequent sniffs in the same space produce less olfactory information due to rapid adaptation (~2–5 minutes for most odorants). This means olfaction is primarily a TRANSITION modality: it encodes arrivals and changes, not steady states. For architecture, this makes olfaction a powerful contributor to TP2's episodic boundaries — the smell changes at thresholds.

### Olfactory Psychology and Environmental Odor

**4. Pamela Dalton** (Monell Chemical Senses Center, Philadelphia)
Research on olfactory adaptation and the cognitive modulation of odor perception (Dalton, 1996, ~300 citations; Dalton et al., 2000, ~200 citations). Her key finding: adaptation to an ambient odor occurs within minutes, and after adaptation the odor is no longer consciously perceived — but it continues to affect mood and behavior subconsciously. Furthermore, cognitive labels modulate olfactory hedonic response: the same chemical stimulus is rated as more pleasant when labeled "parmesan cheese" and less pleasant when labeled "body odor" (de Araujo et al., 2005, ~500 citations — this is Kringelbach's work but Dalton has extended the principle).
*Role*: Provides the adaptation and labeling mechanisms. Olfactory adaptation means that the occupant of a building ceases to consciously smell the building within minutes of arrival — but visitors and returning occupants DO smell it, and the smell influences their first impression. This creates an asymmetry: the building's olfactory identity is invisible to residents but salient to visitors. The labeling effect means that the same chemical compound can produce different hedonic responses depending on what the occupant BELIEVES is producing the smell — the smell of formaldehyde from new furniture is unpleasant; the same chemical profile labeled "new building smell" may be neutral or positive for someone who associates it with renovated spaces.

**5. Asifa Majid** (University of Oxford, Department of Experimental Psychology)
Cross-cultural researcher on olfaction and language, whose work demonstrates that olfactory categorization, communication, and hedonic response vary substantially across cultures (Majid et al., 2018, ~300 citations). In some cultures (Jahai of Malaysia, Maniq of Thailand), odors are categorized with the same precision that English speakers categorize colors — named, shared, and discussed with nuanced vocabulary. In WEIRD (Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic) populations, olfaction is linguistically impoverished and categorization is poor.
*Role*: Provides the cross-cultural calibration. Olfactory hedonic responses are more culturally variable than visual or auditory responses. The smell of fermented food is pleasant in cultures where fermentation is traditional and unpleasant in cultures where it is not. The smell of wood smoke is nostalgic in cultures with hearth traditions and threatening in cultures where it signals wildfire. Architectural olfactory predictions are strongly culturally conditioned, more so than visual or auditory predictions.

### Architecture and Atmosphere

**6. Peter Zumthor** (Atelier Zumthor, Haldenstein, Switzerland)
The architect who most explicitly designs for atmosphere — the holistic, multi-sensory character of a space — including its olfactory dimension (Zumthor, 2006, ~1,000 citations). His Therme Vals spa is designed around the smell of stone and thermal water. His Bruder Klaus Chapel uses charred wood whose smoky scent is integral to the spatial experience. His writing explicitly addresses how "the smell of a building" contributes to its atmosphere and how material choices determine olfactory character.
*Role*: Provides the architectural design perspective. Zumthor demonstrates that olfactory design is not abstract theory — it is practiced by architects who understand that material choice IS olfactory choice. Stone has a distinctive smell (especially when wet). Wood releases terpenes. Concrete has an alkaline scent. Leather, wax, plaster, fresh paint, old books — every architectural material has an olfactory signature, and the ensemble of materials produces the building's characteristic scent profile. The architect who selects materials is composing an olfactory environment whether or not this is conscious.

**7. Victoria Henshaw** (posthumous; formerly University of Sheffield)
Urban researcher who pioneered "smellwalks" — guided walks that draw attention to the olfactory landscape of cities and buildings (Henshaw, 2014, ~100 citations). Her work documented the olfactory sequences of urban movement: the smell of coffee from a café, diesel from a bus stop, flowers from a garden, old stone from a church doorway. Her contribution: the olfactory environment is SPATIALLY STRUCTURED — different zones have different smells, and movement through space produces an olfactory sequence analogous to the visual promenade (SC3).
*Role*: Provides the spatial-olfactory perspective. Just as the visual promenade (SC3) is a sequence of visual revelations, the olfactory promenade is a sequence of smell transitions — each threshold and spatial zone carries a different olfactory signature. Entering a church: first the external air, then the vestibule (stone and wood), then the nave (incense, candle wax, old stone, cold air). Each olfactory transition reinforces the spatial threshold (TP2) with an additional modality, strengthening the episodic boundary.

## Position Statements

### OLFACTORY MEMORY AND THE PROUST EFFECT

**Position Statement 1: Rachel Herz — Smell as Architecture's Backdoor to Memory**

The olfactory system has a unique anatomical relationship to memory and emotion. The olfactory bulb projects directly to the piriform cortex, amygdala, and entorhinal cortex (the gateway to the hippocampus) without the thalamic relay that all other sensory systems pass through (Herz, 2004, ~600 citations). This means that olfactory stimuli are processed by the emotion and memory systems BEFORE they reach the cortical areas that support conscious identification. You feel the emotional impact of a smell before you know what you're smelling.

The consequence for architecture: the smell of a building activates episodic memories and emotional associations INVOLUNTARILY and IMMEDIATELY. The smell of old wood in a library activates memories of previous library visits — specific episodes, complete with the emotional quality of those episodes. The smell of chlorine activates swimming pool memories. The smell of fresh paint activates memories of moved houses. These olfactory-evoked memories are qualitatively different from visually-evoked memories: they are more emotional, more vivid, more contextually specific, and more likely to be described as "reliving" rather than "remembering" (Herz & Cupchik, 1995, ~400 citations).

For the PE framework: olfactory PE is uniquely consequential because it bypasses the cognitive evaluation that moderates visual and auditory PE. A visual surprise (an unexpected room) is evaluated before producing an emotional response. An olfactory surprise (an unexpected smell) produces an emotional response BEFORE evaluation. This makes olfactory PE more emotionally potent per unit of stimulus intensity than PE in other modalities — and it explains why smells that don't match the architectural context (a hospital that smells like a restaurant, a luxury hotel that smells like disinfectant) produce such strong negative reactions.

**Position Statement 2: Jay Gottfried — The Brain Predicts What It Will Smell**

The olfactory system is predictive, not merely reactive (Gottfried, 2010, ~300 citations). The OFC generates olfactory expectations based on multimodal context: visual cues (I see a kitchen; I predict food smells), spatial context (I am entering a hospital; I predict disinfectant), and memory (this building smelled like cedar last time). When the actual olfactory input matches the prediction, processing is fluent and the smell is quickly categorized and assessed. When the actual input violates the prediction, olfactory PE results — and because the olfactory pathway to the amygdala is direct, the PE is immediately emotional.

Architectural olfactory PE occurs at three levels. First, MATERIAL CONGRUENCE: the building looks like wood but smells like plastic → cross-modal PE (connecting to MAT3 material identity integration). Second, FUNCTIONAL CONGRUENCE: the space is labeled "spa" but smells like chlorine and chemical cleaner → functional olfactory PE. Third, CONTEXTUAL CONGRUENCE: the building is a church but smells like a gymnasium (from multipurpose use) → contextual olfactory PE. Each involves a violation of a specific olfactory prediction generated by the multimodal context.

**Position Statement 3: Noam Sobel — Olfaction Is a Transition Modality**

The physics of olfactory adaptation dictate that smell is primarily about CHANGE, not steady state (Sobel et al., 1998, ~400 citations). Receptor neurons adapt within seconds to minutes; central olfactory processing adapts further. After 5–10 minutes in any environment, the ambient odor is no longer consciously detected. The olfactory system has effectively set the ambient scent as its new baseline — the prediction has been updated.

This makes olfaction a THRESHOLD modality in architectural terms. Smell encodes arrivals, transitions, and changes — exactly the moments when TP2's episodic boundaries are being formed. The olfactory contribution to the episodic boundary is the NEW SMELL that appears when crossing a threshold — the shift from outdoor air to interior air at the building entry, from corridor air to room air at a doorway, from one material regime to another at a spatial transition. After minutes in the new space, the olfactory contribution fades and the other modalities (visual, acoustic, thermal) carry the ongoing experience.

This temporal profile is architecturally significant: olfaction CANNOT provide a sustained contribution to ongoing experience (it adapts too rapidly), but it CAN provide a powerful contribution to TRANSITIONS (where its novelty signal is strongest). This makes olfaction a natural partner to SC3's thresholds and TP2's episodic boundaries — it adds a potent modality at exactly the moments that matter most for memory encoding.

**Position Statement 4: Pamela Dalton — The Invisible Atmosphere**

Once adapted, occupants do not consciously smell their building. But visitors do. This creates an olfactory asymmetry that is architecturally important (Dalton, 1996, ~300 citations). The occupant has adapted to the building's olfactory identity — it is invisible. The visitor arrives with no adaptation — the full olfactory profile is immediately salient. The first impression of a building is substantially olfactory, even though the visitor may not consciously identify smell as a component of their impression.

This asymmetry means that architects, who spend months on a construction site and years in their offices, are often olfactorily adapted to the environments they design. They may not notice that the new carpet off-gasses formaldehyde, that the HVAC system circulates a metallic smell, or that the toilet ventilation reaches the corridor. The visitor notices all of these immediately. Olfactory design requires the architect to adopt the visitor's nose — to assess the building's olfactory profile from a non-adapted state.

The cognitive labeling effect adds another layer: the hedonic response to an odor depends partly on what the perceiver believes is causing it. The "new car smell" (actually volatile organic compounds) is pleasant because it is labeled positively. The same chemical profile in a "sick building" context is unpleasant because it is labeled negatively. Architectural olfactory PE includes a cognitive component: the meaning assigned to the smell modulates the hedonic response, just as Elliot's Color-in-Context shows for color.

### OLFACTORY DESIGN AND PLACE IDENTITY

**Position Statement 5: Peter Zumthor — Material Choice Is Olfactory Choice**

Every material has a smell. Stone, wood, concrete, plaster, leather, copper, steel, paper, fabric — each releases volatile compounds that contribute to the building's olfactory identity (Zumthor, 2006, ~1,000 citations). The architect who specifies materials is composing an olfactory environment, whether consciously or not. Natural materials tend to produce olfactory signatures that are complex, multi-compound, and associated with natural environments — wood releases terpenes (α-pinene, limonene), stone releases petrichor-like compounds when damp, wool releases lanolin-derived compounds. Synthetic materials tend to produce olfactory signatures associated with industrial processes — off-gassing of formaldehyde, phthalates, and VOCs.

This connects directly to MAT4 (natural material convergence): wood's stress-reducing effect operates through five parallel channels, one of which is olfactory (terpenes → parasympathetic activation). The olfactory channel is not merely a minor addition — it is the ONLY channel that provides temporal marking of the material's presence. You can close your eyes and still smell the wood. You can habituate visually and still (for the first minutes) smell the newness or age of the material. Olfaction provides a temporal layer to material experience that other modalities do not.

**Position Statement 6: Victoria Henshaw — The Olfactory Promenade**

Movement through architecture produces an olfactory sequence — a succession of smell zones that marks spatial transitions with olfactory change (Henshaw, 2014, ~100 citations). The approach to a building has its own olfactory character (outdoor air, vegetation, street odors). The entry threshold introduces a new olfactory regime (the building's interior scent). Each internal transition may introduce another (the kitchen zone, the library zone, the garden zone). This olfactory sequence parallels and reinforces the visual-spatial promenade (SC3).

The olfactory promenade is particularly powerful for memory because of the Proust effect: each smell zone is encoded as a distinct episodic memory with strong emotional coloring. A visitor who later encounters one of those smells in a different context will involuntarily recall the architectural episode. The smell of incense → the cathedral visit. The smell of cedar → the Japanese tea house. The smell of old books → the university library. Architecture encodes itself in olfactory memory more durably and more emotionally than in visual memory — because the olfactory-hippocampal pathway is more direct and more affectively charged.

## Cross-Examination and Debate

### Debate 1: Is Olfactory Design Practical or Merely Theoretical?

**Challenge**: Architects control visual, spatial, and acoustic properties with precision. But olfactory properties are hard to specify, harder to maintain, and subject to rapid adaptation. Is olfactory design actually possible?

**Zumthor**: I design olfactorily by choosing materials. Stone, wood, concrete, water — each has a characteristic smell. The Therme Vals smells like thermal water and gneiss stone because those ARE the materials. I don't add artificial scent; I choose materials whose natural olfactory properties contribute to the atmosphere I want. This is olfactory design through material selection, and it is entirely practical.

**Dalton**: The challenge is that olfactory adaptation makes sustained olfactory effects impossible. After 10 minutes, the occupant doesn't smell the building. The architect can only design for TRANSITIONS — the arrival moment, the threshold crossing, the first encounter with a new material zone. And for the olfactory experience of VISITORS, who arrive non-adapted.

**Sobel**: This is actually a design advantage, not a limitation. If smell habituates, then it contributes to thresholds and transitions without creating ongoing distraction. A building that smells of cedar at every transition (because the threshold elements are cedar) provides a repeating olfactory marker at each episodic boundary — exactly where it matters for memory — without producing sustained olfactory fatigue.

**Panel consensus**: **Olfactory design is practical through material selection and spatial zoning, not through applied fragrance.** The architect controls the olfactory identity of a building primarily through material choice (natural materials with characteristic scents vs. synthetic materials with off-gassing or olfactory neutrality) and spatial zoning (separating olfactory zones so that transitions produce olfactory change). Applied fragrance (scent marketing, air fresheners) is a separate domain with different mechanisms and different concerns (allergens, manipulation, masking rather than designing).

## Synthesis: Smell as Architecture's Hidden Modality

OLF-I produces a single template rather than the 2–4 of larger panels, reflecting the focused nature of architectural olfaction: the mechanism is specific (olfactory PE at transitions with privileged memorial encoding) and the design implications are clear (material choice determines olfactory identity; spatial zoning determines where olfactory transitions occur). A second template on olfactory place identity was considered but is better captured as a causal pathway within OLF1 rather than as a separate template, since the mechanism (olfactory-hippocampal encoding → place-scent association → Proust-effect retrieval) is the same mechanism as the transition encoding, just operating over longer timescales.

The panel's central discovery is that olfaction is a **transition modality** — it encodes arrivals and changes, not steady states. This makes olfaction a natural partner to TP2 (episodic boundaries) and SC3 (promenade thresholds) rather than a standalone continuous sensory channel like vision or audition. The architect does not design a sustained olfactory experience (adaptation prevents this); the architect designs olfactory MOMENTS at thresholds that strengthen episodic boundaries and encode emotionally vivid memories.

The practical design principle is straightforward: material choice IS olfactory choice. Natural materials with characteristic scent profiles (wood, stone, leather, plaster) create distinct olfactory zones whose transitions reinforce spatial transitions. Synthetic materials that are olfactorily neutral or that off-gas unpleasant VOCs create an olfactory environment that either fails to contribute to spatial identity (neutral) or actively degrades it (unpleasant). This adds an olfactory dimension to MAT4's convergence principle: wood reduces stress through five channels, one of which is olfactory. Remove the olfactory channel (seal the wood with polyurethane, replace with wood-print laminate) and one channel of convergence is lost.

The connection to cross-modal authenticity (MAT3) is particularly important. When a material looks like wood but smells like plastic, the olfactory channel produces a cross-modal PE that signals inauthenticity — the brain's material identity prediction is violated. This "fakeness" signal operates below conscious awareness (the occupant may not consciously identify the olfactory mismatch) but it contributes to the vague sense of "something is wrong" that occupants report in buildings with synthetic material imitations.

## Testable Predictions

### Prediction 1: Olfactory Change at Thresholds Enhances Episodic Memory

**Hypothesis**: Participants who walk through a spatial sequence where each threshold involves an olfactory change (different scented materials or zones) will recall the sequence as more vivid, more segmented into discrete episodes, and more temporally extended than participants who walk through the same sequence without olfactory changes, controlling for visual, acoustic, and spatial variables.

**Critical experiment**: Construct two versions of a spatial sequence in a controlled environment. Version A: each room has a distinct scent (cedar, lavender, coffee, citrus) delivered through concealed material samples. Version B: identical rooms, uniform odorless air. Both versions have the same visual and spatial transitions. Test free recall of room sequence, cued recall of room contents, temporal duration estimation, and vividness ratings at 1 hour and 1 week. Predict: Version A produces richer episodic structure and higher long-term recall.

**Templates tested**: OLF1, TP2.

### Prediction 2: Material Visual-Olfactory Congruence Affects Authenticity Ratings

**Hypothesis**: Rooms constructed with genuine natural materials (real wood, real stone) will receive higher authenticity, pleasantness, and material quality ratings than visually identical rooms using imitation materials (wood-print laminate, faux stone), and that the difference will be partially mediated by olfactory congruence (genuine materials produce material-congruent scents; imitations produce incongruent or absent scents).

**Critical experiment**: Construct matched room pairs: real wood vs. wood-print laminate, real stone vs. faux stone. Measure: (a) ratings while breathing normally (both visual and olfactory cues available), (b) ratings while wearing nose clips (visual cues only). Predict: the real-material advantage is reduced but not eliminated by nose clips, demonstrating an olfactory contribution to material authenticity perception.

**Templates tested**: OLF1 (material congruence PE), MAT3 (multi-modal material identity).

### Prediction 3: Building Scent Triggers Place-Specific Episodic Recall

**Hypothesis**: Exposure to an odor previously encountered in a specific building will trigger more vivid, more emotional, and more contextually specific episodic memories of that building than exposure to a visual photograph of the same building.

**Critical experiment**: Have participants visit a distinctive building (a spice market, a cedar chapel, a leather-workshop). One week later, present three retrieval cues: (a) the characteristic odor of the building (collected as a sample), (b) a photograph of the building, (c) the name of the building. Measure vividness, emotional intensity, contextual specificity, and "reliving" quality of the retrieved memory. Predict: odor cue produces the most emotional, most vivid, and most "reliving"-quality memories, consistent with Herz's Proust effect findings.

**Templates tested**: OLF1 (olfactory-hippocampal episodic encoding).

## Panel OLF-II Prospectus

### Priority 1: Olfactory Contribution to Episodic Boundary Strength

Prediction 1 — does adding olfactory change to a spatial threshold enhance episodic memory? This is the most important empirical test for OLF1's core claim that olfaction strengthens TP2's episodic boundaries.

### Priority 2: Material Olfactory Profiles

A systematic catalogue of architectural material olfactory properties — which materials produce detectable scents, at what concentrations, with what adaptation time courses, and with what hedonic profiles — would provide the empirical foundation for olfactory design. This could be a joint effort with materials science and indoor air quality researchers.

### Priority 3: Health vs. Experience in Indoor Air Quality

The indoor air quality (IAQ) literature focuses on toxicity thresholds (VOC limits, ventilation requirements). OLF1 suggests that IAQ is also an EXPERIENTIAL design variable — the smell of a building affects wellbeing through olfactory PE and memorial encoding, not just through toxicology. A framework that integrates health and experiential perspectives on indoor air would be valuable.

## Scope Exclusions

**1. Scent marketing and applied fragrance** — commercial scent systems (hotel lobbies, retail stores) that add artificial fragrance to environments. These operate through similar olfactory mechanisms but raise distinct ethical questions about sensory manipulation and occupant consent.

**2. Indoor air quality and toxicology** — health effects of VOC exposure, sick building syndrome, ventilation standards. These are important but primarily engineering and health concerns rather than PE-framework mechanisms.

**3. Taste and gustation** — sometimes grouped with olfaction but not architecturally relevant except in food service design contexts.

**4. Pheromones and chemosensory social signaling** — human pheromone effects are controversial and not established as architectural mechanisms.
