## Part 1: Individual Position Statements

### 1.1 Matthew Lieberman — Two Social Brain Systems, Not One

The question of whether Social Brain should be Tier 1 requires first clarifying what "social brain" means. My work (Lieberman, 2013; 2007) has identified two largely dissociable neural systems for social cognition:

**The Mentalizing System** (also called the "theory of mind" network):
- Dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC) — reasoning about others' traits, beliefs, dispositions
- Temporoparietal junction (TPJ) — attributing specific mental states (beliefs, desires, intentions)
- Temporal poles — integrating social knowledge, social scripts, person knowledge
- Posterior cingulate cortex / precuneus — self-referential social comparison, social memory retrieval

**The Mirror/Simulation System**:
- Premotor cortex (ventral and dorsal) — motor resonance when observing others' actions
- Inferior parietal lobule (IPL) — action understanding via motor simulation
- Anterior insula — empathic resonance for others' pain and affect (shared with interoception, Keysers can elaborate)
- Inferior frontal gyrus (IFG, particularly pars opercularis) — action understanding, imitation

These systems do different things. The mentalizing system asks "what is that person thinking?" The mirror system asks "what is that person doing/feeling?" In architectural experience, they are engaged by different environmental features:

- **Seeing people move through a space** primarily engages the mirror system — their actions are automatically simulated by your motor cortex. An open atrium where you can watch people walk, gesture, and interact engages premotor and IPL circuits.
- **Inferring occupancy and social norms** engages the mentalizing system — interpreting social cues (who belongs here? what is the implicit behavioral code? is this a space for quiet contemplation or lively interaction?) activates dmPFC and TPJ.
- **Being in a space designed for social interaction** (café seating, collaborative workspaces) engages *both* systems: mirror system for the people you can see, mentalizing system for reading social affordances and adjusting your behavior.

The critical point for the Tier 1 question is that the mentalizing system shares substantial neural substrate with the DMN. In fact, dmPFC, TPJ, temporal poles, and PCC are all nodes in Andrews-Hanna's DMN subsystems. This is not a coincidence — it reflects what I have called the brain's "default sociality": when the brain is not engaged in a specific external task, it defaults to social cognition (Lieberman, 2013). This raises the independence problem immediately: if social cognition shares substrate with DMN, a Social Brain framework would have low independence from DT (DMN/TPN Dynamics).

**Position**: Do NOT promote to Tier 1. The independence problem is real and serious — the mentalizing system IS part of the DMN. Instead, create 2–3 social templates: one for the mentalizing/social affordance reading system, one for the mirror/presence system, and one for social isolation as an allostatic stressor. House the first in DT (since it shares substrate), the second as cross-framework (EC + IC, since it involves embodied simulation and interoceptive empathy), and the third in NM or the allostatic master framework.

### 1.2 Rebecca Saxe — TPJ Is the Architectural Rosetta Stone

I want to teach the panel something I believe is not widely appreciated outside the theory-of-mind literature: the right TPJ's dual function in spatial reorienting and mental state attribution may represent a deep computational principle with direct architectural implications (Saxe & Kanwisher, 2003; Saxe, 2006; Corbetta, Patel, & Shulman, 2008).

**Spatial reorienting**: The right TPJ activates when you must redirect attention to an unexpected spatial location — a stimulus appears somewhere you weren't looking. This is a ventral attention function, part of the stimulus-driven reorienting network. It is engaged constantly during navigation and spatial exploration.

**Mental state attribution**: The same right TPJ region (there is debate about exact overlap, but Mitchell, 2008 and my own work show substantial overlap) activates when you attribute a belief or desire to another person — especially a belief that differs from your own (false belief tasks).

Why would the same neural territory do both? My hypothesis, which I want to bring to this panel: both functions involve *redirecting an internal model to accommodate unexpected information*. Spatial reorienting means "my model of where things are needs updating." Mental state attribution means "my model of what that person thinks needs updating — their beliefs differ from mine." The common computation is *model updating in response to surprise*, which connects directly to the Predictive Processing framework (PP, T1).

For architecture, this means: a space that demands frequent spatial reorienting (unexpected layout, blind corners, unusual configurations) may *also* enhance or engage theory-of-mind processing — the same neural machinery is activated. Conversely, a highly predictable space that requires no spatial reorienting may *also* reduce social cognitive processing. This is an empirically testable prediction that no current template captures.

**Position**: Agnostic on Tier 1 promotion. But the TPJ dual-function should be captured in a dedicated template, regardless of where it is housed. The computational link between spatial surprise and social cognition is a genuine mechanistic bridge between the SN and social cognition domains — it predicts that architectural legibility affects not just wayfinding but social cognitive engagement.

### 1.3 Robin Dunbar — Dunbar's Number as an Architectural Constraint

The social brain hypothesis (Dunbar, 1998; 2003) proposes that primate neocortical volume evolved primarily to manage the computational demands of social relationships. The human neocortex supports approximately 150 meaningful social relationships ("Dunbar's number") — individuals with whom you have a personalized history and can track reciprocity, alliances, and emotional states.

What the panel may not appreciate is that Dunbar's number is not a single threshold but a series of nested layers, each with its own cognitive demands (Dunbar, 2010; Zhou, Sornette, Hill, & Dunbar, 2005):

| Layer | Size | Relationship quality | Cognitive demand |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intimate | ~5 | Deep emotional bonds, daily contact | Very high mentalizing |
| Close | ~15 | Strong bonds, weekly contact | High mentalizing |
| Affiliate | ~50 | Friends, regular contact | Moderate mentalizing |
| Active network | ~150 | Recognized individuals with history | Low-moderate mentalizing |
| Acquaintances | ~500 | Faces recognized, minimal history | Face recognition only |
| Known names | ~1,500 | Names but no real relationship | Semantic memory only |

Each layer has a characteristic time investment (Dunbar & Spoors, 1995): intimate relationships require ~40% of available social time, close relationships ~20%, and so on. The time budget is zero-sum — investing more in one layer necessarily reduces investment in others.

For architecture, this creates specific design constraints:
1. **Spaces for 5 or fewer people** support intimate interaction (deep conversation, emotional support). Design features: acoustic privacy, comfortable seating for face-to-face orientation, visual enclosure (prospect-refuge at the social scale).
2. **Spaces for 15–50 people** support active friendship maintenance. Design features: flexible seating arrangements, visual access to the group, moderate acoustic separation from outside (you can hear the group but not be distracted by others).
3. **Spaces for 150+ people** exceed the active network capacity. Social interaction becomes *anonymous* — people cannot track all the social relationships in the room. This triggers a qualitative shift in social cognition: from personalized mentalizing to categorical social processing (stereotypes, social scripts, heuristics). Design implications: wayfinding becomes social (how do I find *my people* in this crowd?), and the mirror system is engaged for ambient social monitoring rather than personalized interaction.

The quantitative prediction: architectural spaces that accommodate groups near layer boundaries (e.g., ~15, ~50, ~150) will show qualitatively different social behavior patterns, mediated by shifts in the neural systems engaged. Below 15: high mentalizing, deep interaction. Above 150: categorical processing, anonymity, reduced prosocial behavior.

**Position**: Do NOT promote to Tier 1 — the computational content is about *group size effects on social cognition*, which is better captured as a moderator on social templates than as an independent framework. But the layered Dunbar structure should be formalized as parameters on social templates, with each layer specifying different neural engagement patterns.

### 1.4 Daphne Bavelier — Social Presence as Environmental Enrichment

From the environmental enrichment literature (Diamond, 2001; Sale, Berardi, & Maffei, 2014), social presence is one of the three pillars of an "enriched environment" — along with sensory complexity and motor activity. Importantly, in the rodent literature, social housing *alone* (without added sensory complexity or running wheels) produces measurable neural effects: increased hippocampal neurogenesis, enhanced synaptic plasticity in PFC, and improved learning and memory (Rosenzweig, Bennett, & Diamond, 1972; Kempermann, Kuhn, & Gage, 1997).

What the panel needs to understand: social presence changes the brain's response to the environment. An enriched environment is more enriching when experienced socially than when experienced alone. This is not just additive — there is an *interaction* between social presence and environmental complexity. The neural mechanism appears to involve oxytocin-modulated plasticity: social interaction releases oxytocin, which modulates hippocampal and amygdala plasticity (Guzman et al., 2013), enhancing the consolidation of environmental experiences.

For architecture, this means: the same building experienced with others produces different neural outcomes than the same building experienced alone. This is not currently captured by any template. The allostatic master template (T29) computes metabolic load but doesn't include a social modulation term. The neuromodulatory templates (NM) include oxytocin only implicitly through social affordances (T19) but don't formalize the interaction between social presence and environmental enrichment.

**Position**: Agnostic on Tier 1 promotion. But we need a template that formalizes the social modulation of environmental enrichment — how co-presence changes the brain's response to the built environment.

### 1.5 Stephanie Cacioppo (with Steve Cole) — Loneliness as Allostatic Catastrophe

I want to present data that I believe should change how this project thinks about the allostatic master template (T29). Loneliness — perceived social isolation, not merely being alone — is one of the most potent allostatic stressors we know of. The evidence, from John's work and from our subsequent studies:

**Neural effects**: Lonely individuals show hyperactivated amygdala and ventral striatal responses to negative social stimuli, reduced ventromedial PFC regulation of amygdala, and reduced functional connectivity within the DMN mentalizing subsystem (Cacioppo, Capitanio, & Cacioppo, 2014; Cacioppo & Cacioppo, 2018). The brain of a lonely person is in a chronic state of social threat detection — analogous to the hypervigilance that a threatening physical environment produces via the HPA axis (T5).

**Neuroendocrine effects**: Loneliness is associated with elevated cortisol (particularly a flattened diurnal cortisol slope, indicating chronic HPA axis dysregulation), elevated inflammatory markers (IL-6, C-reactive protein), and impaired immune function (Cacioppo et al., 2002; Cole et al., 2007).

**Genomic effects** (Steve Cole's work): This is what the panel truly may not know. Social isolation produces a measurable shift in gene expression — the Conserved Transcriptional Response to Adversity (CTRA) — that upregulates pro-inflammatory gene expression (NF-κB pathway) and downregulates antiviral/antibody gene expression (interferon response genes) (Cole et al., 2007; Cole, 2013). This genomic signature has been found in lonely humans, socially isolated rhesus macaques, and people with low social integration. It provides the deepest mechanistic link from social isolation to physical disease — loneliness doesn't just *feel* bad, it changes which genes your immune cells express.

**Architectural implications**: Buildings that isolate people — single-occupancy offices without visual or acoustic connection to others, residential designs that eliminate chance encounters, hospital rooms that prevent social contact — are producing measurable neuroendocrine and genomic stress responses. This is *exactly* what the allostatic master template (T29) should capture, but currently doesn't. T29 computes allostatic load from environmental sensory and metabolic inputs, but has no social isolation term.

The magnitude of the effect is large. A meta-analysis (Holt-Lunstad, Smith, & Layton, 2010) found that social isolation increases mortality risk by 26%, comparable to smoking 15 cigarettes per day. This is not a subtle effect.

**Position**: Promote to Tier 1 with reservations. The independence problem is real (as Lieberman notes, the mentalizing system overlaps with DMN). But the *allostatic cost* of social isolation is so large and so mechanistically distinct from other stressors that failing to give it a prominent place in the framework is architecturally irresponsible. At minimum, if not promoted to Tier 1, loneliness should be a first-class input to T29 with its own neuroendocrine pathway.

### 1.6 Christian Keysers — The Mirror System in Architectural Space

The mirror neuron system (Keysers & Gazzola, 2009; Rizzolatti & Craighero, 2004) provides the neural basis for a form of social cognition that is fundamentally different from mentalizing: *embodied simulation*. When you observe another person performing an action — walking, reaching, sitting down — your premotor cortex and inferior parietal lobule activate as if you were performing the same action. This is automatic, fast, and does not require conscious inference about the other person's mental states.

For architecture, the mirror system is activated whenever you can *see other people doing things*. The implications are specific:

1. **Visual access to others' actions**: A design that provides sightlines to other people (glass walls, open mezzanines, central atriums) continuously engages the mirror system. This is not mere "social awareness" — it is a specific neural circuit generating motor representations of observed actions.

2. **Empathic resonance via anterior insula**: The mirror system includes the anterior insula for empathic affect (Singer et al., 2004). When you see someone in apparent comfort (relaxed posture, smiling), your anterior insula generates a vicarious positive affective state. When you see someone in distress, the same circuit generates vicarious distress. This means the *visible emotional states of other occupants* modulate your own affective state via the anterior insula — the same anterior insula that Uddin identified as the salience network hub in Panel D-1b.

3. **Action affordance in shared space**: The mirror system also activates for *affordances observed being used by others* (Buccino et al., 2001). Seeing someone sit in a chair activates the sitting motor program, making you more likely to sit. Seeing someone stand at a bar activates standing. This means other people's behavior in a space *primes your own behavioral repertoire* through the mirror system. Architectural design that makes others' actions visible shapes behavior through this motor resonance pathway.

What the panel may not know: the strength of mirror system activation depends on *spatial proximity* and *viewing angle*. Face-to-face observation at conversation distance (1–3m) produces much stronger premotor activation than distant observation (>10m) or observation from behind (Caggiano et al., 2009). This gives specific parametric predictions for architectural design: the social mirror effect has a distance function and an angle function that constrain seating arrangements, sightline design, and spatial layout.

**Position**: Do NOT promote to Tier 1. The mirror system is part of Embodied Cognition (EC) — it is a motor simulation system, and EC already formalizes embodied processes. But a dedicated "social mirroring" template should be created in EC (or cross-framework EC + IC, since the affective resonance component involves interoception).

### 1.7 Kay Tye — Approach-Avoidance Circuits for Social Interaction

Let me bring the perspective of cellular-level circuit neuroscience to this discussion. My work uses optogenetics and calcium imaging to dissect the circuits controlling social approach and avoidance behavior (Tye et al., 2011; Matthews et al., 2016; Tye, 2018).

The core circuit for social approach/avoidance involves:
- **Basolateral amygdala (BLA)** — computes the valence (positive/negative) of social stimuli
- **Medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC)** — provides top-down regulation of BLA and computes social safety/threat
- **Ventral tegmental area (VTA)** — provides dopaminergic reward signals for social interaction (social reward)
- **Nucleus accumbens (NAc)** — integrates social reward signals with motivational state (wanting to be social)
- **Bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST)** — mediates sustained social anxiety (distinct from acute amygdala fear)

What we have shown in rodent models, with emerging human support: social isolation produces measurable changes in all five of these nodes. Isolated animals show increased BLA reactivity, decreased mPFC regulation, decreased VTA social reward signaling, and increased BNST anxiety-like responses (Matthews et al., 2016). These changes are *reversible* with social re-housing — but only if re-housing occurs within a critical window. Extended isolation produces changes that become resistant to reversal.

For architecture, the most important finding: the BLA→mPFC→VTA circuit computes whether a social opportunity is "worth approaching." This computation integrates:
- Social reward history (have previous interactions in this space been rewarding?)
- Social threat assessment (does this space feel safe for social vulnerability?)
- Current social motivation (am I currently lonely, i.e., in social need?)
- Environmental affordances for social interaction (does the physical space *support* interaction?)

The last factor is where architecture directly enters the circuit. An environment that provides visual access to others (they can be assessed before approach), acoustic conditions for conversation (60–70 dB background, no speech masking), comfortable interpersonal distances (proxemics), and escape routes (the ability to withdraw without social embarrassment) increases the probability that the BLA→mPFC→VTA computation resolves toward approach rather than avoidance.

**Position**: Agnostic on Tier 1. But the social approach/avoidance circuit should be formalized in a template that connects to T5 (threat/HPA), T41 (reward prediction error), and T42 (wanting/liking). Social interaction has its own reward circuitry — architecturally "wanting" to interact (mesolimbic DA, approaching the café) is dissociable from "liking" the interaction (opioid, enjoying the conversation). This mirrors the wanting/liking dissociation Schultz identified in Panel IV, applied to social behavior.

### 1.8 Ralph Adolphs — From Person Perception to Social Inference

I study how the brain perceives and evaluates other people (Adolphs, 2009; Todorov, Olivola, Dotsch, & Mende-Siedlecki, 2015). The processing chain from seeing a person in a space to making social inferences about them is:

1. **Face detection** — fusiform face area (FFA) and occipital face area (OFA), automatic, ~100ms. Detects that a face is present, extracts identity if known.
2. **Body perception** — extrastriate body area (EBA) and fusiform body area (FBA), ~120ms. Extracts body posture, movement, direction of heading.
3. **Gaze processing** — superior temporal sulcus (pSTS), ~150ms. Detects where the person is looking. Gaze direction is a powerful social cue — someone looking at you triggers a qualitatively different response than someone looking away.
4. **Emotion recognition** — amygdala + orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), ~170ms. Rapid assessment of the person's emotional state from facial expression and body posture.
5. **Social evaluation** — ventromedial PFC (vmPFC), ~200ms+. Integration of all the above into an evaluation: trustworthy? threatening? attractive? belonging to in-group or out-group?

This entire chain is automatic and takes less than 300ms. It means that the moment you enter a space with other people, your brain has already assessed who is there, what they're doing, what they're feeling, whether they're looking at you, and whether they represent opportunity or threat.

For architecture, the design implications follow from the information each processing stage needs:

- **Face detection requires ~2 arc-minutes of visual angle** for recognition. At 20m, faces subtend about 3 arc-minutes — at the threshold of recognition. Beyond 20m, people are detected but not identified. Design implication: spaces meant for social recognition (lobbies, cafés) should bring people within 20m of each other.
- **Gaze detection requires ~4 arc-degrees of head visibility**. At 40m, you can tell roughly where someone is facing. Beyond that, gaze direction is lost. Design implication: monitoring spaces (nurse stations, reception areas) should keep monitored zones within 40m.
- **Body posture and emotion are readable at greater distances** — body language can be read at 50–100m under good lighting conditions. Design implication: large atriums can support *ambient social awareness* (knowing the general emotional tone of the crowd) without supporting personalized interaction.

What the panel should know: these distance parameters are well-established in the vision science literature (Loomis, Kelly, Pusch, Bailenson, & Beall, 2008) and provide hard quantitative constraints for social templates. They are modulated by lighting, contrast, and visual clutter — poorly lit or visually busy spaces effectively reduce the "social perception range."

**Position**: Do NOT promote to Tier 1. The person perception system is part of the visual processing hierarchy that already feeds into multiple existing frameworks (PP for prediction error, EC for embodied response, IC for affective evaluation). But the distance parameters and processing stages should be formalized in a social template as quantitative scope conditions.

### 1.9 Uri Hasson — Inter-Brain Coupling and Shared Space

I want to introduce a dimension of social neuroscience that is absent from the current template library and from most architectural neuroscience: *what happens between brains* when people share a space (Hasson, Ghazanfar, Galantucci, Garrod, & Keysers, 2012; Hasson, Nir, Levy, Fuhrmann, & Malach, 2004).

Our hyperscanning work (simultaneous neural recording from two or more people) has shown that when people engage in conversation, their neural activity becomes *synchronized* — particularly in temporal cortex (language processing), prefrontal cortex (shared narrative construction), and the TPJ/mentalizing network (mutual mental state tracking). The degree of neural coupling predicts mutual understanding: the more coupled the brains, the better people understand each other (Stephens, Silbert, & Hasson, 2010).

What the panel needs to know: inter-brain coupling is strongly modulated by the *physical and acoustic conditions* of the shared space.

1. **Acoustic conditions**: Speech intelligibility is the bottleneck. When background noise masks speech, neural coupling drops precipitously — prefrontal and temporal coupling requires that both people can hear and process each other's speech. The Speech Transmission Index (STI > 0.6) is the critical threshold for coupling.
2. **Visual co-presence**: Face-to-face interaction produces stronger coupling than side-by-side or separated interaction. The visual channel (gaze, facial expression, gesture) provides timing cues that synchronize turn-taking rhythms.
3. **Physical proximity**: Coupling is strongest at conversation distance (1–2m) and degrades with distance. This is partly acoustic and partly social — people modulate their communication style at different distances.
4. **Group size**: Coupling in groups follows a power law — in a group of N, the computational demand for maintaining mutual coupling scales approximately as N(N-1)/2 (all pairwise couplings). Beyond about 4–5 people, full mutual coupling becomes impossible, and the group must switch to a broadcast/audience model (one speaker, many listeners) or fragment into sub-groups.

This last point connects to Dunbar's layered structure: the "intimate" layer of ~5 corresponds approximately to the maximum number of people who can maintain full mutual neural coupling in a shared conversation.

For architecture: a space designed for deep collaborative work should support groups of 4–5 with high STI, visual co-presence, and proximity. A space designed for broader social mixing should support fragmentation into sub-groups of 4–5 while maintaining sightlines between groups.

**Position**: Do NOT promote to Tier 1. But inter-brain coupling is a measurable dependent variable that should be predicted by social templates. It provides an objective neural metric for "social interaction quality" that could replace or complement self-report measures.

### 1.10 Jan Gehl — What We Actually Observe in Built Environments

I approach this question differently from the neuroscientists. I have spent five decades observing how people actually behave in public and semi-public spaces (Gehl, 1971/2011; Gehl, 2010; Gehl & Svarre, 2013). My observational methods — systematically counting social interactions, mapping stationary activity, tracking pedestrian flows — provide the behavioral ground truth that neural mechanisms must ultimately explain.

Here is what we consistently observe:

1. **The 100-meter rule**: Social interaction in public space requires visual contact within approximately 100 meters for awareness, 25 meters for recognition of mood and activity, and 7 meters or less for normal conversation. These distances correspond remarkably to what Adolphs describes neurally.

2. **Edge effects**: People overwhelmingly choose to linger at the edges of spaces — against walls, at the transition between inside and outside, at the boundary between public and private. This is consistent with the prospect-refuge framework (T3 + T5) but also has a social dimension: edges provide the ability to observe others while controlling one's own exposure. This is the spatial expression of Tye's approach/avoidance circuit.

3. **The café chair principle**: When given a choice, people in public spaces orient themselves to face other people rather than away from them. This is so consistent across cultures that it suggests a deep neural drive — the mirror system (Keysers) and the face detection system (Adolphs) both benefit from facing others.

4. **Triangulation**: Social interaction between strangers is facilitated by shared external stimuli — a street musician, a public sculpture, a view. The external stimulus provides a socially acceptable reason to establish proximity and a shared attention focus. This is the behavioral manifestation of what Hasson describes as coupling: the external stimulus provides a shared input that synchronizes the brains of two strangers, lowering the threshold for social approach.

5. **The 25-person threshold**: In our observations, public spaces with fewer than ~25 people per 1000m² feel "empty" and socially uninviting. Spaces with more than ~100 people per 1000m² feel "crowded" and socially overwhelming. The optimal range for lively, pleasant social activity is roughly 25–100 people per 1000m². This maps onto Dunbar's layers — the "affiliate" zone (~50) is the sweet spot.

**Position**: Do NOT promote to Tier 1. But the behavioral observations I've described provide the testable predictions that any social template must ultimately explain. The templates should be constrained by these observed regularities, not just derived from neural mechanisms.

### 1.11 Colin Ellard — What We Can Measure Ecologically

Following D-1b's lesson 3, let me specify what is and isn't measurable for social brain processes in real buildings:

**Measurable with current ambulatory technology:**
- Social behavior (interaction frequency, duration, group size) — video analysis, RFID badges, Bluetooth proximity
- Skin conductance response to social encounters — mobile EDA
- Heart rate variability during social vs. solitary phases — mobile HRV (vagal tone as proxy for "social engagement" via polyvagal theory)
- Approach/avoidance behavior — GPS/IMU tracking of movement toward or away from social zones
- Self-reported loneliness, social satisfaction — ecological momentary assessment via smartphone

**Partially measurable:**
- Frontal alpha asymmetry (approach motivation) — mobile EEG, but social contexts introduce movement artifacts
- Theta power changes (cognitive load from social processing) — mobile EEG
- Neural coupling between co-present people — mobile hyperscanning EEG is technically possible but extremely noisy

**Not measurable ecologically:**
- Mirror system activation (premotor cortex) — requires fMRI
- Amygdala responses to social stimuli — deep source, not accessible by EEG
- DMN mentalizing network subsystem dynamics — requires fMRI
- Oxytocin, cortisol in real-time — requires saliva samples, ~15 minute lag

**My most important point**: vagal tone (HRV) may be the best available ecological proxy for social engagement. Polyvagal theory (Porges, 2007) proposes that the ventral vagal complex supports "social engagement" — a physiological state of calm alertness that facilitates social interaction. High vagal tone indicates readiness for social engagement; low vagal tone indicates defensive withdrawal. This is measurable with a chest strap or smart watch, and it provides a continuous physiological signal that can be mapped against spatial location and social context.

**Position**: Same as Gehl — the behavioral and physiological data are the ground truth. Templates should generate predictions testable with available ecological methods.

### Thread 1: The Independence Problem (Again)

**Opus**: This panel recapitulates the same structural question as Panel IV (Cognitive Control). Does Social Brain have sufficient independence from existing frameworks to justify Tier 1 status?

**Lieberman**: Let me be explicit about the overlaps. The mentalizing system shares:
- With DT (DMN/TPN): dmPFC, PCC, TPJ — all DMN nodes. Independence ~0.2.
- With PP: Social prediction error is a special case of prediction error. Independence ~0.4.
- With IC (Interoception): Anterior insula is shared between empathic resonance and interoceptive processing. Independence ~0.3.
- With EC (Embodied Cognition): The mirror system is fundamentally embodied simulation. Independence ~0.3.
- With NM (Neuromodulation): Oxytocin and dopamine social reward circuits. Independence ~0.4.

Average independence across all 10 frameworks: approximately 0.35. This is even lower than the ~0.40 estimated for Cognitive Control in Panel IV.

**Dunbar**: But I want to push back on this analysis. The independence matrix measures statistical independence of *evidence*, not conceptual independence. Social behavior generates its own unique evidence — observational data on interaction patterns, group sizes, network structures — that is not produced by any other framework. The studies I cite on social group size and brain volume are not studies of DMN, or PP, or EC. They are studies of social cognition specifically. The evidence base is independent even if the neural substrate overlaps.

**Saxe**: Robin makes a fair point, but the CMR pipeline uses the independence matrix for convergence scoring (Step 5). If Social Brain has independence 0.2 with DT, then a prediction supported by both Social Brain and DT gets almost no convergence bonus. The pipeline doesn't distinguish "independent evidence" from "independent neural substrate." Both would need to be true for convergence to work.

**Lieberman**: And to be frank, the evidence is not always independent either. Many studies of "social cognition" measure DMN activity — resting-state connectivity, task-based DMN suppression — and call it social neuroscience because the task involved social stimuli. The neural evidence for mentalizing is largely DMN evidence viewed through a social lens.

**Tye**: I want to offer a different perspective. The circuits I study — BLA, mPFC, VTA, NAc, BNST — are NOT DMN circuits. The social approach/avoidance system is subcortical and operates at a different level from the mentalizing/DMN system. The overlap problem is specific to cortical social cognition. Subcortical social circuits have much higher independence — they share substrate with NM (dopamine, VTA) but not with DT or PP.

**S. Cacioppo / Cole**: Similarly, the neuroendocrine and genomic effects of loneliness — HPA dysregulation, CTRA gene expression changes — are not DMN phenomena. They are allostatic phenomena that happen to be triggered by social isolation rather than by physical stressors. Their independence from DT is high (>0.8) because the evidence base is entirely different (cortisol, inflammatory markers, gene expression vs. fMRI BOLD signal).

### Thread 2: The Decomposition Alternative

**Opus**: I'm hearing a pattern that mirrors Panel IV. The social brain is not one thing — it is at least three dissociable systems:
1. **Cortical mentalizing** (dmPFC, TPJ, temporal poles, PCC) — high overlap with DMN, low independence
2. **Mirror/embodied simulation** (premotor, IPL, anterior insula) — moderate overlap with EC and IC
3. **Subcortical social motivation + allostatic cost** (BLA, mPFC, VTA, NAc, HPA axis, CTRA) — low overlap with existing frameworks, high independence

Does the panel agree with this decomposition?

**Keysers**: Yes, and I want to specify the mirror system more carefully. It has two components:
- **Motor mirroring** (premotor cortex, IPL) — action simulation. This is clearly in EC territory.
- **Affective mirroring** (anterior insula, ACC for empathic pain) — shared affect. This is more in IC territory, since it involves interoceptive simulation of another's emotional state.

So the mirror system itself decomposes across two existing frameworks.

**Adolphs**: I'd add a fourth system to Opus's list: the **person perception pipeline** (FFA, EBA, pSTS, amygdala → vmPFC). This is the rapid, automatic processing chain that extracts social information from visual input. It is a perceptual system, distinct from the mentalizing, mirror, and motivational systems. It is the *input* to all three.

**Hasson**: And a fifth: **inter-brain coupling** itself — the synchronization of neural activity between co-present people. This is not reducible to any individual brain's processing. It is a dyadic or group-level phenomenon that emerges from the interaction. But I acknowledge this may be beyond the scope of the template library, which models individual brains.

**Bavelier**: I want to resist letting this decomposition make social cognition seem unimportant. Yes, the cortical components overlap with existing frameworks. But the *social function* — the fact that these circuits are deployed in service of social cognition specifically — is what matters for architecture. A building that isolates people produces bad outcomes not because DMN is disrupted (it may not be) but because the *social use* of DMN mentalizing is prevented. The function matters, not just the substrate.

**Gehl**: From the design perspective, I don't care whether social cognition is one framework or five. What I need are templates that predict: if I change the built environment in way X, social behavior will change in way Y. The neural decomposition is interesting but architecturally what matters is the *mapping from design features to social behavioral outcomes*.

- **Frameworks**: NM + IC (cross-framework)
- Cacioppo/Cole's loneliness → HPA dysregulation → CTRA gene expression → chronic inflammation pathway
- Tye's subcortical circuit changes: BLA hyperreactivity, reduced mPFC regulation, reduced VTA social reward

- The "surprise → model updating" computation as the shared mechanism

- Novel prediction that no current template generates

**T52: Social Enrichment Modulation**
- **Frameworks**: NM + MS (cross-framework)
- Bavelier's social presence × environmental enrichment interaction
- Mechanism: social interaction → oxytocin release → enhanced hippocampal and amygdala plasticity → enhanced consolidation of environmental experience
- Prediction: the same enriching environment produces greater neural benefit when experienced with others than alone
- Longitudinal implications: co-working spaces should show greater cognitive benefit than isolated work in the same environment
- Connects to Kühn's longitudinal hippocampal data from D-1b

## Part 6: Testable Predictions from New Templates

### Prediction 1 (from T48 — Social Affordance Reading)
**Dunbar layer threshold effects in architectural spaces**: Social behavior quality should shift qualitatively at group sizes matching Dunbar's layers. Specifically: in a workspace designed for 15 people, adding a 16th person should not simply increase social noise linearly — it should trigger a qualitative shift from "personalized mentalizing" to "categorical processing" that is detectable as increased DMN dorsal medial subsystem activation (fMRI) or increased frontal theta (EEG). **Ecological test**: Compare social interaction quality (conversation depth, eye contact duration, reciprocal disclosure) in groups of 12 vs. 18 in identical spaces.

### Prediction 2 (from T49 — Social Mirror)
**The glass wall paradox**: Open-plan offices with glass walls to corridors provide visual access to others (engaging mirror system) without acoustic coupling. This should produce a specific pattern: increased motor resonance (people unconsciously mirroring observed actions) but without the social satisfaction of actual interaction. Prediction: occupants of glass-walled offices show increased restlessness (motor activation from mirror system) and increased loneliness (visual social presence without real interaction — "social snacking" that doesn't satisfy). **Ecological test**: Compare movement patterns (accelerometer), self-reported loneliness, and HRV between glass-walled and opaque-walled offices.

### Prediction 3 (from T50 — Social Isolation Allostatic Load)
**Hospital isolation room cortisol**: Patients in single-occupancy hospital rooms without social contact (beyond medical visits) should show flattened diurnal cortisol slope within 5–7 days, detectable via salivary cortisol at 4 timepoints daily. Patients in shared rooms or rooms with regular social visitor access should maintain normal cortisol rhythms. **Critical test**: the cortisol difference should be mediated by perceived loneliness (UCLA Loneliness Scale), not by objective aloneness — a patient who prefers solitude should not show the effect.

### Prediction 4 (from T51 — TPJ Spatial-Social Bridge)
**Spatial complexity primes social cognition**: After navigating a spatially complex building (many turns, unexpected vistas, wayfinding challenges), participants should perform better on a subsequent theory-of-mind task (Reading the Mind in the Eyes test, false belief task) compared to after navigating a simple building. **Critical test**: The effect should be specific to right TPJ-mediated tasks and should not generalize to non-social cognitive tasks (e.g., working memory), ruling out general arousal as an explanation. **Ecological test**: Administer theory-of-mind tasks via smartphone before and after navigating different building types.

### Prediction 5 (from T52 — Social Enrichment Modulation)
**Co-working vs. solo-working environmental benefit**: Workers in a well-designed (high environmental quality) co-working space should show greater cognitive benefit (attention tests, creative problem-solving) than workers in the same well-designed space working alone, and greater benefit than workers in a poorly designed space working together. The prediction is an interaction: environment × social presence, not a main effect of either. **Ecological test**: Within-subject crossover design comparing cognitive test performance across 4 conditions (good space × co-working, good space × solo, poor space × co-working, poor space × solo).
